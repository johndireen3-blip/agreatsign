Prophecy in Micah

A country prophet from Judah who spoke hard words to the powerful, and the promise the scribes read out to Herod: the ruler of Israel would come from little Bethlehem. Micah names the Messiah's birthplace, sees the nations beating swords into plowshares, and ends by asking, “Who is a God like you?”

The book at a glance

Micah of Moresheth, a small town in the Judean foothills, prophesied in the late eighth century before Christ, in the days of Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah (1:1), and so was a contemporary of Isaiah. He denounced the rich who seized fields and the leaders who sold justice, and he warned that Jerusalem itself would be plowed like a field (3:12). A century later his words were remembered as having moved King Hezekiah to repent (Jeremiah 26:18–19). Many scholars hold that the book as we have it gathers Micah's own oracles with later prophetic additions, especially in chapters 4–7; the Church receives the whole as inspired Scripture either way.

Its prophecy moves in waves of judgment and promise. Doom on Samaria and Jerusalem gives way to the vision of the LORD's mountain raised above the hills, and the fall of Davidic Jerusalem gives way to a ruler from David's own town, Bethlehem. In between stands a word that is not prediction at all but the heart of what prophecy is for: what the LORD requires.

The passages

Micah 4:1–4ProphecyUnfoldingFuture
“They shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruning hooks; One nation shall not raise the sword against another, nor shall they train for war again.”
Reading
“In days to come” the mountain of the LORD's house will be raised above the hills, and “peoples shall stream to it” (4:1), saying, “Come, let us climb the LORD's mountain” (4:2). God himself teaches them and judges between them, and peace follows from his instruction. The same oracle stands almost word for word in Isaiah 2:2–4; whether one prophet took it from the other or both drew on an older word, it was precious enough to be kept twice. Micah adds a homely close: “They shall all sit under their own vines, under their own fig trees, undisturbed” (4:4).
Why two labels
The nations have begun to stream to Zion since the Gospel went out from Jerusalem (Luke 24:47; Acts 2) and the Church gathers peoples of every tongue. But the end of war is plainly still ahead. Revelation sees it completed in the city whose light the nations walk by (Revelation 21:24–26).
For now
The promise does not wait passively. Those who belong to the coming city are called to be peacemakers now (Matthew 5:9).
Micah 5:1–4ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“But you, Bethlehem-Ephrathah least among the clans of Judah, From you shall come forth for me one who is to be ruler in Israel; Whose origin is from of old, from ancient times.”
Reading
When Jerusalem's kings have failed, God goes back to the beginning, to the small town where David was born, and promises a new ruler from there. The NABRE note calls the passage a promise of salvation through a “messiah,” an anointed ruler. His people are given up only “when she who is to give birth has borne” (5:2); then “he shall take his place as shepherd by the strength of the LORD” (5:3), his greatness reaching “to the ends of the earth,” and “he shall be peace” (5:4).
Fulfilled
When Herod asks where the Messiah is to be born, the chief priests and scribes answer, “In Bethlehem of Judea, for thus it has been written through the prophet” (Matthew 2:5). Matthew's form of the text turns Micah's “least” around: Bethlehem is now “by no means least,” because of the ruler “who is to shepherd my people Israel” (Matthew 2:6). The crowds in John's Gospel know the same expectation (John 7:42).
Unfolding
“He shall be peace” is said of a person, not a treaty. St. Paul writes that Christ “is our peace” (Ephesians 2:14). The woman in labor whose child brings deliverance returns in the woman clothed with the sun (Revelation 12:1–5).
Note
The NABRE numbers this passage 5:1–4; many other Bibles number it 5:2–5, so “Micah 5:2” elsewhere is the same verse.
Micah 6:8ProphecyUnfolding
“You have been told, O mortal, what is good, and what the LORD requires of you: Only to do justice and to love goodness, and to walk humbly with your God.”
Reading
This is not a prediction. It is prophecy in its first sense, God's word spoken to his people now. In a courtroom scene God calls Israel to account (6:1–5), and the people ask what offering would satisfy him: burnt offerings, “calves a year old,” rivers of oil, even “my firstborn for my crime” (6:6–7). The answer is none of these. God asks for a life: justice, steadfast love, and humble walking with him. The Reproaches sung on Good Friday take up God's complaint from earlier in this chapter (6:3).
How it serves prophecy
The verse belongs on this page because it shows what all prophecy is for. The prophets foretell so that people will turn; their predictions serve conversion, not curiosity. Jesus gives the same summary when he names “judgment and mercy and fidelity” as the weightier things of the law (Matthew 23:23).
Why unfolding
The requirement is asked of every generation, and it is how the Church waits for her Lord.
Micah 7:18–20PromiseFulfilledUnfolding
“Who is a God like you, who removes guilt and pardons sin for the remnant of his inheritance; Who does not persist in anger forever, but instead delights in mercy…”
Reading
The book ends with a play on the prophet's own name, which means “Who is like the LORD?” After all the judgments, the answer is mercy. God will tread our iniquities underfoot: “You will cast into the depths of the sea all our sins” (7:19), as Pharaoh's chariots were cast into the sea at the Exodus. And he will keep his oldest word: “You will show faithfulness to Jacob, and loyalty to Abraham, As you have sworn to our ancestors from days of old” (7:20).
Fulfilled
Mary and Zechariah sing of the same oath in the Gospel, God mindful of his mercy “to Abraham and to his descendants forever” (Luke 1:54–55, 72–73). The sins cast into the sea are taken away by the Lamb of God (John 1:29).
Why unfolding
The pardon is given now, above all in Baptism and in the sacrament of Penance, and it is received anew by every sinner who turns back.
See also
Exodus 15:4–5; Micah 7:8; Genesis 22:16–18.
