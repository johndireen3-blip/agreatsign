Prophecy in Proverbs

Most of Proverbs is practical advice for daily life. But in a few places Wisdom herself speaks: she was beside God when he made the world, she sets a table and calls the simple to come and eat. The Church heard in that voice the Word through whom all things were made.

The book at a glance

Proverbs is a collection of collections: an opening set of instructions and poems on Wisdom (chapters 1–9), the proverbs of Solomon (10:1–22:16 and 25–29), sayings of the wise, the words of Agur and of King Lemuel's mother (30–31), and a closing poem on the woman of worth (31:10–31). The book bears the name of Solomon (1:1), Israel's patron of wisdom, and some of its sayings may go back to his court. Many scholars think chapters 1–9 were added later as a frame and that the book reached its final form after the exile. The Church receives the whole, whatever the stages of its gathering, as inspired Scripture.

Proverbs is wisdom, not prophecy, and nearly all of it looks no further than the next day's choices. Its forward-looking passages are few, and they look forward chiefly through images, above all the figure of Woman Wisdom, which the New Testament and the Fathers read in the light of Christ. These are readings in the Church's spiritual sense, not predictions in the strict sense, and each card below says so.

The passages

Proverbs 8:22–31ImageFulfilled
“When he established the heavens, there was I” (8:27) … “then was I beside him as artisan; I was his delight day by day, playing before him all the while” (8:30).
Reading
Wisdom speaks as a person: “The LORD begot me, the beginning of his works” (8:22), “at the first, before the earth” (8:23). In its first sense this is poetry: God's wisdom personified, present in all his works and “having my delight with human beings” (8:31).
The Fathers' reading
The New Testament speaks of Christ in the same language. “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God … All things came to be through him” (John 1:1, 3). He is “the firstborn of all creation,” and “all things were created through him and for him” (Colossians 1:15–16). St. Paul calls Christ “the wisdom of God” (1 Corinthians 1:24). From St. Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho 61) onward, the Fathers read Wisdom in Proverbs 8 as the eternal Son. The passage was fought over in the Arian crisis, because the Greek Old Testament read “the Lord created me” in verse 22, and St. Athanasius answered at length that the verse cannot make the Son a creature (Orations against the Arians, book 2). The NABRE's “begot” stands close to the Church's confession of the Son “begotten, not made.”
In the liturgy
The Church also applies this text to the Virgin Mary. In the older Roman Missal it was read on her feasts, among them the Immaculate Conception and her Nativity: she whom God prepared before all ages for the Word's coming. This is a liturgical accommodation, not the passage's first meaning, and it honors Mary only because of the Son.
Why fulfilled
The reading belongs to the spiritual sense, but it rests on the New Testament itself: what Proverbs said of Wisdom, the Gospel says of the Word made flesh (John 1:14). This page marks it as the Fathers' reading.
Proverbs 9:1–6ImageFulfilledFuture
“Wisdom has built her house, she has set up her seven columns” (9:1) … “Come, eat of my food, and drink of the wine I have mixed!” (9:5)
Reading
Wisdom has prepared meat and wine and spread her table. She sends out her maidservants to call from the heights of the city, and invites the naive in: “Forsake foolishness that you may live” (9:6). Her rival, Folly, sets a table of her own that leads to death (9:13–18).
Fulfilled
Jesus speaks as Wisdom spoke. He calls the weary, “Come to me” (Matthew 11:28), says “wisdom is vindicated by her works” (Matthew 11:19), and gives his own flesh as food: “I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me will never hunger” (John 6:35); “the bread that I will give is my flesh for the life of the world” (John 6:51). St. Cyprian quotes this passage of Proverbs in his letter on the Eucharistic cup (Letter 63), and the Fathers often read Wisdom's house as the Incarnation or the Church and her table as the Eucharist. That is the Church's spiritual reading.
Future
The banquet points to the last feast of all: “Blessed are those who have been called to the wedding feast of the Lamb” (Revelation 19:9). Each Eucharist is a foretaste of that table.
Proverbs 30:4ImageMystery
“Who has gone up to heaven and come down again— who has cupped the wind in the hollow of the hand? … What is that person's name, or the name of his son?”
Reading
The words of Agur, whom the NABRE's note calls an unknown person. He confesses he has no knowledge of the Holy One and asks a string of questions, like God's questions to Job (Job 38), that only God can answer. The note explains that Agur denies having secret heavenly knowledge, and that God gives wisdom directly to those whose conduct pleases him. In its first sense the question “his son” is probably part of the riddle-form, not a doctrine.
Why mystery
Christian readers have long heard more. Jesus says, “No one has gone up to heaven except the one who has come down from heaven, the Son of Man” (John 3:13), and the God whose name Agur asks has revealed himself as Father and Son. The echo is real, but no New Testament writer cites this verse, so this page marks the Christian reading as a reverent echo rather than a fulfilled prophecy.
Proverbs 31:10–31ImageUnfoldingFuture
“Far beyond jewels is her value.” (31:10) … “the woman who fears the LORD is to be praised.” (31:30)
Reading
The book ends with an alphabetic poem, each of its twenty-two lines beginning with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet. It praises a woman of worth who feeds and clothes her household, trades and gives to the poor, and “laughs at the days to come” (31:25). The NABRE's note suggests the poem may be a portrait of a household ruled by Woman Wisdom, so the book that began with Wisdom's call ends with Wisdom at home.
A spiritual reading
The Fathers also read the woman as the Church, the bride of Christ. St. Augustine preached on this poem in that sense (Sermon 37). St. Paul teaches that marriage speaks “in reference to Christ and the church” (Ephesians 5:32), and that Christ gave himself to “present to himself the church in splendor, without spot or wrinkle” (Ephesians 5:27). This is a spiritual reading, not the poem's first meaning, which honors real wives and mothers.
Unfolding
The bride is still being made ready, in the faithful work of every household of faith.
Future
“The wedding day of the Lamb has come, his bride has made herself ready” (Revelation 19:7). John sees the new Jerusalem “prepared as a bride adorned for her husband” (Revelation 21:2).
