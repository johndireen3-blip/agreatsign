Prophecy in Jonah

A prophet who runs the other way, a great fish, a pagan city that repents in sackcloth, and a God who will not stop caring for it. Jesus called himself “greater than Jonah” and gave his own generation one sign only: the sign of Jonah.

The book at a glance

Jonah, son of Amittai, appears in 2 Kings 14:25 as a prophet in the eighth century before Christ, when Assyria and its great city Nineveh were Israel's most feared enemy. The book that bears his name is not a collection of oracles but a story about him, and the NABRE introduction, with many scholars, judges it to have been written after the exile, perhaps as a gentle rebuke to a view of God's care that stopped at Israel's borders. Others have read it as plain history. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture either way, and this page does not need to settle the question: what it teaches about God's mercy and about Christ is the same.

Its prophecy works in two ways. As prophecy in the first sense, it shows how God's warnings are meant: Nineveh is told it will be overthrown, it repents, and it is spared. And as a type, Jonah three days in the fish and then given back to preach to the nations is read by Jesus himself as a sign of his death and resurrection.

The passages

Jonah 1:1–16TypeUnfolding
“Set out for the great city of Nineveh, and preach against it; for their wickedness has come before me.”
Reading
God sends a prophet of Israel to Israel's enemy. “But Jonah made ready to flee to Tarshish, away from the LORD” (1:3), as far west as a ship could take him. A storm breaks; the pagan sailors pray to their gods while the prophet sleeps below, and in the end Jonah tells them to throw him into the sea so that they may live. They do, the sea grows calm, and the sailors fear the LORD and offer him sacrifice (1:16).
Why a type
The contrast with Jesus is the point. Jonah sleeps in the storm because he is running from God; Jesus sleeps in the storm because he is at peace with his Father, and rises to calm it with a word (Mark 4:37–41). Jonah is thrown into the sea for his own disobedience, and strangers are saved; Jesus gives himself up for the disobedience of others, and the nations are saved.
Why unfolding
The flight from a call is not only Jonah's. The NABRE introduction notes that the story conveys something of how the prophetic calling cannot finally be escaped. God goes on calling, and goes after those who run.
Jonah 2:1, 11TypeFulfilled
“But the LORD sent a great fish to swallow Jonah, and he remained in the belly of the fish three days and three nights.”
Reading
The sea that should have been Jonah's grave becomes the place of his rescue. After three days and three nights, “the LORD commanded the fish to vomit Jonah upon dry land” (2:11), and the prophet who had gone down is given back to finish his mission.
Fulfilled
Asked for a sign, Jesus answers: “An evil and unfaithful generation seeks a sign, but no sign will be given it except the sign of Jonah the prophet” (Matthew 12:39). And he explains: “Just as Jonah was in the belly of the whale three days and three nights, so will the Son of Man be in the heart of the earth three days and three nights” (Matthew 12:40). In Luke the sign is the prophet himself: “Just as Jonah became a sign to the Ninevites, so will the Son of Man be to this generation” (Luke 11:30). The Catechism notes that Jesus speaks of his resurrection, an event of a different order from the raisings he worked for others, as the “sign of Jonah” (CCC 994). The Fathers, St. Jerome among them in his Commentary on Jonah, read the whole story this way, as a figure of Christ's death and rising.
Note
The NABRE numbers this verse 2:1, and the verse about the fish casting Jonah out 2:11. Many other Bibles number them 1:17 and 2:10, so “Jonah 1:17” elsewhere is the same verse, and the prayer that follows (NABRE 2:2–10) is 2:1–9 there.
See also
Matthew 16:4; Luke 11:29–32; on the sign Jesus gave in the Temple, The Temple of His Body.
Jonah 2:3–10TypeFulfilledUnfolding
“Out of my distress I called to the LORD, and he answered me; From the womb of Sheol I cried for help, and you heard my voice.”
Reading
“Jonah prayed to the LORD, his God, from the belly of the fish” (2:2), and what he prays is a psalm of thanksgiving, sung as though the rescue had already come. He has gone down to the very bottom: “You cast me into the deep, into the heart of the sea” (2:4). Yet: “But you brought my life up from the pit, O LORD, my God” (2:7). The prayer ends, “deliverance is from the LORD” (2:10). The NABRE notes suggest the psalm may once have stood on its own before it was placed in the story; it is now part of the inspired book.
Fulfilled
Sheol, the pit and the deep are the Old Testament's words for death. The prayer of one brought up from them is fulfilled in the one who truly went down among the dead and was raised on the third day. The Church confesses that Christ descended to the realm of the dead and rose again (CCC 631–635).
Why unfolding
The prayer is still prayed. It is the prayer of every Christian who calls out of distress, and it is answered in Baptism, where we go down with Christ into death and rise with him to new life (Romans 6:4).
See also
Psalm 18:5–7; Psalm 30:4; Psalm 69:2–3; Acts 2:24–27.
Jonah 3:4–10ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“When God saw by their actions how they turned from their evil way, he repented of the evil he had threatened to do to them; he did not carry it out.”
Reading
Jonah's sermon is the shortest in the Bible: “Forty days more and Nineveh shall be overthrown” (3:4). There is no offer of mercy in it. Yet “the people of Nineveh believed God; they proclaimed a fast and all of them, great and small, put on sackcloth” (3:5). The king comes down from his throne and sits in ashes, reasoning, “Who knows? God may again repent and turn from his blazing wrath, so that we will not perish” (3:9). And God spares them.
Prophecy is conditional
The prophecy did not fail. It did what it was sent to do. God's warnings are given so that they need not come true; Jeremiah states the rule that when a nation turns from its evil, God relents of the evil he planned (Jeremiah 18:7–8). That is why this site does not treat prophecies of judgment as fixed timetables, and why it sets no dates. Scripture speaks of God “repenting” in human terms; what changes is not God's will but the people, and so the same unchanging will now meets them as mercy.
Fulfilled and unfolding
Jesus holds up Nineveh against his own hearers: “At the judgment, the men of Nineveh will arise with this generation and condemn it, because they repented at the preaching of Jonah; and there is something greater than Jonah here” (Matthew 12:41). The call to repent is still being preached, and the Church reads Nineveh's story in the first week of Lent, her own forty days of penance.
See also
Jeremiah 26:3; Joel 2:12–14; Ezekiel 33:11; Luke 11:32.
Jonah 4:1–11ProphecyUnfolding
“And should I not be concerned over the great city of Nineveh, in which there are more than a hundred and twenty thousand persons who cannot know their right hand from their left, not to mention all the animals?”
Reading
The success makes Jonah angry, and he finally says why he ran: “I knew that you are a gracious and merciful God, slow to anger, abounding in kindness, repenting of punishment” (4:2), echoing the words in which God revealed his name to Moses (Exodus 34:6). God answers with a question, “Are you right to be angry?” (4:4), and a gentle lesson: a plant that shades Jonah for a day and then withers. If Jonah grieves for a plant, may God not care for a city? The book ends on that question, unanswered, left for the reader.
Why unfolding
The question reaches its answer in the Gospel. The God who cared for Nineveh sends his Son for the whole world (John 3:16), and the risen Christ sends his apostles to “all nations” (Matthew 28:19). Jonah was sent unwillingly to one pagan city; the Church is sent gladly to every people, and her mission is not finished.
For now
Jonah is every believer tempted to want mercy for himself and justice for his enemies. The book does not condemn him; it invites him in, as the father in the parable invites the elder brother (Luke 15:28–32).
