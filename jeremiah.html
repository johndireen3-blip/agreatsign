Prophecy in Jeremiah

Jeremiah watched Jerusalem fall. He was mocked, beaten, thrown into a cistern and carried off to Egypt, and he wept for his people more than any other prophet. Yet in the darkest years he bought a field, wrote to the exiles of “a future of hope,” and heard the promise on which the whole New Testament is named: “I will make a new covenant.”

The book at a glance

Jeremiah, from a priestly family of Anathoth just north of Jerusalem, was called in the thirteenth year of King Josiah (about 627 BC) and preached for some forty years, through Josiah's reform, the rise of Babylon, the first deportation in 597 and the destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple in 587. After the fall he was taken against his will to Egypt (chapters 43–44). He dictated his oracles to his secretary Baruch, and when the king burned the scroll he dictated them again (chapter 36). The book gathers his oracles, Baruch's account of his sufferings, and later editing; its Greek form is about an eighth shorter than the Hebrew and places the oracles against the nations in a different order. Scholars differ on how much comes from the prophet himself; the Church receives the whole as inspired Scripture either way.

Jeremiah's prophecy is mostly warning: judgment is coming unless Judah turns back, and when it does not turn back, judgment comes. But the warnings carry promises inside them: a righteous branch for David, a return after seventy years, a new covenant written on the heart. His own life became a kind of prophecy too. The suffering prophet, “like a trusting lamb led to slaughter” (11:19), was so like the one to come that when Jesus asked who people said he was, some answered “Jeremiah” (Matthew 16:14). And his oracles against Babylon gave Revelation many of its images for the city that falls.

The passages

Jeremiah 1:4–10TypeFulfilled
“Before I formed you in the womb I knew you, before you were born I dedicated you, a prophet to the nations I appointed you.”
Reading
Jeremiah protests, “I do not know how to speak. I am too young!” (1:6), and God answers, “Do not be afraid of them, for I am with you to deliver you” (1:8). He touches the young man's mouth: “See, I place my words in your mouth!” (1:9). The mission has two sides, “to uproot and to tear down, to destroy and to demolish, to build and to plant” (1:10), and the book follows that order: first the tearing down, then the planting.
Fulfilled
Jeremiah's call is a pattern that others recognize in their own. St. Paul describes his calling in Jeremiah's words, as the God “who from my mother's womb had set me apart” (Galatians 1:15). The prophet sent to the nations, rejected by his own city and led like a lamb to slaughter (11:19), foreshadows Christ, the Word sent to all nations, whom some took for Jeremiah returned (Matthew 16:14).
See also
The Catechism cites this verse in its teaching that human life must be respected and protected absolutely from the moment of conception (CCC 2270, using a translation that reads “consecrated” where the NABRE has “dedicated”).
Jeremiah 2:1–13ImageFulfilledFuture
“Two evils my people have done: they have forsaken me, the source of living waters; They have dug themselves cisterns, broken cisterns that cannot hold water.” (2:13)
Reading
Jeremiah's first oracle begins with tenderness: “I remember the devotion of your youth” (2:2), when Israel followed God through the desert like a bride. Then comes the charge. A spring of fresh running water was theirs for the asking, and they chose cracked tanks dug in the rock that leak and leave only mud: idols, and alliances with Egypt and Assyria (2:18). The sin is not only disobedience but a foolish exchange.
Fulfilled
Jesus offers the Samaritan woman what Jeremiah's people had forsaken: “he would have given you living water,” and “the water I shall give will become in him a spring of water welling up to eternal life” (John 4:10, 14). At the feast he cries out, “Let anyone who thirsts come to me and drink” (John 7:37). The Catechism meditates on that meeting at the well, “God thirsts that we may thirst for him,” and quotes this verse in its own translation: “They have forsaken me, the fountain of living waters” (CCC 2560–2561).
Future
Revelation ends with the same invitation: “Let the one who thirsts come forward, and the one who wants it receive the gift of life-giving water” (Revelation 22:17; compare 21:6). No one is turned away from the spring.
Jeremiah 3:14–15PromiseFulfilledUnfolding
“I will appoint for you shepherds after my own heart, who will shepherd you wisely and prudently.” (3:15)
Reading
God calls the scattered people home: “Return, rebellious children—oracle of the LORD” (3:14). He promises to gather them to Zion and to give them shepherds of his own choosing, unlike the rulers who led them astray (see 23:1–6). The next verses promise that they will no longer miss the ark of the covenant (3:16–17; see the hidden ark).
Fulfilled
David was remembered as the shepherd after God's own heart (1 Samuel 13:14; Acts 13:22), and Christ is the Good Shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep (John 10:11). The risen Lord gives his Church shepherds in his turn, beginning with Peter: “Feed my sheep” (John 21:17), and the gifts of pastors and teachers (Ephesians 4:11).
Unfolding
St. John Paul II took this verse as the opening words and the title of his 1992 apostolic exhortation on the formation of priests, Pastores dabo vobis (“I will give you shepherds”). The promise is still being kept wherever God calls men and women to care for his people, and the Church still prays for it.
Jeremiah 7:1–15ProphecyFulfilled
“Has this house which bears my name become in your eyes a den of thieves?” (7:11)
Reading
At the gate of the Temple Jeremiah warns those who trust in the building instead of in God: “Do not put your trust in these deceptive words: ‘The temple of the LORD! The temple of the LORD! The temple of the LORD!’” (7:4). If they reform their ways and do justice to the stranger, the orphan and the widow, God will remain “so that I may dwell with you in this place” (7:3). If not, the Temple will go the way of Shiloh, the old sanctuary that lay in ruins (7:12–14). When he repeats the sermon in chapter 26, priests and prophets cry, “Sentence this man to death! He has prophesied against this city!” (26:11).
Fulfilled
Babylon burned the Temple in 587 BC. Six centuries later Jesus drives the sellers from the Temple and joins Isaiah's words to Jeremiah's: “It is written: ‘My house shall be a house of prayer,’ but you are making it a den of thieves” (Matthew 21:13; Isaiah 56:7). The Catechism notes that he acted out of zeal for his Father's house (CCC 584), and that he announced the coming destruction of the Temple (CCC 585; see Matthew 24:2), which fell in AD 70. Like Jeremiah, he was accused at his trial of speaking against the Temple (Matthew 26:61).
Note
The warning is for every age. A holy place, or membership in a holy people, is no guarantee for those who do not live by it. St. Paul tells Christians that they themselves are God's temple (1 Corinthians 3:16–17).
Jeremiah 11:18–23TypeFulfilled
“Yet I was like a trusting lamb led to slaughter, not knowing that they were hatching plots against me: ‘Let us destroy the tree in its vigor; let us cut him off from the land of the living, so that his name will no longer be remembered.’” (11:19)
Reading
The men of his own town, Anathoth, plot to kill Jeremiah for his preaching (11:21). He learns of it only when God reveals it to him (11:18). This is the first of the laments known as his “confessions” (see the card on 20:7–18). The NABRE cross-references Wisdom 2:20, where the wicked plot against the just one.
Fulfilled
Jeremiah stands beside the Servant of Isaiah, led like a sheep to the slaughter and cut off from the land of the living (Isaiah 53:7–8; see Prophecy in Isaiah). When the Ethiopian official is reading that passage, “Like a sheep he was led to the slaughter” (Acts 8:32), Philip begins with it and proclaims Jesus to him (Acts 8:35). The innocent prophet rejected by his own town foreshadows the Lamb rejected by his own (John 1:11).
The Fathers' reading
The ancient Greek translation read the plotters' words differently, as a plan to put wood into his bread. St. Justin Martyr cites the verse in that form as a prophecy of the Cross (Dialogue with Trypho 72). This is a reading of the Greek text, which the NABRE, translating the Hebrew, does not have.
Jeremiah 18:1–10ImageUnfolding
“Indeed, like clay in the hand of the potter, so are you in my hand, house of Israel.”
Reading
God sends Jeremiah to watch a potter at his wheel. “Whenever the vessel of clay he was making turned out badly in his hand, he tried again” (18:4). Then the lesson: “Can I not do to you, house of Israel, as this potter has done?” (18:6). The passage goes on to say plainly that God's threats are conditional: “if that nation against whom I have decreed turns from its evil, then I will have a change of heart” (18:8). This is the key to all prophetic warning. It is spoken so that it need not come true, as Nineveh learned (Jonah 3).
Unfolding
Isaiah had prayed, “we are the clay and you our potter” (Isaiah 64:7), and St. Paul takes up the image again (Romans 9:20–21). The potter does not throw the spoiled clay away; he works it again. That is how God still deals with sinners and with nations: while there is time, there is the offer to be reshaped.
See also
In chapter 19 Jeremiah smashes a finished flask to show what happens to a vessel that has hardened. Matthew's account of the potter's field (Matthew 27:9–10) draws on these chapters together with Zechariah 11:12–13, as the NABRE note there explains.
Jeremiah 19:1–13ImageFulfilledFuture
“Thus will I smash this people and this city, as one smashes a clay pot so that it cannot be repaired.” (19:11)
Reading
“Thus said the LORD: Go, buy a potter's earthenware flask” (19:1). Jeremiah takes elders and priests to the Valley of Ben-hinnom, where children had been burned in sacrifice to idols (19:4–5; 7:31), and announces that it will be renamed “the Valley of Slaughter” (19:6). Then he breaks the flask before their eyes (19:10). In chapter 18 the soft clay could still be reshaped (18:1–10); a fired pot can only be broken. There comes a point where refusal hardens.
Fulfilled
Jerusalem fell to Babylon in 587 BC, as Jeremiah had warned. Yet even this was not God's last word: the Book of Consolation (chapters 30–31) comes after it.
Future
The Valley of Hinnom, in Hebrew ge-hinnom, gave its name to Gehenna, the word Jesus uses for final loss (Matthew 5:22, 29–30). The Catechism notes that Jesus often speaks of Gehenna and the unquenchable fire reserved for those who to the end of their lives refuse to believe and be converted (CCC 1034). The warning is spoken so that it need not come true: while there is time, the clay can still be reshaped.
Jeremiah 20:7–18TypeFulfilledUnfolding
“You seduced me, LORD, and I let myself be seduced; you were too strong for me, and you prevailed.” (20:7)
Reading
Scattered through chapters 11–20 are laments that scholars call the “confessions” of Jeremiah (11:18–12:6; 15:10–21; 17:14–18; 18:18–23; 20:7–18). No prophet lets us see so far into his heart. “Woe to me, my mother, that you gave me birth!” (15:10); “Why is my pain continuous, my wound incurable, refusing to be healed?” (15:18). Yet he also says, “When I found your words, I devoured them” (15:16). He decides to stop speaking in God's name, “But then it is as if fire is burning in my heart, imprisoned in my bones” (20:9). He hears the whispers, “Terror on every side!” (20:10; compare Psalm 31:14), and confesses, “But the LORD is with me, like a mighty champion” (20:11), only to cry a few verses later, “Cursed be the day” he was born (20:14). Faith and anguish stand side by side, and Scripture does not tidy them up.
Fulfilled
The prophet who could not stop speaking the word and was rejected for it foreshadows the Word himself. In Gethsemane and on the Cross Jesus prayed with the same honesty: “he offered prayers and supplications with loud cries and tears to the one who was able to save him from death” (Hebrews 5:7).
Unfolding
The Catechism says of the prophets' prayer: “Their prayer is not flight from this unfaithful world, but rather attentiveness to the Word of God” (CCC 2584). Jeremiah gives every servant of the word, and every suffering believer, permission to pray honestly, complaint included, and to keep going.
Jeremiah 23:5–6ProphecyFulfilledFuture
“As king he shall reign and govern wisely, he shall do what is just and right in the land.”
Reading
After condemning the shepherds who scattered the flock (23:1–2), God promises: “I will raise up a righteous branch for David” (23:5). The new king's name will be “The LORD our justice” (23:6), a pointed contrast with the last king of Judah, Zedekiah, whose name means “the LORD is justice” but who did not live up to it. The tree of David has been cut down; a branch will grow from it, as Isaiah also promised (Isaiah 11:1).
Fulfilled
The Church reads the branch as Christ, the son of David who reigns with justice. St. Augustine quotes this oracle among Jeremiah's prophecies of Christ (City of God 18.33). The Church reads it at Mass on December 18, beside the angel's word to Joseph, “son of David” (Matthew 1:20).
Future
“In his days Judah shall be saved, Israel shall dwell in security” (23:6). The reign of justice has begun in Christ, but the land of perfect security is the city still to come (Revelation 21:3–4).
Jeremiah 25:11–12ProphecyFulfilled
“Seventy years these nations shall serve the king of Babylon; but when the seventy years have elapsed, I will punish the king of Babylon and that nation and the land of the Chaldeans for their guilt.”
Reading
Babylon is God's instrument of judgment, but not forever. Seventy years is a round number, a lifetime: long enough that those who went into exile would mostly not see the return. It can be counted from the first deportations (605 BC) to the decree of Cyrus (538) or from the burning of the Temple (587) to its rebuilding (515); the Bible itself does not tie it to one reckoning.
Fulfilled
Babylon fell to Cyrus of Persia in 539 BC, and Ezra records that Cyrus let the exiles go home “in order to fulfill the word of the LORD spoken by Jeremiah” (Ezra 1:1; compare 2 Chronicles 36:21–22).
See also
Daniel, near the end of the exile, “perceived in the books the number of years the LORD had decreed to the prophet Jeremiah: Jerusalem was to lie in ruins for seventy years” (Daniel 9:2). His prayer over that number leads to the vision of the seventy weeks of years (Daniel 9:24–27), one of the great unsettled passages of Scripture. See Prophecy in Daniel.
Jeremiah 25:15–29ImageFulfilledFuture
“Take this cup of the wine of wrath from my hand and have all the nations to whom I will send you drink it.” (25:15)
Reading
Judgment is pictured as a cup that makes the nations reel: “They shall drink, and retch, and go mad, because of the sword I will send among them” (25:16). It begins at home, “Now that I am inflicting evil on this city, called by my name” (25:29), and goes out to all the kingdoms of the earth. Refusing is not possible: “Thus says the LORD of hosts: You must drink!” (25:28). In Scripture God's wrath is not a temper but the steady opposition of his justice to evil.
Fulfilled
In Gethsemane Jesus prays, “My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from me; yet, not as I will, but as you will” (Matthew 26:39). The Catechism teaches that he accepts from his Father's hands in his agony “The cup of the New Covenant, which Jesus anticipated when he offered himself at the Last Supper” (CCC 612). Christian writers have long seen here the cup of judgment drunk by the one innocent man, so that it becomes for us the cup of salvation (Psalm 116:13). This is a theological reading of the image, not a claim that Jeremiah foresaw it.
Future
Revelation takes up the cup for the end of every proud power. Those who worship the beast will drink “the wine of God's fury, poured full strength into the cup of his wrath” (Revelation 14:10), and “God remembered great Babylon, giving it the cup filled with the wine of his fury and wrath” (16:19). These are images of God's justice against evil, not a timetable of events.
Jeremiah 27–28ImageFulfilled
“By breaking a wooden yoke bar, you make an iron yoke!” (28:13)
Reading
“The LORD said to me: Make for yourself thongs and yoke bars and put them on your shoulders” (27:2). Wearing a yoke, Jeremiah tells the envoys of the neighboring kings, and then King Zedekiah: “Bend your necks to the yoke of the king of Babylon; serve him and his people, so that you may live” (27:12). The prophet Hananiah breaks the yoke off his neck and promises that the exiles will be home within two years (28:2–4, 10–11). Jeremiah answers, “Amen! thus may the LORD do!” (28:6); he would gladly be wrong. But a prophet of peace is shown to be sent by God “only when his word comes to pass” (28:9; compare Deuteronomy 18:21–22). God's word comes back: “The LORD has not sent you” (28:15), and “Hananiah the prophet died in that year, in the seventh month” (28:17).
Fulfilled
The exile came and lasted, as Jeremiah said, and the rebellion Hananiah encouraged ended in the ruin of Jerusalem (chapter 39). Christ offers a different yoke, not of empire but of love: “Take my yoke upon you and learn from me, for I am meek and humble of heart; and you will find rest for yourselves. For my yoke is easy, and my burden light” (Matthew 11:29–30).
Note
Jesus warns against false prophets (Matthew 7:15; 24:11), and St. John tells Christians to test the spirits (1 John 4:1). A prediction is not truer for being comforting, nor for being frightening. This site sets no dates and names no one as a fulfillment, in keeping with the Church's caution.
Jeremiah 29:10–14PromiseFulfilledUnfolding
“For I know well the plans I have in mind for you—oracle of the LORD—plans for your welfare and not for woe, so as to give you a future of hope.”
Reading
Jeremiah writes from Jerusalem to the first exiles in Babylon. False prophets were promising a quick return; Jeremiah tells them to build houses, plant gardens and pray for the city where they live, because the exile will be long. Then comes the promise: “Only after seventy years have elapsed for Babylon will I deal with you and fulfill for you my promise to bring you back to this place” (29:10). “When you look for me, you will find me. Yes, when you seek me with all your heart, I will let you find me” (29:13–14).
Fulfilled
The exiles' children and grandchildren came home, as the previous card shows. The plan of welfare was real, though most who first heard the letter did not live to see it.
Unfolding
This is one of the most loved verses in Scripture, often given to one person as a private promise. It was first spoken to a whole people in exile, and it teaches patience as much as comfort: God's plan for welfare runs through long years in a strange land. Christians, who are “sojourners” in this world (1 Peter 2:11), may rightly take it to heart in that sense. The promise to be found by those who seek with all their heart still holds for everyone.
Jeremiah 30–31; 31:3PromiseFulfilledMystery
“With age-old love I have loved you; so I have kept my mercy toward you.” (31:3)
Reading
In the middle of a book of warnings, chapters 30–31 form the Book of Consolation, which God tells Jeremiah to write down (30:2). It promises the return of the scattered, the rebuilding of the city, and a king from David's line (30:9). God speaks as a parent: “I am a father to Israel” (31:9); “I will turn their mourning into joy” (31:13). Rachel weeping and the new covenant, both on this page, belong to the same book.
Fulfilled
The love that is “age-old” is not a reaction to Israel's faithfulness; it was there first and outlasts every betrayal. The New Testament sees it revealed fully in the sending of the Son: “In this is love: not that we have loved God, but that he loved us and sent his Son as expiation for our sins” (1 John 4:10).
Why mystery
The book contains one of the strangest lines in the prophets: “The LORD has created a new thing upon the earth: woman encompasses man” (31:22). The NABRE note admits, “No satisfactory explanation has been given for this text.” From early centuries many Christian interpreters have read it of the Virgin who would carry the Son of God in her womb. That is a spiritual reading, not an established literal sense, and the verse remains open.
Jeremiah 31:15–17TypeFulfilled
“In Ramah is heard the sound of sobbing, bitter weeping! Rachel mourns for her children, she refuses to be consoled for her children—they are no more!”
Reading
Rachel, the mother of Joseph and Benjamin, died near Bethlehem (Genesis 35:19). Jeremiah hears her weeping from her grave at Ramah, north of Jerusalem, where the captives were gathered before being marched to Babylon (Jeremiah 40:1). But the oracle does not end in tears: “Cease your cries of weeping, hold back your tears! … There is hope for your future—oracle of the LORD—your children shall return to their own territory” (31:16–17).
Fulfilled
When Herod kills the boys of Bethlehem, Matthew writes: “Then was fulfilled what had been said through Jeremiah the prophet: A voice was heard in Ramah, sobbing and loud lamentation; Rachel weeping for her children, and she would not be consoled, since they were no more” (Matthew 2:17–18). Matthew does not say “in order that”; God did not will the massacre. The ancient grief of Israel's mothers is heard again at Bethlehem, and the hope Jeremiah attached to it comes with it: the child who escaped is the one who will bring all the scattered children home. The Church keeps the Holy Innocents on December 28.
Jeremiah 31:31–34PromiseFulfilledUnfoldingFuture
“I will place my law within them, and write it upon their hearts; I will be their God, and they shall be my people.”
Reading
“See, days are coming—oracle of the LORD—when I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah” (31:31). It will not be like the covenant of Sinai, which “they broke” (31:32). The law will no longer be carved only on stone but written within. And it ends in mercy: “for I will forgive their iniquity and no longer remember their sin” (31:34). This is the only place in the Old Testament that speaks of a “new covenant” in these words. The Catechism lists it among the prophets' promises of “a new and everlasting Covenant intended for all, to be written on their hearts” (CCC 64).
Fulfilled
At the Last Supper Jesus takes the cup and says, “This cup is the new covenant in my blood, which will be shed for you” (Luke 22:20; compare 1 Corinthians 11:25). The Letter to the Hebrews quotes the whole oracle, “Behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, when I will conclude a new covenant” (Hebrews 8:8–12), and quotes it again (10:16–17). From this promise the second part of the Bible takes its name: the New Testament, the new covenant.
Unfolding
The Catechism calls the New Law of the Gospel the perfection here on earth of the divine law, the work of the Holy Spirit who writes it on hearts, and cites this promise through Hebrews (CCC 1965). It is unfolding wherever grace makes God's law the desire of a heart rather than a burden on it.
Future
“Everyone, from least to greatest, shall know me” (31:34) is not yet true of the world. Its completion is the city where “they will be his people and God himself will always be with them” (Revelation 21:3).
Jeremiah 32:6–15PromiseFulfilled
“They shall again purchase houses and fields and vineyards in this land.”
Reading
Jerusalem is under siege and Jeremiah is under arrest in “the court of the guard” (32:8) when his cousin offers him the family field at Anathoth, already in Babylonian hands. No sane man would buy. Jeremiah buys it, “weighing out for him the silver, seventeen shekels of silver” (32:9), has the deed signed, sealed and stored in a clay jar, and explains why (32:15). Then he prays: “Ah, my Lord GOD! You made the heavens and the earth with your great power and your outstretched arm; nothing is too difficult for you” (32:17). God answers with the same words turned into a question: “I am the LORD, the God of all the living! Is anything too difficult for me?” (32:27).
Fulfilled
The exiles returned and the land was bought and sold again (Ezra 2; Nehemiah 11). The deed in the clay jar is a picture of hope as an act, not only a feeling: investing in God's future while the city burns. The angel's word to Mary, that nothing will be impossible for God (Luke 1:37), echoes the same faith.
Jeremiah 33:14–16PromiseFulfilledMystery
“In those days, at that time, I will make a just shoot spring up for David; he shall do what is right and just in the land.”
Reading
“The days are coming—oracle of the LORD—when I will fulfill the promise I made to the house of Israel and the house of Judah” (33:14). The oracle repeats 23:5–6 with a change: now it is Jerusalem that receives the name, “this is the name they shall call her: ‘The LORD our justice’” (33:16). The king's justice becomes the city's.
Fulfilled
The Church opens Advent with this passage (First Sunday of Advent, Year C). The just shoot is Christ, and the city that bears his justice is his Church, the new Jerusalem prepared as a bride (Revelation 21:2).
Why mystery
The NABRE note observes that 33:14–26 is the longest continuous passage of the Hebrew Jeremiah missing from the Greek, and is probably the work of a later writer applying Jeremiah's words to a new situation; it adds that the hope of an unbroken Davidic dynasty and priesthood was not realized after the exile. The Church receives the passage as inspired Scripture, and its literal hope is met in a way no one expected: in one Son of David who is King and Priest forever (Hebrews 7:24).
Jeremiah 50–51ImageFulfilledFuture
“Babylon was a golden cup in the hand of the LORD making the whole earth drunk.” (51:7)
Reading
The longest of Jeremiah's oracles against the nations is against the empire that destroyed Jerusalem. God used Babylon, and now Babylon is judged for its pride: “Babylon suddenly falls and is broken: wail over her!” (51:8). God's people are told to get out: “Flee from Babylon” (51:6); “Leave her, my people; each of you save your own life” (51:45). Heaven and earth “shall shout over Babylon with joy” (51:48).
Fulfilled
Babylon fell to the Persians in 539 BC and in time became a ruin, as Isaiah and Jeremiah had both foretold (Isaiah 21:9).
Future
Revelation takes up these chapters for the final city of pride. The harlot “who lives near the many waters” (Revelation 17:1; compare Jeremiah 51:13) holds “a gold cup” (17:4); “all the nations have drunk the wine of her licentious passion” (18:3); a voice from heaven cries, “Depart from her, my people” (18:4); and heaven is told to rejoice over her (18:20). The table below sets the echoes side by side.
Note
Babylon in Revelation names the proud city of this world in every age, beginning with the Rome that persecuted the first Christians (see 1 Peter on 5:13). It is not a name to be pinned on any church or nation today. The call to “depart” is a call to holiness, not to fear.
Jeremiah 51:59–64ImageFulfilledFuture
“When you have finished reading this scroll, tie a stone to it and throw it into the Euphrates, and say: Thus Babylon shall sink. It will never rise, because of the disaster I am bringing upon it.”
Reading
Jeremiah writes his oracles against Babylon “on one scroll” (51:60) and sends them with Seraiah, a court officer going to Babylon with King Zedekiah. Read aloud in the city, then sunk in its river, the scroll is a prophecy acted out. The book of his words ends there: “Thus far the words of Jeremiah” (51:64).
Future
John sees the same sign on a cosmic scale: “A mighty angel picked up a stone like a huge millstone and threw it into the sea and said: With such force will Babylon the great city be thrown down, and will never be found again” (Revelation 18:21). The river of one empire becomes the sea of all history; the city that sinks is every power that sets itself against God. What it leaves behind is the city that comes down from heaven.

“The place is to remain unknown until God gathers his people together again and shows them mercy.” (2 Maccabees 2:7)
Reading
The ark of the covenant disappears from the Bible with the fall of Jerusalem. Jeremiah himself foretells a day when no one will need it: “They will no longer think of it, or remember it, or miss it, or make another one” (Jeremiah 3:16), for Jerusalem itself will be called “the LORD's throne” (3:17). Centuries later, 2 Maccabees reports a tradition found in old records: that the prophet took the tent, the ark and the altar of incense to the mountain of Moses, “found a chamber in a cave,” “then he sealed the entrance” (2 Maccabees 2:5), and said its place would stay hidden until God gathers his people.
Revelation
When the seventh trumpet sounds, “God's temple in heaven was opened, and the ark of his covenant could be seen in the temple” (Revelation 11:19), and at once John sees “a great sign”: the woman clothed with the sun (12:1). The Church calls Mary “the ark of the covenant, the place where the glory of the Lord dwells” (CCC 2676), and many have read the two verses together. See Revelation 12.
Why mystery
The hiding of the ark by Jeremiah is a tradition that 2 Maccabees cites from earlier writings, not an event the Book of Jeremiah narrates. Where the earthly ark lies is unknown, and claims to have found it are not part of the faith. What the tradition and Revelation agree on is that the presence of God was not lost with the ark.
