Revelation 12: The Woman and the Dragon

At the center of the Book of Revelation, heaven opens and a sign appears: a woman in labor, a dragon waiting to devour her child, and a war that reaches from heaven to earth. This page reads the chapter with the Church and sorts its prophecies into what is fulfilled, unfolding, future and mystery.

The ark and the woman

Chapter 12 cannot be read apart from the verse just before it. When the seventh trumpet sounds, “God's temple in heaven was opened, and the ark of his covenant could be seen in the temple” (Revelation 11:19). The ark had been lost since the Babylonian conquest. John's readers would have strained to see it. What John sees next is not a gold chest but a woman.

The Church has long heard in this a picture of Mary as the Ark of the New Covenant. The old ark carried the tablets of the Law, the manna and Aaron's rod; Mary carried the Word made flesh, the Bread of Life and the eternal Priest. Luke tells the Visitation in words that echo David bringing up the ark (2 Samuel 6), and the Church prays to Mary as Ark of the Covenant in the Litany of Loreto.

Who is the woman?

Catholic tradition gives three answers, and holds them together rather than choosing one.

Israel

The twelve stars recall the twelve tribes and Joseph's dream of sun, moon and stars (Genesis 37:9). The People of God labored through the centuries to bring forth the Messiah (Isaiah 66:7–8; Micah 5:2).

Mary

She gives birth to the son “destined to rule all the nations with an iron rod” (12:5; Psalm 2:9), who can only be Christ. Mary is the woman of Genesis 3:15, of Cana and of the Cross, whom Jesus twice calls “Woman.”

The Church

The woman flees to the desert and is nourished there, and the dragon wars against “the rest of her offspring, those who keep God's commandments and bear witness to Jesus” (12:17). That is the Church in every age.

The earliest surviving Latin commentary on Revelation, by St. Victorinus of Pettau (martyred around 304), reads the woman as the ancient Church of the fathers, prophets and apostles. Later writers saw Mary first. The USCCB notes to the New American Bible keep both: the woman is God's people of the Old and New Testaments, and the Church has also seen in her Mary, the mother of Jesus. Pope St. John Paul II reads Revelation 12:1 alongside Genesis 3:15 and the “Woman” of John's Gospel (Redemptoris Mater 24), and Pius XII notes that theologians saw in the woman clothed with the sun a sign of Mary's Assumption (Munificentissimus Deus 27).

The passages, verse by verse

The chapter moves in three steps: the woman and the child (12:1–6), the war in heaven (12:7–12), and the war on earth (12:13–17).

Revelation 12:1–2MysteryFulfilled
“A great sign appeared in the sky, a woman clothed with the sun, with the moon under her feet, and on her head a crown of twelve stars.”
Reading
The woman is Israel, Mary and the Church at once. Her labor is the long waiting of Israel and, as many Fathers read it, the sorrow of Mary at Calvary, where she became Mother of the Church (John 19:26–27).
Why two labels
The birth of the Messiah is fulfilled. The sign itself, the woman crowned in heaven, is still being revealed as the Church grows toward her glory.
Revelation 12:3–4Mystery
The huge red dragon with seven heads and ten horns sweeps a third of the stars from the sky.
Reading
The seven heads and ten horns point ahead to the beast of chapter 13 and to Daniel 7. The stars swept down have been read as the angels who fell with Satan, and as members of the Church drawn away by deception, apostasy or heresy (compare Daniel 8:10).
Why mystery
The Church has not settled which reading is meant, and both have patristic support. St. Victorinus of Pettau gives both at once: the verse “may be taken in two ways,” either that the devil “may be able to seduce the third part of the men who believe,” or that “of the angels that were subject to him … he seduced the third part” (Commentary on the Apocalypse, ch. 12). St. Methodius of Olympus reads the stars as “the bodies of heresies,” those who claimed a place in the heavens and were “dragged down” (Symposium 8.10). St. Gregory the Great reads them as people who seemed to shine in the Church but fall away to pursue worldly glory (Moralia in Job 32.15), and St. Bede as angels and men together (Explanation of the Apocalypse, on 12:4). More on the Fathers and Revelation 12 is on Fathers, Saints & Teachers.
Revelation 12:5Fulfilled
“She gave birth to a son, a male child, destined to rule all the nations with an iron rod. Her child was caught up to God and his throne.”
Reading
The whole mystery of Christ in one sentence: his birth, and his being “caught up” in the Resurrection and Ascension. The dragon fails. Psalm 2:9 is fulfilled in the risen King.
See also
Isaiah 7:14; Micah 5:1–2; Psalm 2; Acts 1:9–11.
Revelation 12:6Unfolding
The woman flees into the desert, to a place prepared by God, for twelve hundred and sixty days.
Reading
1,260 days is three and a half years, the “time, two times and half a time” of Daniel 7:25: a limited time of trial. The desert recalls Israel fed with manna on the way to the promised land. The Church lives this now, sheltered and fed by God, above all in the Eucharist.
See also
Exodus 16; 1 Kings 19:4–8; Forty-two months in Revelation.
Revelation 12:7–12FulfilledUnfolding
“They conquered him by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony; love for life did not deter them from death.” (12:11)
Reading
Michael and his angels cast the dragon out of heaven. Heaven's song makes clear whose victory it is: the blood of the Lamb. Jesus said, “Now the ruler of this world will be driven out” (John 12:31), and “I have observed Satan fall like lightning from the sky” (Luke 10:18).
Why two labels
Christ's victory is won. The martyrs' share in it continues in every generation.
Revelation 12:13–17Unfolding
“Then the dragon became angry with the woman and went off to wage war against the rest of her offspring, those who keep God's commandments and bear witness to Jesus.” (12:17)
Reading
Given “the two wings of the great eagle” (compare Exodus 19:4), the woman is carried to safety. The earth swallows the dragon's flood. Unable to destroy her, the dragon turns on her children. This is the struggle of the Church in history, which the Catechism links to the Our Father's last petition (CCC 2853).
Looking ahead
The dragon stands on the sand of the sea (12:18) and calls up the beast of chapter 13. His final end is still Future (Revelation 20:10).

With the Fathers and the Church

Genesis 3:15. “I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers.” Revelation 12 shows that enmity reaching its climax: the ancient serpent (12:9) against the woman and her offspring.

St. Irenaeus of Lyons (died c. 202), bishop and Doctor of the Church, already called Mary the New Eve: “the knot of Eve's disobedience was loosed by the obedience of Mary. For what the virgin Eve had bound fast through unbelief, this did the virgin Mary set free through faith” (Against Heresies 3.22.4). Mary became “the patroness (advocata) of the virgin Eve” (5.19.1). Irenaeus does not comment on the woman of Revelation 12, but he does speak of the dragon who “will with his tail cause a third part of the stars to fall from their place” (2.31.3).

St. Victorinus of Pettau (died c. 304), bishop and martyr, wrote the earliest surviving Latin commentary on Revelation. The woman clothed with the sun is “the ancient Church of fathers, and prophets, and saints, and apostles”; her crown of twelve stars is “the choir of fathers”; her child is Christ, “the fruit of its people according to the flesh”; and the eagle's wings given her are “the two prophets” (Commentary on the Apocalypse, ch. 12). His commentary survives in a later, revised form, so its wording is not always his own.

See Fathers, Saints & Teachers for these and other witnesses on Revelation 12.

The liturgy. The Church reads Revelation 11:19a; 12:1–6a, 10ab at Mass on the Solemnity of the Assumption (August 15), and the Catechism places “the Woman” of Revelation 12 among those who share in the heavenly liturgy (CCC 1138).

The Second Vatican Council calls Mary, already glorified in body and soul, “a sign of sure hope and solace to the people of God” on their pilgrimage (Lumen Gentium 68).

The Woman in approved apparitions

Several apparitions the Church has recognized carry the imagery of Revelation 12. They do not interpret Scripture for us; they echo it. Each has its own page showing the Church's judgment and what the messages ask.

Read with careRevelation 12 has drawn many date-setting schemes, including claims about star alignments that “fulfill” the sign in a particular year. The Church reads the sign as the mystery of Christ, his mother and his people, not as a calendar. No one knows the day or the hour (Matthew 24:36), and private revelation never adds to what Christ has revealed (CCC 67).

For reflection

Where am I in the desert?

The woman is not spared the desert; she is fed in it. Where in my life is God sheltering and feeding me in a time of trial, and do I come to the Eucharist as the manna for that journey?
