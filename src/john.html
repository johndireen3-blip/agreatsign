Prophecy in John

John's Gospel has no Olivet Discourse. Its prophecy works through signs and types: the Lamb, the ladder, the temple, the serpent lifted up. And it speaks of an “hour” that is coming and is already here, so that the last things begin in Christ even before his return.

The book at a glance

Tradition attributes the fourth Gospel to the apostle John, the disciple whom Jesus loved (13:23; 21:20, 24), writing late in the first century. After a prologue on the Word made flesh, it falls into two parts: a Book of Signs (chapters 1–12), in which seven signs reveal who Jesus is, and a Book of Glory (chapters 13–21), the Last Supper, the Passion and the Resurrection.

John looks forward in his own way. He shows the Old Testament fulfilled through types: the Passover lamb, Jacob's stairway, the Temple, the bronze serpent. He records Jesus' prophecies of his death, his resurrection and the fate of Peter. And he joins present and future: eternal life begins now for whoever believes, while the resurrection of the dead and Christ's coming are still to come.

The passages

John 1:14TypeFulfilled
“And the Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us, and we saw his glory, the glory as of the Father’s only Son, full of grace and truth.”
Reading
The NABRE note explains that “made his dwelling” literally means he pitched his tent, or tabernacle. The Word dwells among us as God once dwelt in the tent of meeting in the desert (Exodus 25:8–9), and the glory that filled it is now seen in a human life. The Catechism takes this verse as the source of the Church's word “Incarnation” (CCC 461). John's next lines set Christ beside Moses: “while the law was given through Moses, grace and truth came through Jesus Christ” (1:17).
Why fulfilled
The tabernacle and the Temple were types; the Word made flesh is what they foreshadowed (2:21). The image returns at the end, when God's dwelling is with the human race in the new Jerusalem (Revelation 21:3).
John 1:29TypeFulfilled
“Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world.”
Reading
John the Baptist gathers two figures into one title. Jesus is the servant of Isaiah 53:7, led like a lamb to the slaughter and bearing the sins of many, and he is the Passover lamb of Exodus 12, whose blood marked Israel's redemption. The Catechism reads the title exactly this way (CCC 608). At the Cross John applies to him the Passover rule that no bone be broken (19:36).
See also
The Lamb is the central figure of Revelation, “a Lamb that seemed to have been slain” (Revelation 5:6). See The Lamb's Victory, chapter 5.
John 1:51TypeFulfilled
“Amen, amen, I say to you, you will see the sky opened and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man.”
Reading
Jesus answers Nathanael with Jacob's dream at Bethel, where angels went up and down a stairway between earth and heaven (Genesis 28:12). What was a stairway in the dream is now a person: the Son of Man himself is the meeting place of heaven and earth, the true house of God and gate of heaven that Jacob named at Bethel.
See also
Prophecy in Genesis, on Jacob's ladder.
John 2:1–11ImageFulfilled
“Woman, how does your concern affect me? My hour has not yet come.”
Reading
At the wedding at Cana, Mary notices the need: “They have no wine” (2:3). Jesus' answer sounds like a refusal, but it opens the theme of his “hour,” which runs through the Gospel until “The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified” (12:23), the hour of the Cross. St. Augustine reads it so: the hour is that of the Passion, when Jesus will acknowledge his mother as she stands by the Cross (Tractates on John 8.9). Mary's last words in this Gospel are for every disciple: “Do whatever he tells you” (2:5).
Why fulfilled
The Woman of Cana is the Woman at the Cross (19:26), when the hour has come; the Catechism calls her there the Woman, the new Eve (CCC 2618). Water for Jewish purification becomes the best wine, a sign of the abundance the prophets promised for the messianic age (Isaiah 25:6; Amos 9:13). At this first of his signs Jesus “so revealed his glory,” and his disciples began to believe in him (2:11).
John 2:19–22ProphecyFulfilled
“Destroy this temple and in three days I will raise it up.” … But he was speaking about the temple of his body.
Reading
After cleansing the Temple, Jesus is asked for a sign and gives only one, his death and resurrection, the same sign of Jonah he gives in Matthew 12:40. His hearers take him to mean Herod's Temple, “under construction for forty-six years” (2:20). John explains that he meant his body, and that the disciples understood only “when he was raised from the dead” (2:22). Jesus identifies himself with the Temple as God's dwelling among us (CCC 586).
Note
John himself shows here how a prophecy can be understood only after its fulfillment. Fr. John's own reflection on this passage, offered as a reflection and not as Church teaching, is The Temple of His Body.
See also
Matthew 24:1–2; Revelation 21:22.
John 3:14–15TypeFulfilled
“And just as Moses lifted up the serpent in the desert, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, so that everyone who believes in him may have eternal life.”
Reading
In the desert, the people bitten by serpents looked at the bronze serpent Moses set on a pole and lived (Numbers 21:8–9). Jesus names himself as what it foreshadowed. The Catechism lists the bronze serpent among the Old Testament images that pointed to salvation through the incarnate Word (CCC 2130).
Note
“Lifted up” in John means both the Cross and the exaltation to glory. See 8:28 and 12:32–33.
John 4:21–24ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“But the hour is coming, and is now here, when true worshipers will worship the Father in Spirit and truth…”
Reading
At Jacob's well the Samaritan woman raises the old dispute between Mount Gerizim and Jerusalem (4:20). Jesus answers, “Believe me, woman, the hour is coming when you will worship the Father neither on this mountain nor in Jerusalem” (4:21). He does not dismiss Israel's worship, “because salvation is from the Jews” (4:22), but he announces worship no longer bound to one place. The Catechism cites this prophecy among Jesus' words about the Temple, whose place he himself takes as God's definitive dwelling among us (CCC 586).
Why two labels
The hour is “now here” in Jesus himself, and it came fully when the temple of his body was raised and the Spirit was given. Since then the Father is worshiped in every place, as the Church has long read Malachi's promise of a pure offering among the nations (Malachi 1:11). The worship of the heavenly city, which needs no temple because its temple is the Lord God and the Lamb, is still ahead (Revelation 21:22).
John 5:25–29ProphecyUnfoldingFuture
“Amen, amen, I say to you, the hour is coming and is now here when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God, and those who hear will live.”
Reading
Jesus speaks of two resurrections. The first is “now here”: whoever hears his word and believes passes from death to life already (5:24). The second is only “coming”: all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and come out, to the resurrection of life or of condemnation (5:28–29). The Catechism takes up these words for the resurrection of all the dead before the Last Judgment (CCC 1038), which will take place “at the last day” (CCC 1001).
Why two labels
The life of faith is given now, in the age of the Church. The bodily resurrection waits for Christ's return.
John 6:39–54PromiseUnfoldingFuture
“Whoever eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him on the last day.”
Reading
In the discourse on the bread of life a refrain returns four times: “I shall raise him [on] the last day” (6:40; compare 6:39, 44, 54). As in chapter 5, Jesus joins two times. Whoever believes and eats “has eternal life” now, and will be raised at the end. And the gift is himself: “the bread that I will give is my flesh for the life of the world” (6:51). The Catechism cites these verses for the resurrection “at the last day” (CCC 1001) and teaches that Christ will raise up on the last day those who have believed in him and have eaten his body and drunk his blood (CCC 994).
Why two labels
Each Communion is a pledge already given. The Catechism calls the Eucharist the surest pledge of the hope of a new heaven and a new earth and, with St. Ignatius of Antioch, the medicine of immortality (CCC 1405; Ignatius, Letter to the Ephesians 20). The bodily resurrection waits for the last day.
John 11:47–52ProphecyFulfilled
“He did not say this on his own, but since he was high priest for that year, he prophesied that Jesus was going to die for the nation.”
Reading
After the raising of Lazarus, the council fears that if Jesus goes on, the Romans will come and take everything from them (11:48). Caiaphas cuts the debate short: “it is better for you that one man should die instead of the people” (11:50). He means a political calculation. John sees in it, beyond the speaker's intention, a true prophecy, given because of his office. The Catechism recounts Caiaphas' words among the events that led to Jesus' condemnation (CCC 596).
Why fulfilled
Jesus died not only for the nation, John adds, “but also to gather into one the dispersed children of God” (11:52). The Catechism takes up this verse for the Spirit poured out through Christ, gathering into one the children of God scattered abroad (CCC 706).
Note
A lesson for every reader of prophecy: God can speak even through one who does not understand what he is saying.
John 12:23–26ImageFulfilledUnfolding
“Amen, amen, I say to you, unless a grain of wheat falls to the ground and dies, it remains just a grain of wheat; but if it dies, it produces much fruit.”
Reading
When some Greeks come asking to see Jesus (12:20–21), he answers, “The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified” (12:23), and explains the hour with a seed. His death will not be loss but harvest, and the Gentiles who come asking for him are the first of the fruit. Then the image widens to his followers: “Whoever serves me must follow me, and where I am, there also will my servant be” (12:26).
Why two labels
Fulfilled in his death and resurrection. Unfolding in the martyrs: St. Ignatius of Antioch, on his way to die at Rome, wrote, “I am the wheat of God, and let me be ground by the teeth of the wild beasts, that I may be found the pure bread of Christ” (Letter to the Romans 4), and Tertullian told the persecutors that “the blood of Christians is seed” (Apology 50).
John 12:31–33ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“Now is the time of judgment on this world; now the ruler of this world will be driven out. And when I am lifted up from the earth, I will draw everyone to myself.”
Reading
On the eve of the Passion, Jesus says that his death is the judgment of the world and the defeat of its ruler. The Catechism teaches that Christ's freely accepted death won the victory over evil once for all, and the “prince of this world” was cast out (CCC 2853). The lifting up begins his exaltation, from which he draws all to himself (CCC 662).
Why two labels
The victory was won on the Cross. The drawing of “everyone” goes on wherever Christ crucified is preached.
See also
The dragon thrown down from heaven (Revelation 12:9–10); The Woman Clothed with the Sun.
John 14:2–3PromiseFuture
“And if I go and prepare a place for you, I will come back again and take you to myself, so that where I am you also may be.”
Reading
At the Last Supper Jesus promises a place in his Father's house, which has “many dwelling places” (14:2). He goes ahead of his members so that they may follow him there (CCC 661), and heaven itself is being with Christ (CCC 1025). His coming back again is the return the Church professes in the Creed.
Note
Some Christians read this verse as a secret coming of Christ to take believers away before a time of tribulation (the “rapture”). The Catholic reading sees one visible return of Christ in glory; the promise is also tasted already in the death of each believer who goes to be with him. See The Lamb's Victory: the rapture.
John 16:12–15PromiseFulfilledUnfolding
“But when he comes, the Spirit of truth, he will guide you to all truth. He will not speak on his own, but he will speak what he hears, and will declare to you the things that are coming.”
Reading
At the Last Supper Jesus tells the disciples that he has more to say than they can bear now (16:12). The Spirit will complete his teaching, not with a message of his own, but by taking what is Christ's and declaring it (16:14). The Catechism gathers these promises: the Spirit will teach the disciples, remind them of all that Christ said, and lead them into all truth (CCC 729).
Why two labels
Fulfilled when the Spirit was given and the apostles preached and wrote what they had received. “The things that are coming” reach further, into the Church's whole road; John's own Revelation came to him in the Spirit on the Lord's day (Revelation 1:10). The Church keeps growing in understanding what was handed on, but no new public revelation is to be expected before Christ's glorious return (CCC 66).
John 16:20–22ImageFulfilledFuture
“When a woman is in labor, she is in anguish because her hour has arrived; but when she has given birth to a child, she no longer remembers the pain because of her joy that a child has been born into the world.”
Reading
“Amen, amen, I say to you, you will weep and mourn, while the world rejoices; you will grieve, but your grief will become joy” (16:20). Jesus takes up an image the prophets used for Zion in labor with a new people (Isaiah 26:17; 66:7–10), and the woman's “hour” echoes his own. Within days the grief of Good Friday becomes the joy of Easter: “But I will see you again, and your hearts will rejoice, and no one will take your joy away from you” (16:22).
Why two labels
Fulfilled at the Resurrection. Yet the Church still lives between the labor and the joy. Revelation shows the same image on the scale of history, the woman crying out in labor as she gives birth to the Messiah (Revelation 12:2), and Paul hears all creation groaning in labor pains until the full redemption (Romans 8:22). The joy no one can take away is the joy of the end.
See also
The Woman Clothed with the Sun.
John 19:26–27ImageUnfolding
“Woman, behold, your son.” Then he said to the disciple, “Behold, your mother.”
Reading
These words are not a prediction. They are placed here because the Church reads them as reaching beyond that afternoon. From the Cross Jesus gives his mother to the disciple he loved, and the Catechism sees in this the motherhood of Mary in the Church (CCC 964). He calls her “Woman,” as at Cana (2:4); she is “the Woman, the new Eve,” the true mother of all the living (CCC 2618).
Why unfolding
Every disciple is invited to take her “into his home” (19:27), in the time between the Cross and the return.
See also
Genesis 3:15; Revelation 12:1, 17. See The Woman Clothed with the Sun.
John 19:36–37ProphecyFulfilledFuture
“They will look upon him whom they have pierced.”
Reading
John, who stood at the Cross, gives two fulfillments. No bone of Jesus is broken, as with the Passover lamb (Exodus 12:46; Psalm 34:21). And the soldier's lance fulfills Zechariah 12:10, where God promises a spirit of mourning for the one they have pierced. The blood and water from his side the Church sees as figures of Baptism and the Eucharist (CCC 1225).
Why two labels
Fulfilled at Calvary. Revelation takes up the same words for his return: “every eye will see him, even those who pierced him” (Revelation 1:7).
John 21:18–23ProphecyFulfilled
“…when you grow old, you will stretch out your hands, and someone else will dress you and lead you where you do not want to go.”
Reading
The risen Lord tells Peter how he will die, and John adds that he said this “signifying by what kind of death he would glorify God” (21:19). By the time the Gospel was written Peter had died a martyr. Eusebius, citing Origen, records that he was crucified at Rome, head downward at his own request (Church History 3.1).
Note
Peter then asks about the beloved disciple, and a rumor spreads that this disciple would not die. John corrects it himself: “Jesus had not told him that he would not die, just ‘What if I want him to remain until I come?’” (21:23). Saint Augustine comments on this correction (Tractates on John 124). A lesson for every reader of prophecy: a saying of the Lord is not a timetable.
