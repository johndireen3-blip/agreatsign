Prophecy in Obadiah

The shortest book of the Old Testament, twenty-one verses against one nation. Obadiah's subject is narrow: Edom, which stood by and gloated when Jerusalem fell. But his last words open out to every nation and to the end of all things: “the kingship shall be the LORD's.”

The book at a glance

The book is headed simply “The vision of Obadiah” (verse 1), and nothing else is known of the prophet. Most scholars, with the NABRE introduction, date it some time after the Babylonians destroyed Jerusalem in 587 BC, when the Edomites, descendants of Jacob's brother Esau (Genesis 36:1), took advantage of Judah's ruin (verse 11; Psalm 137:7). Its opening verses closely resemble part of Jeremiah's oracle against Edom (Jeremiah 49:9, 14–16), a sign that the prophets drew on a shared store of words. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture, one of the twelve “minor” prophets, minor only in length.

Its prophecy is honest about its own scope. Most of it is a single oracle against a nation, a kind the larger prophets also contain (Isaiah 34; Ezekiel 35). It does not speak of a Messiah, and this page does not read one into it. What lifts it beyond its moment is the turn at verse 15, from Edom to the day of the LORD for all the nations, and its closing promise that the kingdom belongs to God.

The passages

Obadiah 1–14ProphecyFulfilled
“Because of violence to your brother Jacob, disgrace will cover you…”
Reading
Edom lived in the rock-cut heights south of the Dead Sea and thought itself out of reach. “The pride of your heart has deceived you” (verse 3), God says: “Though you soar like the eagle,” he will bring it down (verse 4). Its sin is named precisely. Edom was a brother, and “On the day you stood by” (verse 11), it did nothing; then it gloated, looted and handed over those who fled. The refrain is a plea as much as a charge: “Do not gloat over the day of your brother” (verse 12).
Fulfilled
Edom did not outlast its pride. Within a few centuries Arab peoples, later the Nabataeans, held its old homeland, and the Edomites who had pressed into southern Judah became the Idumeans of later Jewish history. As a nation with its own land and name, Edom was gone, as Obadiah foretold.
For now
The heart of the charge is not cruelty from strangers but indifference from a brother. Scripture's first question about a brother is Cain's, “Am I my brother's keeper?” (Genesis 4:9), and Obadiah answers it.
See also
Jeremiah 49:7–22; Ezekiel 35; Psalm 137:7; Amos 1:11.
Obadiah 15–16ProphecyUnfoldingFuture
“Near is the day of the LORD against all the nations! As you have done, so will it be done to you, your conduct will come back upon your own head…”
Reading
Here the horizon widens. Edom's judgment becomes one instance of a day that is coming for every nation. The cup image follows: “As you drank upon my holy mountain, so will all the nations drink continually” (verse 16). The rule of that day is not arbitrary. What is done returns on the one who does it.
Why two labels
The principle is at work in history now, as the fall of proud powers shows, and Jesus makes it a rule for his disciples: the measure we use will be measured back to us (Matthew 7:1–2). But “the day of the LORD” in its full sense is the last judgment, still ahead, when all the nations are gathered before the Son of Man (Matthew 25:31–32). This site sets no date for it.
Hope, not fear
The same Lord who judges by our conduct teaches us to pray, “forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors” (Matthew 6:12). The measure can be mercy, if we choose it.
See also
Joel 4:14; Amos 5:18; Zephaniah 1:14; Revelation 18:6.
Obadiah 17–21PromiseUnfoldingFuture
“And deliverers will ascend Mount Zion to rule Mount Esau, and the kingship shall be the LORD's.”
Reading
Against Edom's mountain stands God's. “But on Mount Zion there will be some who escape; the mountain will be holy” (verse 17), and the scattered exiles will come back to their land. The book's last words lift the whole oracle above national rivalry. The point is not finally that Jacob will rule Esau, but that God will rule all.
Unfolding
Jesus began his preaching with the same news: “The kingdom of God is at hand” (Mark 1:15). The Catechism teaches that the kingdom is already present in mystery in the Church, its seed and beginning on earth (CCC 669, 763).
Future
Obadiah's last line is heard again at the seventh trumpet, when loud voices in heaven cry, “The kingdom of the world now belongs to our Lord and to his Anointed, and he will reign forever and ever” (Revelation 11:15).
