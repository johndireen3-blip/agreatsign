Prophecy in 2 Peter

The letter answers people who mocked the promise of Christ's coming because it had not happened yet. Its answer is the Church's charter for waiting: with the Lord a thousand years are like one day, and what looks like delay is patience, “not wishing that any should perish but that all should come to repentance.”

The book at a glance

The letter presents itself as the testament of “Symeon Peter, a slave and apostle of Jesus Christ” (1:1), written as he knows his death is near (1:14). Its authorship is more disputed than that of any other New Testament book. Most modern scholars, including many Catholic scholars, think it was written late in the first century or early in the second by a disciple in Peter's name, drawing on the Letter of Jude; already in the early centuries some doubted it, as St. Jerome reports (On Illustrious Men, 1). Others still defend a link to Peter himself. The Church receives it as inspired and canonical Scripture either way.

Its prophecy is mostly prophecy in the strict sense, about the last things: false teachers who will come, scoffers in the last days, and the day of the Lord, followed by new heavens and a new earth. It also says something about prophecy itself, that it comes from God and is not a matter of anyone's private reading.

The passages

2 Peter 1:16–19ProphecyFulfilledFuture
“You will do well to be attentive to it, as to a lamp shining in a dark place, until day dawns and the morning star rises in your hearts.”
Reading
“We did not follow cleverly devised myths when we made known to you the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, but we had been eyewitnesses of his majesty” (1:16). The witness is the Transfiguration: the voice from the majestic glory, “This is my Son, my beloved, with whom I am well pleased,” heard “on the holy mountain” (1:17–18; Matthew 17:1–8). The glory seen there is a preview of the glory of his coming. So the letter adds, “we possess the prophetic message that is altogether reliable” (1:19).
Why two labels
The glory has been shown once, on the mountain. The prophetic word is a lamp for the night that remains, until the day dawns. The morning star is Christ himself: “I am the root and offspring of David, the bright morning star” (Revelation 22:16; compare 2:28).
For now
Prophecy is not given to satisfy curiosity but to light the way in a dark place. Its proper end is a dawn “in your hearts.”
2 Peter 1:20–21ProphecyUnfolding
“Know this first of all, that there is no prophecy of scripture that is a matter of personal interpretation, for no prophecy ever came through human will…”
Reading
Prophecy came as “human beings moved by the holy Spirit spoke under the influence of God” (1:21). The NABRE note observes that these verses are often cited on the inspiration of Scripture and against private interpretation, and that in context they are aimed at the false teachers of chapter 2: the prophetic word means what the author and the Spirit intended, “not the personal interpretation of false teachers.”
Note
The Catechism teaches the same principle in its own words: Scripture must be read “within ‘the living Tradition of the whole Church’” (CCC 113), and all that concerns its interpretation is subject finally to the judgment of the Church (CCC 119). It also teaches that God is the author of Scripture, written under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit (CCC 105). The Catechism does not cite this verse in those paragraphs; the link is one of teaching, not of footnote. This is the rule behind every page on this site.
Why unfolding
The Church receives and interprets the prophetic word in every generation until the morning star rises.
2 Peter 2:1–3ProphecyUnfolding
“There were also false prophets among the people, just as there will be false teachers among you, who will introduce destructive heresies…”
Reading
Israel had false prophets alongside true ones (Jeremiah 23:16; Deuteronomy 13:2–6), and the Church will have false teachers. Their mark is that they “even deny the Master who ransomed them” (2:1). The chapter recalls God's past judgments, and also his rescues: he “preserved Noah, a herald of righteousness, together with seven others” (2:5) and rescued Lot (2:7).
Why unfolding
The warning was already being fulfilled when the letter was written, and it has been in every age since. Jesus foretold the same (Matthew 24:11).
For now
The chapter is severe, but its conclusion is that “the Lord knows how to rescue the devout from trial” (2:9).
2 Peter 3:3–7ProphecyUnfolding
“Where is the promise of his coming? From the time when our ancestors fell asleep, everything has remained as it was from the beginning of creation.”
Reading
“In the last days scoffers will come [to] scoff, living according to their own desires” (3:3). Their argument is that nothing ever changes. The letter answers that it already has: the world “was destroyed, deluged with water” in Noah's day (3:6), and the same word of God holds the present world for judgment (3:7). Jude gives the same warning, as “the words spoken beforehand by the apostles” (Jude 17–18).
Why unfolding
The “last days” began with Christ (Hebrews 1:2; CCC 670), and the scoffing question has been asked ever since, sometimes by the world and sometimes in the heart of the believer.
2 Peter 3:8–9PromiseUnfolding
“But do not ignore this one fact, beloved, that with the Lord one day is like a thousand years and a thousand years like one day.”
Reading
The verse echoes Psalm 90:4. God does not live inside our calendar. Then the heart of the answer: “The Lord does not delay his promise, as some regard ‘delay,’ but he is patient with you, not wishing that any should perish but that all should come to repentance” (3:9). The NABRE note puts it plainly: the delay is not a failure to keep his word but a sign of his patience, God giving time for repentance. The Catechism quotes this verse when it teaches that God predestines no one to go to hell (CCC 1037), and the letter says it again: “consider the patience of our Lord as salvation” (3:15).
Note
This is the key text against date-setting. It does not supply a formula; it removes the ground for one. Some have tried to turn “a thousand years like one day” into a calendar of six thousand years of history, or similar schemes. The verse says the opposite: God's time cannot be measured out by ours. “But of that day and hour no one knows” (Matthew 24:36; CCC 673).
For now
Every day the Lord has not yet come is a day of mercy given to someone.
2 Peter 3:10–13ApocalypseFuture
“But according to his promise we await new heavens and a new earth in which righteousness dwells.”
Reading
“But the day of the Lord will come like a thief” (3:10), as Jesus and Paul both said (Matthew 24:43; 1 Thessalonians 5:2). The language of heavens passing away with a roar and elements dissolved by fire is the vivid speech of apocalypse; the NABRE note records that the ancient manuscripts differ on the end of verse 10. The point is that everything “done on it will be found out” (3:10): nothing is hidden from God. And the end is not destruction but promise: new heavens and a new earth (Isaiah 65:17; Revelation 21:1).
Note
The Catechism cites these verses for the renewal of the universe at the end of time, and names the hope from verse 13: “new heavens and a new earth” (CCC 1042–1043). Believers are not only waiting but “waiting for and hastening the coming of the day of God” (3:12), by holiness and prayer (CCC 671).
For now
“Therefore, beloved, since you await these things, be eager to be found without spot or blemish before him, at peace” (3:14).
