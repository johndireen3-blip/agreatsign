Prophecy in Malachi

The last book of the Old Testament ends with a promise and a name: “Now I am sending to you Elijah the prophet, before the day of the LORD comes.” Malachi foresees a pure offering made among all the nations, a messenger who prepares the way, and the sun of justice rising with healing in its wings. Turn the page, and the Gospel opens with John the Baptist.

The book at a glance

“Malachi” means “my messenger” (compare 3:1), and many scholars take it as a title rather than a personal name; the prophet himself is otherwise unknown. He spoke in Jerusalem in the fifth century before Christ, after the Temple had been rebuilt but before or around the reforms of Ezra and Nehemiah. Worship had grown careless: priests offered blind and lame animals, marriages were broken, tithes withheld. The book is a series of disputes in which God makes a statement, the people object, and God answers. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture, and in the Christian ordering of the Old Testament it stands last, a bridge to the Gospel.

Its prophecy is mostly promise. God will be honored by a pure offering among the nations; he will send his messenger and come suddenly to his Temple; he will refine his people; and before his great day he will send Elijah. The New Testament reads the messenger and Elijah as John the Baptist, and the coming Lord as Jesus.

A note on numbering. In the NABRE, as in the Hebrew Bible, Malachi has three chapters, and the book ends at 3:24. Many other Bibles divide it into four chapters, so NABRE 3:19–24 is 4:1–6 elsewhere. This page uses the NABRE numbers: “Malachi 3:23” here is “Malachi 4:5” in many English Bibles.

The passages

Malachi 1:2–5PromiseFulfilled
“I love you, says the LORD; but you say, ‘How do you love us?’”
Reading
The first word of the last prophet is love. The people doubt it, and God points them to their own history: “I loved Jacob, but rejected Esau” (1:3). The NABRE note explains that the thought passes from the persons Jacob and Esau to their descendants, Israel and Edom. The point is election: God chose this people freely and kept them, while Edom's land lay waste. The first dispute ends with a glimpse beyond Israel: “Great is the LORD, even beyond the territory of Israel” (1:5), preparing for the pure offering among the nations (1:11).
Fulfilled
St. Paul quotes the verse as “I loved Jacob but hated Esau” (Romans 9:13). The NABRE note there explains that in Semitic usage “hate” means “to love less,” and that the verse speaks of God's sovereign freedom to choose, not of hostility; Paul uses it to show that God's free call now reaches the Gentiles. The love that Israel doubted is shown beyond doubt when God gives his only Son (John 3:16; 1 John 4:9–10).
Note
This is a declaration more than a prediction. It belongs on this page because the whole of Malachi's prophecy rests on it: every rebuke that follows is the complaint of a lover.
Malachi 1:11ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“From the rising of the sun to its setting, my name is great among the nations; Incense offerings are made to my name everywhere, and a pure offering…”
Reading
God rebukes the priests of Jerusalem for offering him polluted sacrifices and wishes someone would shut the Temple doors (1:10). Then, startlingly, he looks beyond Israel: everywhere, among the nations, his name is great and a pure offering is made. Interpreters differ on what the prophet first meant; the NABRE note says that a Christian reading sees here sacrifice in the Messianic Age.
Fulfilled
From the beginning the Church has heard this verse as a prophecy of the Eucharist, the one sacrifice of Christ offered in every place. The Didache applies it to the Sunday Eucharist (Didache 14), as do St. Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho 41) and St. Irenaeus (Against Heresies 4.17.5). The NABRE note records that the Council of Trent endorsed this interpretation. The Catechism, listing the names of the Eucharist, includes “pure and holy sacrifice,” citing this verse (CCC 1330). The third Eucharistic Prayer echoes it: “from the rising of the sun to its setting a pure sacrifice may be offered to your name.”
Why unfolding
Each day, as the sun moves round the earth, Mass is offered somewhere. The prophecy is being fulfilled hour by hour.
Malachi 2:4–7TypeFulfilledUnfolding
“For a priest's lips preserve knowledge, and instruction is to be sought from his mouth, because he is the messenger of the LORD of hosts.”
Reading
Against careless priests (1:6–2:9), God recalls his covenant with Levi: “My covenant with him was the life and peace which I gave him” (2:5). The faithful priest of old is drawn in a few lines: “Reliable instruction was in his mouth, no perversity was found upon his lips; He walked with me in integrity and uprightness, and turned many away from evil” (2:6). Then comes a striking title. The priest is “the messenger of the LORD of hosts” (2:7), the same Hebrew word, mal'akh, that gives the book its name and returns in 3:1.
Fulfilled
The Catechism teaches that within Israel God chose the tribe of Levi “and set it apart for liturgical service” (CCC 1539), and that “Everything that the priesthood of the Old Covenant prefigured finds its fulfillment in Christ Jesus, the ‘one mediator between God and men’” (CCC 1544). In Christ the priest and the messenger are one: he does not only carry God's word, he is the Word (Hebrews 1:1–2; 3:1).
Why unfolding
The Church still asks of her priests what Malachi asked of Levi: sound teaching and an upright life, so that people may seek instruction from their lips and be turned away from evil.
See also
Deuteronomy 33:8–11; Numbers 25:12–13; Leviticus 10:10–11.
Malachi 2:10–16ImageFulfilledUnfolding
“Have we not all one father? Has not one God created us? Why, then, do we break faith with each other, profaning the covenant of our ancestors?”
Reading
Malachi names two kinds of faithlessness: marrying “a daughter of a foreign god” (2:11), a woman who does not worship the God of Israel, and divorcing the wife one already has. The NABRE note says that the foreign marriages are portrayed as a covenantal violation, made worse when they came with the divorce of Israelite wives. God himself is witness “between you and the wife of your youth With whom you have broken faith, though she is your companion, your covenanted wife” (2:14); the note explains that the Hebrew word for companion signifies an equal, a partner. Tears on the altar are no substitute for faithfulness at home (2:13). Then the verdict: “For I hate divorce, says the LORD, the God of Israel” (2:16).
Why an image
Marriage here is a covenant, made before God and kept or broken before him, and so it mirrors his own covenant with his people. Breaking faith with one's wife and breaking faith with God are the same sin in two places.
Fulfilled
Jesus goes back to the beginning: “Therefore, what God has joined together, no human being must separate” (Matthew 19:6), for “from the beginning it was not so” (Matthew 19:8). The Catechism says that “The Lord Jesus insisted on the original intention of the Creator,” and teaches that “a ratified and consummated marriage cannot be dissolved by any human power or for any reason other than death” (CCC 2382). St. Paul names the deepest meaning of the covenant of marriage: it speaks of Christ and the Church (Ephesians 5:31–32).
Why unfolding
Every faithful marriage is a living sign of God's faithfulness. Malachi's warning is given twice, and it is meant for every generation: “You should be on guard, then, for your life, and do not break faith with the wife of your youth” (2:15).
See also
Genesis 2:24; Mark 10:2–12; Hosea 2:21–22.
Malachi 3:1ProphecyFulfilled
“Now I am sending my messenger— he will prepare the way before me; And the lord whom you seek will come suddenly to his temple…”
Reading
The people complain that God does not judge (2:17). He answers that he is coming himself, and that a messenger will go first to prepare the way. The one who comes is called both “the lord whom you seek” and “the messenger of the covenant whom you desire.”
Fulfilled
Mark opens his Gospel with these words joined to Isaiah 40:3: “Behold, I am sending my messenger ahead of you; he will prepare your way” (Mark 1:2). Jesus himself applies the verse to John the Baptist (Matthew 11:10; Luke 7:27). The Catechism calls John “the Lord's immediate precursor or forerunner” (CCC 523). In Malachi the way is prepared before God; in the Gospels it is prepared before Jesus. The Lord who comes suddenly to his Temple is the child presented there by Mary and Joseph (Luke 2:22–38), a scene the Church celebrates on the feast of the Presentation with this very reading.
See also
Exodus 23:20; Prophecy in Mark.
Malachi 3:2–3ProphecyUnfoldingFuture
“But who can endure the day of his coming? Who can stand firm when he appears? For he will be like a refiner's fire, like fullers' lye.”
Reading
The people want God to come, but his coming is not comfortable. He comes to purify, like fire that melts silver and lye that whitens cloth. “He will sit refining and purifying silver, and he will purify the Levites” (3:3), so that once again the offering is made rightly. The fire is not to destroy but to make pure.
Why two labels
John the Baptist preached a coming one who would baptize “with the holy Spirit and fire” (Matthew 3:11). That refining goes on now in the life of grace, and in the purification the Church calls purgatory, for which tradition also speaks of a cleansing fire, citing 1 Corinthians 3:15 (CCC 1031). It will be complete when the Lord appears in glory; Revelation asks the same question as Malachi, “who can withstand it?” (Revelation 6:17).
For now
The question “who can endure?” has an answer in the Gospel: those whom he himself has made pure.
Malachi 3:16–18PromiseFuture
“Then those who fear the LORD spoke with one another, and the LORD listened attentively; A record book was written before him of those who fear the LORD and esteem his name.”
Reading
Some had said, “It is useless to serve God” (3:14), since the arrogant seemed to prosper. The faithful talk it over among themselves, and God is listening. Their names are written down. “They shall be mine, says the LORD of hosts, my own special possession, on the day when I take action” (3:17), words that recall God's choice of Israel at Sinai (Exodus 19:5). On that day, “you will again distinguish between the just and the wicked” (3:18). The NABRE note links the record book to the book Moses speaks of in Exodus 32:32. In many Bibles these verses keep the same numbers; only 3:19–24 are renumbered.
Future
Revelation sees the books opened at the last judgment: “Then another scroll was opened, the book of life. The dead were judged according to their deeds, by what was written in the scrolls” (Revelation 20:12). Jesus tells his disciples to “rejoice because your names are written in heaven” (Luke 10:20). St. Peter calls the Church “a people of his own” (1 Peter 2:9), the special possession Malachi foresaw.
For now
The faithful who wonder whether serving God is worth it are heard, even as they speak of it with one another. God's answer to doubt is not an argument but a promise: he remembers.

Psalm 69:29; Daniel 12:1; Philippians 4:3; Are we in the end times?
Malachi 3:19–20ImageFulfilledFuture
“But for you who fear my name, the sun of justice will arise with healing in its wings…” (3:20)
Reading
“The day is coming, blazing like an oven” (3:19): for the arrogant it will burn like stubble, but for those who fear God the same day is sunrise. The NABRE note explains the background: the winged sun disk was a common ancient symbol of a god's appearing. Malachi takes it for the LORD's own justice, dawning to heal. The righteous “go out leaping like calves from the stall” (3:20). In many Bibles these verses are 4:1–2.
Fulfilled
The Church has always applied the title to Christ. Zechariah, father of John the Baptist, sings of “the daybreak from on high” that will “shine on those who sit in darkness” (Luke 1:78–79), and the Advent antiphon of December 21 greets Christ as the Dawn and Sun of Justice. The early Christian custom of praying toward the east, and keeping Christmas near the winter solstice, drew on this image of Christ as the rising sun.
Future
In the new Jerusalem there is no need of sun, “for the glory of God gave it light, and its lamp was the Lamb” (Revelation 21:23).
Malachi 3:23–24ProphecyFulfilledMystery
“Now I am sending to you Elijah the prophet, Before the day of the LORD comes, the great and terrible day; He will turn the heart of fathers to their sons, and the heart of sons to their fathers…”
Reading
The Old Testament closes on this promise. Elijah had been taken up to heaven in a whirlwind (2 Kings 2:11), and here his return is foretold, before the day of the LORD. His work is reconciliation, turning hearts within families and within the people, so that the day may come as blessing rather than curse. In many Bibles these verses are 4:5–6.
Fulfilled
The angel tells Zechariah that his son will go before the Lord “in the spirit and power of Elijah to turn the hearts of fathers toward children” (Luke 1:17). Jesus says of John the Baptist, “And if you are willing to accept it, he is Elijah, the one who is to come” (Matthew 11:14). After the Transfiguration, when the disciples ask why Elijah must come first, Jesus answers, “Elijah will indeed come and restore all things; but I tell you that Elijah has already come,” and “the disciples understood that he was speaking to them of John the Baptist” (Matthew 17:11–13). The Catechism likewise speaks of John going before the Lord in the spirit and power of Elijah (CCC 523).
Why mystery
Jesus' words hold two things together: Elijah “will indeed come” and “has already come.” The NABRE note observes that a Jewish tradition read Malachi literally, while the Gospels saw Elijah in John. Many of the Fathers, among them St. Augustine (City of God 20.29), also expected Elijah himself to return before the end to turn the hearts of Israel to Christ, and some identified him, with Enoch, as one of the two witnesses of Revelation 11, who are called “the two olive trees and the two lampstands” (compare Zechariah 4) and work signs like those of Moses and Elijah (Revelation 11:5–6). The Church has not defined this. What is certain is that John fulfilled the promise in the way Jesus himself declared, and that the work of Elijah, turning hearts back before the Lord comes, belongs to every age.

Mark 9:11–13; Sirach 48:10; Zechariah 4; Revelation 11 at The Lamb's Victory.

From Malachi to Matthew

Between the last prophet and the first Gospel lie about four centuries of silence in the prophetic word. The Gospel breaks that silence exactly where Malachi left off.
