Prophecy in Nahum

Jonah saw Nineveh spared; Nahum sees it fall. A short, fierce book about the end of an empire that had terrorized the world, and in the middle of it a messenger running over the mountains with news of peace, the same messenger Isaiah and St. Paul would sing of.

The book at a glance

The book is an “Oracle concerning Nineveh. The book of the vision of Nahum of Elkosh” (1:1). Nothing more is known of Nahum or his town. He wrote after the Assyrians sacked Thebes in Egypt in 663 BC, which he recalls (3:8), and, as the NABRE introduction holds, shortly before Nineveh itself fell in 612 BC. For nearly three centuries Assyria had dominated the Near East with famous cruelty; it had destroyed Samaria in 722/721 BC and nearly taken Jerusalem twenty years later. Many scholars think the acrostic hymn of chapter 1 was set before the oracles later; the Church receives the whole as inspired Scripture.

Its prophecy is mostly a prophecy of judgment on one nation, and it was fulfilled within a generation. The book is not simply a cry for revenge. As the NABRE introduction puts it, Nahum affirms God's moral rule over the world, and Nineveh's doom shows that God stands against oppression and the abuse of power. One verse reaches further: the herald of good news (2:1), whom the New Testament hears again in the preachers of the Gospel.

The passages

Nahum 1:2–8ProphecyUnfolding
“The LORD is slow to anger, yet great in power; the LORD will not leave the guilty unpunished.”
Reading
The book opens with a hymn to God as judge. Its first lines are severe: “A jealous and avenging God is the LORD” (1:2). But verse 3 takes up the words God spoke of himself to Moses (Exodus 34:6–7), keeping both halves together: patience and justice. Storm, drought and trembling mountains follow, and then, in the middle of the storm, comfort: “The LORD is good to those who wait for him, a refuge on the day of distress” (1:7).
Jonah and Nahum
The two books are best read together. Jonah shows God's patience: Nineveh repents and is spared. Nahum shows that patience is not indifference: when the city returns to its violence, judgment comes. Both quote the same self-description of God (Jonah 4:2), and both are true of him at once.
Why unfolding
St. Peter gives the same teaching to Christians who wondered why the Lord delayed: God is patient, “not wishing that any should perish but that all should come to repentance” (2 Peter 3:9). The patience is mercy, and it is still being offered.
Nahum 2:1ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“At this moment on the mountains the footsteps of one bearing good news, of one announcing peace!”
Reading
A runner appears on the hills above Jerusalem with news that the oppressor is fallen. Judah may keep its feasts again: “Celebrate your feasts, Judah, fulfill your vows!” The NABRE note on this verse is frank that Judah's safety did not last, for Babylon and later Rome would invade it; the prophets were sure God's people would survive, though the way they survived often differed from what they expected.
Fulfilled
Isaiah uses the same image for the return from exile: “How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of the one bringing good news, Announcing peace” (Isaiah 52:7). St. Paul applies it to the apostles sent to preach Christ: “How beautiful are the feet of those who bring [the] good news!” (Romans 10:15). The good news of Nahum was the fall of a tyrant; the good news of the Gospel is the defeat of sin and death.
Why unfolding
Paul's point is that the news must still be carried: “how can people preach unless they are sent?” (Romans 10:15). Every missionary and every preacher is one more pair of feet on the mountains.
Note
The NABRE numbers this verse 2:1; many other Bibles number it 1:15, so “Nahum 1:15” elsewhere is the same verse, and the rest of chapter 2 is one verse lower there.
Nahum 2:2–3:19ProphecyFulfilled
“Nineveh is destroyed; who can pity her? Where can I find any to console you?”
Reading
The siege is told in rapid, vivid strokes: chariots flashing in the streets, the defenders stumbling, and then “The river gates are opened, the palace is washed away” (2:7). “Nineveh is like a pool whose waters escape” (2:9). Chapter 3 opens, “Ah! The bloody city,” and names the reason: Assyria's long cruelty to the nations. The book ends with a question that is also a verdict: “For who has not suffered under your endless malice?” (3:19).
Fulfilled
In 612 BC Nineveh fell to an alliance of Babylonians and Medes, and the Assyrian empire collapsed within a few years. The city was not rebuilt; its ruins lay buried in mounds near modern Mosul until they were excavated in the nineteenth century. The Book of Tobit, set among the exiles in Nineveh, has the dying Tobit tell his son to leave the city, “for I believe God's word that Nahum spoke against Nineveh” (Tobit 14:4).
Hope, not fear
The fall of a cruel power is good news for the weak. Revelation's lament over Babylon, the great city that persecuted the saints (Revelation 17:6), carries the same message to Christians under persecution: no empire built on violence lasts (Revelation 18).
