Prophecy and apocalypse

The Bible looks to the future in two different ways, and they are read differently. The Church asks us to attend to the literary form of every text, because truth is expressed one way in history, another in prophecy, another in poetry (Dei Verbum 12).

Prophecy

God's word to his people now

The prophet speaks for God (“Thus says the LORD”) to a particular people at a particular moment: calling them back to the covenant, warning of judgment, promising mercy. Prophecy is more forth-telling than fore-telling, and its warnings are often conditional: Nineveh repented and was spared (Jonah 3). Its promises reach beyond their first hearers to Christ.

Examples: Isaiah, Jeremiah, Amos, Hosea, Micah.

Apocalypse

The veil drawn back

Apokalypsis means “unveiling” (Revelation 1:1). This form flourished in times of persecution. Through visions explained by angels, with beasts, numbers and cosmic battles, it lifts the curtain to show history from God's side: evil is real but its time is short, and God has already won. Its symbols are not a coded timetable; they are meant to give the suffering faithful courage.

Examples: Daniel 7–12, Zechariah 1–6, Isaiah 24–27, Mark 13, Revelation.

Revelation is both. It calls itself an apocalypse (1:1) and a prophecy (1:3; 22:7, 18–19): a word of God to seven real churches in Asia Minor, written in the language of visions. So each passage card on this site also notes its form, because a prophet's oracle and an apocalyptic vision are read differently.

What kind of passage it is

Each card also names the passage's form, because each kind looks forward in its own way and is read in its own way.

Prophecy

God's word spoken through a prophet, or by Christ himself, about what will come: a warning, a judgment, a hope. Example: Genesis 15:13–14; Matthew 24:1–2.

Promise

A pledge God makes and keeps across generations, often renewed and widened as it goes. Example: Genesis 3:15; 12:1–3.

Type

A real person or event that foreshadows Christ, the Church or the sacraments, recognized in the New Testament or the Fathers (CCC 128–130). Example: Melchizedek; Noah's ark.

Image

A picture planted early in Scripture that returns and is completed at the end. Example: the tree of life, Genesis 2:9 and Revelation 22:2.

Apocalypse

A vision that unveils history from heaven's side, in symbols, numbers and cosmic battle. Example: Daniel 7; Matthew 24:29–31; Revelation. More below.

One prophecy, more than one fulfillmentMany prophecies are fulfilled more than once. Isaiah 7:14 spoke first to King Ahaz in his own day and is fulfilled fully in the virgin birth of Christ (Matthew 1:23). Such passages can carry more than one label.

The seventy-three books

The study goes through the Bible in order, in two sections. The Old Testament begins with Genesis and its first promise of a Redeemer (Genesis 3:15). The New Testament begins with Matthew. Revelation 12 is already available as a preview of where the whole story leads.
