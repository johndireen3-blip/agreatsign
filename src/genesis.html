Prophecy in Genesis

The Bible's first book holds its first promise of a Redeemer. Genesis begins with a garden, a tree of life and a serpent; Revelation ends with a city, the tree of life and the serpent thrown down. Everything between is the story of God keeping the promises he made here.

The book at a glance

Genesis tells of beginnings: creation and fall (chapters 1–11), then the call of Abraham and the stories of Isaac, Jacob and Joseph (chapters 12–50). Jewish and Christian tradition associate it with Moses; the Church reads it as inspired Scripture that teaches the truth about God, creation and sin through the literary forms of its time (CCC 337, 390).

Genesis is not a book of prophets, and few of its passages are prophecy in the strict sense. It looks forward in three ways: through promises God makes (to Eve, to Abraham, to Judah), through types, people and events that foreshadow Christ (Melchizedek, Isaac, Jacob's ladder), and through images that Revelation takes up again at the end. The passages

Genesis 1:1–2:3ImageFulfilledFuture
“God blessed the seventh day and made it holy, because on it he rested from all the work he had done in creation.” (2:3)
Reading
Creation unfolds in six days of ordered work, each one seen to be good, and comes to rest in a seventh: “On the seventh day God completed the work he had been doing; he rested on the seventh day from all the work he had undertaken” (2:2). Unlike the six days before it, the seventh is not closed with “Evening came, and morning followed.” The Church reads this account first among the readings of the Easter Vigil, so that the night of the Resurrection begins with the first creation.
Fulfilled
Jesus rose on the first day of the week (Mark 16:2). The Catechism teaches that as the “first day,” Sunday recalls the first creation, and as the “eighth day” following the sabbath, it symbolizes the new creation ushered in by Christ's Resurrection (CCC 2174).
Future
The Letter to the Hebrews reads God's rest as a promise still open: “Therefore, a sabbath rest still remains for the people of God. And whoever enters into God's rest, rests from his own works as God did from his” (Hebrews 4:9–10). The story that began “In the beginning” ends where Revelation ends: “I saw a new heaven and a new earth” (Revelation 21:1).
Genesis 2:9; 3:22–24ImageFuture
In the middle of the garden stands “the tree of life.” After the fall, cherubim guard “the way to the tree of life.”
Reading
Sin closes the way to the tree of life. Revelation opens it again: “To the victor I will give the right to eat from the tree of life that is in the garden of God” (Revelation 2:7), and in the new Jerusalem the tree of life stands beside the river, its leaves “serve as medicine for the nations” (22:2).
Why future
Christ has opened the way by his Cross, which the Fathers called the true tree of life, but the garden-city itself is still to come.
Genesis 3:15PromiseFulfilledUnfoldingFuture
“I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your offspring and hers; they will strike at your head, while you strike at their heel.”
Reading
The Church calls this the Protoevangelium, the first announcement of the Messiah and Redeemer, of a battle between the serpent and the woman and of the final victory of her descendant (CCC 410–411). The Fathers saw in the woman Mary, the New Eve.
Fulfilled
On the Cross Christ crushed the serpent's head, while the serpent struck his heel.
Unfolding
The enmity continues: the dragon, “the ancient serpent,” makes war on “the rest of her offspring” (Revelation 12:9, 17).
Future
“The God of peace will quickly crush Satan under your feet” (Romans 16:20); the devil is thrown into the lake of fire (Revelation 20:10).

The Temple of His Body; John 2:4; 19:26.
Genesis 4:1–16TypeFulfilled
“Your brother's blood cries out to me from the ground!” (4:10)
Reading
Abel brings “the fatty portion of the firstlings of his flock,” and “the LORD looked with favor on Abel and his offering” (4:4). Cain is warned that “sin lies in wait at the door” (4:7), yet he kills his brother in the field, and God asks, “Where is your brother Abel?” (4:9). The first death in the Bible is the murder of a brother, and the first blood shed cries out to God. The Catechism counts Abel's offering among the first prayers of Scripture (CCC 2569).
Fulfilled
Jesus speaks of “the righteous blood of Abel” as the first of all the innocent blood shed on earth (Matthew 23:35). Hebrews says, “By faith Abel offered to God a sacrifice greater than Cain's,” and “though dead, he still speaks” (Hebrews 11:4). Then it names a blood that speaks better: we have approached “Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and the sprinkled blood that speaks more eloquently than that of Abel” (Hebrews 12:24). Abel's blood cried out for justice; Christ's blood, shed by his brothers, pleads for their forgiveness.
See also
Under the heavenly altar the souls of the martyrs cry out, “How long will it be, holy and true master, before you sit in judgment and avenge our blood on the inhabitants of the earth?” (Revelation 6:10); 1 John 3:12.
Genesis 5:21–24TypeMystery
“Enoch walked with God, and he was no longer here, for God took him.” (5:24)
Reading
In a genealogy where every other life ends with “then he died,” Enoch's does not. The NABRE note says the change implies that he did not die but, like Elijah (2 Kings 2:11–12), was taken alive to God's abode. Sirach and Wisdom remember him (Sirach 44:16; Wisdom 4:10–11), and Hebrews says, “By faith Enoch was taken up so that he should not see death” (Hebrews 11:5). The Catechism names his walking with God among the first forms of prayer (CCC 2569). He is a sign that friendship with God is stronger than death.
Why mystery
Some Fathers expected Enoch to return with Elijah before the end. St. Hippolytus speaks of “the two prophets Enoch and Elias” preaching in the last half-week, the same span in which he places the two witnesses of Revelation 11:3 (On Christ and Antichrist 43, 47). Others read the two witnesses as a figure of the Church's witness, or of Moses and Elijah. The Church has not settled it. Jude 14–15 also quotes a prophecy of Enoch from the book called 1 Enoch, which the Church does not count as Scripture; the NABRE note on 5:24 mentions it.
See also
The Lamb's Victory: Revelation chapter 11.
Genesis 6–9TypeFulfilledFuture
Noah and his household are saved through the waters in the ark; God sets his bow in the clouds as the sign of his covenant.
Fulfilled
St. Peter reads the flood as a figure of Baptism, which “saves you now” (1 Peter 3:20–21; CCC 1219). The ark has long been read as a figure of the Church.
Future
Jesus says his coming will be “as it was in the days of Noah”: people eating, drinking and marrying, unaware until the flood came (Matthew 24:37–39). The rainbow reappears around God's throne (Revelation 4:3).
Genesis 11:1–9ImageFulfilledUnfolding
“Come, let us build ourselves a city and a tower with its top in the sky, and so make a name for ourselves” (11:4)
Reading
“The whole world had the same language and the same words” (11:1). United, humanity sets out to reach heaven by its own power and make its own name. God answers, “Come, let us go down and there confuse their language, so that no one will understand the speech of another” (11:7), and the city is called Babel, “because there the LORD confused the speech of all the world” (11:9). The Catechism sees in the division of the nations a check on the pride of fallen humanity, “united only in its perverse ambition to forge its own unity as at Babel” (CCC 57). In the very next chapter God calls Abram and promises that he himself will make Abram's name great (12:2).
Fulfilled
At Pentecost the confusion is undone. The apostles “began to speak in different tongues, as the Spirit enabled them to proclaim,” and the crowd “were confused because each one heard them speaking in his own language” (Acts 2:4, 6). Unity is given as the Spirit's gift, not seized. The Church reads the story of Babel at the Vigil of Pentecost.
Unfolding
Babel's name lives on in Babylon, the proud city of Revelation that falls (Revelation 18:2), while the Spirit gathers one people “from every nation, race, people, and tongue” (Revelation 7:9).
Genesis 12:1–3PromiseFulfilledUnfolding
“All the families of the earth will find blessing in you.”
Reading
God calls Abram from Ur and promises land, descendants and a blessing for every nation. The promise is repeated to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob (22:18; 26:4; 28:14).
Fulfilled
St. Paul says Scripture “foretold the good news to Abraham” and that the promised descendant is Christ (Galatians 3:8, 16).
Unfolding
The blessing reaches the nations as the Gospel is preached to the ends of the earth (Matthew 28:19). Revelation sees its completion: a multitude “from every nation, race, people, and tongue” (Revelation 7:9).
Genesis 14:18–20TypeFulfilled
Melchizedek, king of Salem and “a priest of God Most High,” brings out bread and wine and blesses Abram.
Reading
Psalm 110:4 promises a king who is “a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.” The Letter to the Hebrews applies this to Christ (Hebrews 5–7). The Church sees in Melchizedek's offering of bread and wine a prefiguring of the Eucharist (CCC 1333, 1544), and names him in the Roman Canon.
Genesis 15:13–14ProphecyFulfilled
Abram's descendants will be aliens “in a land not their own,” enslaved and oppressed for four hundred years, and will come out with great wealth.
Reading
One of the few predictions in the strict sense in Genesis. It is fulfilled in Israel's slavery in Egypt and the Exodus (Exodus 12:40–41), the great act of deliverance that Revelation echoes in its plagues and in “the song of Moses” (Revelation 15:3).
Genesis 17PromiseFulfilled
“I will maintain my covenant between me and you and your descendants after you throughout the ages as an everlasting covenant, to be your God and the God of your descendants after you.” (17:7)
Reading
God renames Abram: “your name will be Abraham, for I am making you the father of a multitude of nations” (17:5). Circumcision on the eighth day is to be “the sign of the covenant between me and you” (17:11), and the covenant will run through Isaac, the son Sarah is to bear (17:19). St. Paul calls circumcision the seal of the righteousness Abraham already had by faith, so that he could be the father of all who believe (Romans 4:11).
Fulfilled
Jesus was circumcised on the eighth day (Luke 2:21). The Catechism teaches that this sign incorporated him into Abraham's descendants, the people of the covenant, and that it “prefigures that ‘circumcision of Christ’ which is Baptism” (CCC 527). Paul writes: “In him you were also circumcised with a circumcision not administered by hand, by stripping off the carnal body, with the circumcision of Christ. You were buried with him in baptism” (Colossians 2:11–12). The father of a multitude of nations becomes, in Christ, the father of believers from every people (Galatians 3:29).
Genesis 18–19ImageFuture
“Should not the judge of all the world do what is just?” (18:25)
Reading
At Mamre the LORD appears to Abraham, who looks up and sees “three men standing near him” (18:2). He welcomes them, and they promise that Sarah will have a son: “Is anything too marvelous for the LORD to do?” (18:14). Then Abraham pleads for Sodom, asking whether the LORD will “really sweep away the righteous with the wicked” (18:23), and bargains from fifty righteous people down to ten. The Catechism sees in his hospitality to the mysterious Guest a prelude to the annunciation of the true son of the promise, and in his plea a heart attuned to the Lord's compassion, that “dares to intercede for them with bold confidence” (CCC 2571). Judgment falls: “the LORD rained down sulfur upon Sodom and Gomorrah, fire from the LORD out of heaven” (19:24). Yet God “remembered Abraham and sent Lot away” (19:29).
Future
Jesus makes Sodom a picture of his coming: “as it was in the days of Lot: they were eating, drinking, buying, selling, planting, building,” until fire rained down, and “So it will be on the day the Son of Man is revealed” (Luke 17:28–30). He adds, “Remember the wife of Lot” (Luke 17:32), who looked back. In Revelation the bodies of the two witnesses lie in “the great city, which has the symbolic names ‘Sodom’ and ‘Egypt,’ where indeed their Lord was crucified” (Revelation 11:8): Sodom has become a name for any city that rejects God's messengers. Compare Jude 7.
For now
The judge of all the world looks for the righteous, and for the sake of a few he is ready to spare many. Abraham's bold prayer is still the Church's.
Genesis 22:1–18TypeFulfilled
“God will provide the sheep for the burnt offering, my son.” (22:8)
Reading
Abraham's beloved son carries the wood up the mountain. Isaac is spared and a ram is offered in his place. The Fathers saw in Isaac a figure of Christ carrying his Cross, and the Church sees the Father who “did not spare his own Son” (Romans 8:32; CCC 2572). Revelation's central image, the Lamb that was slain (Revelation 5:6), is the sheep God himself provided.
Genesis 28:10–17TypeFulfilled
Jacob dreams of a stairway reaching to the heavens, with God's angels going up and down on it. He calls the place “the house of God” and “the gateway to heaven.”
Reading
Jesus applies the dream to himself: “you will see the sky opened and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man” (John 1:51). Christ is the meeting place of heaven and earth, the true house of God, and the open door John sees in heaven (Revelation 4:1).
Genesis 37–50TypeFulfilled
“Even though you meant harm to me, God meant it for good, to achieve this present end, the survival of many people.” (50:20)
Reading
Joseph is the beloved son, given “a long ornamented tunic” (37:3), who dreams that “the sun and the moon and eleven stars were bowing down to me” (37:9). His brothers plot, “Come now, let us kill him,” and “We will see then what comes of his dreams” (37:20). They sell him “for twenty pieces of silver” (37:28). Through slavery and prison God raises him to rule Egypt, and when famine drives his brothers to him, he makes himself known: “I am your brother Joseph, whom you sold into Egypt,” and “It was really for the sake of saving lives that God sent me here ahead of you” (45:4–5).
Fulfilled
St. Stephen tells Joseph's story as the pattern of the rejected deliverer: “the patriarchs, jealous of Joseph, sold him into slavery in Egypt; but God was with him” (Acts 7:9), and only “the second time” did he make himself known to his brothers (Acts 7:13). The Fathers read Joseph as one of the clearest types of Christ, the beloved Son handed over by his own for silver (Matthew 26:15), humbled and then exalted, who becomes the source of life for those who rejected him. What his brothers meant for harm, God meant for good; so it is with the Cross.
See also
The sun, moon and stars of Joseph's dream, the whole family of Israel, return in the woman “clothed with the sun, with the moon under her feet, and on her head a crown of twelve stars” (Revelation 12:1).
Genesis 49:8–12ProphecyFulfilledMystery
Jacob blesses Judah as a lion's cub: “The scepter shall never depart from Judah.”
Reading
The kingship of Israel came through Judah in David's line, and the Church has always read the blessing as messianic. In Revelation, Christ is “the lion of the tribe of Judah, the root of David,” who has triumphed and can open the scroll (Revelation 5:5).
Why mystery
The Hebrew of verse 10 is notoriously difficult. Older translations read “until Shiloh comes”; the NABRE reads “until tribute comes to him.” The messianic sense is clear; the exact wording is still debated.

Genesis and Revelation: the Bible's bookends

The last book answers the first. What was lost in the garden is restored, and more, in the city.
