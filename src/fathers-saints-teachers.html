Fathers, Saints and Teachers on the Last Things

From the second century to our own, holy men and women have pondered the end of the world and the coming of Christ. Their words carry different kinds of weight, so each one here is labeled.

Who speaks here, and with what weight

LabelMeaning
Father of the ChurchAn early Christian writer of holy life and orthodox teaching whom the Church recognizes as a witness to Tradition. Where the Fathers agree, their witness carries great weight.
Doctor of the ChurchA saint formally declared a teacher of the whole Church for the depth and soundness of their teaching.
SaintCanonized for holiness. A saint's private visions or predictions are not thereby approved as true.
Approved teacherA priest or theologian writing in communion with the Church, with ecclesiastical approval where given. A respected voice, not an authority.

Even the greatest of these witnesses could be wrong on details of the end times; several Fathers, for example, expected the end in their own day. This site quotes them for their insight, gives the source of every quotation, and never treats a prediction as settled.

The Fathers on Revelation 12

How the early writers read the woman clothed with the sun and the dragon. Each line gives the work and place, with a link to a public translation. See also the study of Revelation 12.

WitnessWhat they saySource
St. Irenaeus of Lyons (died c. 202)
Father; Doctor of the Church		Mary is the New Eve: “the knot of Eve's disobedience was loosed by the obedience of Mary,” and she became “the patroness (advocata) of the virgin Eve.” He does not comment on the woman of Revelation 12, but speaks of the dragon who “will with his tail cause a third part of the stars to fall.”Against Heresies 3.22.4; 5.19.1; 2.31.3 (ANF vol. 1)
St. Victorinus of Pettau (died c. 304)
Father; saint and martyr		The woman is “the ancient Church of fathers, and prophets, and saints, and apostles”; her child is Christ. The stars swept down “may be taken in two ways”: believers seduced, or the angels who fell with the devil. The eagle's wings are “the two prophets.” His text survives in a later, revised form.Commentary on the Apocalypse, ch. 12 (ANF vol. 7)
St. Methodius of Olympus (died c. 311)
Father; saint and martyr		The woman is “our mother the Church,” who “labours and gives birth to those who are baptized.” The stars are “the bodies of heresies,” “dragged down” by the dragon.Symposium 8.5, 8.7, 8.10–11 (ANF vol. 6)
St. Epiphanius of Salamis (died 403)
Father; saint		Tentatively applies the woman's flight on eagle's wings (12:13–14) to Mary at the end of her life: “Perhaps this can be applied to her; I cannot decide for certain.”Panarion 78.11, in The Panarion of Epiphanius of Salamis, Books II and III, trans. Frank Williams, 2nd rev. ed. (Brill, 2012); no free English text is online
St. Gregory the Great (died 604)
Father; Doctor of the Church		Heaven is the Church shining with the virtues of the saints; the dragon's tail casts down those who seemed to be among the elect but abandon heavenly hope for worldly glory.Moralia in Job 32.15
St. Bede the Venerable (died 735)
Doctor of the Church		The dragon has cast down “an innumerable host of angels and men by deceptive testimony.” He reports that Tyconius read the fallen stars as “false brethren.”Explanation of the Apocalypse, on 12:4, trans. E. Marshall (1878)

The Fathers on Revelation 13

How the early writers read the beast from the sea (13:1–10), the second beast from the land, the false prophet (13:11–17), and the mark and the number (13:16–18). Each line gives the work and place, with a link to a public translation. Their guesses at the name are their own, and they said so; the Church has not adopted any of them.

WitnessWhat they saySource
St. Irenaeus of Lyons (died c. 202)
Father; Doctor of the Church		The beast is the Antichrist, and the second beast is his “armour-bearer, whom he also terms a false prophet,” who works wonders “by the working of magic.” In the beast “a recapitulation made of all sorts of iniquity and of every deceit” takes place, so that “all apostate power” may be shut up in him and cast into the fire. On the number he defends 666, found “in all the most approved and ancient copies,” against copies that cut it by fifty (the reading 616), an error he thinks came “through the fault of the copyists.” He weighs Evanthas, Lateinos (“a very probable [solution]”) and Teitan, but concludes: “It is therefore more certain, and less hazardous, to await the fulfilment of the prophecy.” He also expected the Antichrist to reign three and a half years in the temple at Jerusalem and the world to end after six thousand years; these are his own opinions, which the Church has not adopted.Against Heresies 5.28.2–3; 5.29.2; 5.30.1–4 (ANF vol. 1)
St. Hippolytus of Rome (died c. 235)
Father; saint and martyr		“By the beast, then, coming up out of the earth, he means the kingdom of Antichrist”; the two horns are “him and the false prophet after him.” Like a lamb, “he will make himself like the Son of God,” but he speaks like a dragon, a deceiver. On the number, he admits “it is not in our power to explain it exactly,” only “to give a conjectural account of it.” He lists Titan and Evanthas, and favors Latinus, since “those who at present still hold the power are Latins,” yet warns that “we ought neither to give it out as if this were certainly his name.”On Christ and Antichrist 48–50 (ANF vol. 5)
St. Victorinus of Pettau (died c. 304)
Father; saint and martyr		The beast from the sea “signifies the kingdom of that time of Antichrist.” The second beast is “the great and false prophet who is to do signs, and portents, and falsehoods,” who will set “a golden image of Antichrist” in the temple and mark his followers on their foreheads. For the number he reports Greek names that make 666, among them τειταν. On chapter 17 he reads the head “slain to death” as Nero, whom God will raise up and send as a false king for those who will receive him “as if he were the Christ.” This is the old Nero legend, his own opinion and not the Church's. His text survives in a later, revised form.Commentary on the Apocalypse, ch. 13; ch. 17 (ANF vol. 7)
St. Augustine of Hippo (died 430)
Father; Doctor of the Church		Commenting on those who had not worshiped “the beast or its image” (Revelation 20:4), he says that what the beast is “requires a more careful investigation,” yet it is not against the faith “to understand it of the ungodly city itself, and the community of unbelievers set in opposition to the faithful people.” The image is the pretense of those “who profess to believe, but live as unbelievers.” Of what restrains the man of lawlessness in 2 Thessalonians 2:6 he writes, “I frankly confess I do not know what he means,” and of those who expected Nero to return he says, “I wonder that men can be so audacious in their conjectures.”The City of God 20.9; 20.19 (NPNF series 1, vol. 2)
Where this site standsThe Fathers' guesses at the name were offered tentatively, and St. Irenaeus himself counseled waiting for the fulfillment rather than guessing. The Church teaches that before Christ's coming she “must pass through a final trial that will shake the faith of many believers,” and that the Antichrist is “the supreme religious deception” (CCC 675–677); she names no one and sets no date. The mark of the beast stands against the seal of God: the angels hold back harm “until we put the seal on the foreheads of the servants of our God” (Revelation 7:3), and the Lamb's companions bear his name and his Father's name on their foreheads (Revelation 14:1). Our hope is in the Lamb who has already won.
