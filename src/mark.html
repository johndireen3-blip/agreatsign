Prophecy in Mark

Mark is the shortest Gospel and moves fastest. It opens with a prophecy fulfilled and Jesus announcing that the time has come. Three times on the road to Jerusalem he foretells his death and resurrection, and his last long teaching ends with one word for everyone: “Watch!”

The book at a glance

Tradition attributes the second Gospel to Mark, the companion of Peter. Papias, quoted by Eusebius, calls Mark “the interpreter of Peter,” who wrote down accurately what he remembered (Eusebius, Church History 3.39.15), and Irenaeus says the same (Against Heresies 3.1.1). Eusebius also records that Peter's hearers asked Mark to leave them a written record of his preaching (Church History 2.15.1). Most scholars today hold that Mark is the earliest of the four Gospels and that Matthew and Luke drew on it.

Mark has few fulfillment formulas and little apocalyptic imagery of its own. Its prophecy is concentrated in Jesus' own words: the kingdom at hand, the three predictions of the Passion, and the discourse on the Mount of Olives (chapter 13). That discourse is read at length on the Matthew page; the cards below are shorter and note what Mark alone stresses: the Gospel preached first to all nations, and the call to stay awake.

The passages

Mark 1:2–3ProphecyFulfilled
“Behold, I am sending my messenger ahead of you; he will prepare your way. A voice of one crying out in the desert: ‘Prepare the way of the Lord, make straight his paths.’”
Reading
The one time Mark himself quotes the prophets in his narrative is at the very start. It joins Malachi 3:1 and Exodus 23:20 to Isaiah 40:3 under Isaiah's name. The messenger is John the Baptist, the last of the prophets and the forerunner (CCC 523). In Isaiah the way is prepared for “the Lord”; Mark applies it to Jesus.
See also
Malachi 3:23–24 promises Elijah before the day of the Lord; Jesus says Elijah has come (Mark 9:13).
Mark 1:14–15ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“This is the time of fulfillment. The kingdom of God is at hand. Repent, and believe in the gospel.”
Reading
Jesus' first words in Mark announce that the waiting of the prophets is over. The Catechism takes this verse as the opening of the kingdom's proclamation and teaches that the Church is “the seed and beginning” of that kingdom on earth (CCC 541).
Why two labels
The time is fulfilled in Christ's coming. The kingdom he announced keeps growing, hidden like seed in the ground (Mark 4:26–29), until it is complete at his return.
Mark 4:26–32ImageUnfoldingFuture
“This is how it is with the kingdom of God; it is as if a man were to scatter seed on the land and would sleep and rise night and day and the seed would sprout and grow, he knows not how.”
Reading
Two seed parables. The first is found only in Mark: the seed grows while the sower sleeps, until “when the grain is ripe, he wields the sickle at once, for the harvest has come” (4:29). The second is the mustard seed, “the smallest of all the seeds on the earth,” which becomes the largest of plants, “so that the birds of the sky can dwell in its shade” (4:31–32). The Catechism, quoting the Council, says that the word of the Lord is compared to a seed sown in a field, and that those who hear it with faith have received the kingdom itself (CCC 543).
Why two labels
The growth is under way now, hidden and beyond our control. The sickle and the harvest belong to the end (Joel 4:13; Revelation 14:15). The birds in the branches recall the great trees of Ezekiel 17:23 and Daniel 4:9, and many readers have seen in them the nations finding shelter in the kingdom.
Mark 8:31; 9:31; 10:33–34ProphecyFulfilled
“The Son of Man is to be handed over to men and they will kill him, and three days after his death he will rise.”
Reading
Mark's central section is built around three predictions, each more detailed than the last. The third names the chief priests and scribes, the Gentiles, the mocking, spitting and scourging, and ends: “but after three days he will rise” (10:34). Each time the disciples fail to understand, and each time Jesus teaches that following him means taking up the cross. The Catechism cites all three as signs that Jesus knew and freely went to meet his Passion (CCC 474, 557).
See also
“The Son of Man did not come to be served but to serve and to give his life as a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45), echoing the suffering Servant of Isaiah 53.
Mark 9:1ProphecyFulfilledMystery
“Amen, I say to you, there are some standing here who will not taste death until they see that the kingdom of God has come in power.”
Reading
Mark places the Transfiguration immediately after (9:2–8), and many Fathers read it as the fulfillment: Peter, James and John saw the kingdom's glory (St. John Chrysostom, Homilies on Matthew 56; Pope St. Leo the Great, Sermon 51.2). The NABRE note mentions other readings, among them the kingdom established in power through the Church.
Why two labels
The Church has not fixed one reading. Mark's wording, “has come in power,” fits the Transfiguration, the Resurrection and Pentecost alike. It sets no date for the end (13:32). See the parallel at Matthew 16:28.
Mark 9:11–13ProphecyFulfilledMystery
“But I tell you that Elijah has come and they did to him whatever they pleased, as it is written of him.”
Reading
On the way down from the Transfiguration the disciples ask, “Why do the scribes say that Elijah must come first?” (9:11). They have in mind Malachi 3:23–24. Jesus agrees, “Elijah will indeed come first and restore all things,” and at once turns the question to himself: “yet how is it written regarding the Son of Man that he must suffer greatly and be treated with contempt?” (9:12). Elijah has come in John the Baptist, whom Herod put to death (6:17–29), and the Son of Man will suffer too. Mark does not name John here, as Matthew does (Matthew 17:13); his reader already knows. Citing the parallel, the Catechism names John the Elijah who was to come (CCC 718).
Why two labels
Fulfilled in John, the forerunner of 1:2–3. Whether Elijah will also come in person before the end, as St. Augustine reports the faithful expected (City of God 20.29), the Church has not defined. The fuller card is on the Matthew page: Matthew 17:10–13.
Mark 10:35–40ProphecyFulfilled
“The cup that I drink, you will drink, and with the baptism with which I am baptized, you will be baptized…”
Reading
Right after the third prediction of the Passion, James and John ask for the places of honor: “Grant that in your glory we may sit one at your right and the other at your left” (10:37). Jesus asks whether they can drink his cup and share his baptism, the suffering and death he is going to meet (14:36; Luke 12:50). They say they can, and he foretells that they will. The places at his right and left, he says, belong to those for whom they have been prepared (10:40). On Calvary two thieves are crucified there (15:27).
Why fulfilled
James is the first of the Twelve whose martyrdom the New Testament records: King Herod had him put to the sword (Acts 12:2). John's cup was a long witness, which brought him to the island of Patmos for proclaiming God's word (Revelation 1:9). Tertullian records the tradition that at Rome John “was first plunged, unhurt, into boiling oil, and thence remitted to his island-exile” (The Prescription against Heretics 36).
Mark 12:1–12ProphecyFulfilled
“The stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone; by the Lord has this been done, and it is wonderful in our eyes.”
Reading
In the Temple, days before his death, Jesus tells of a vineyard planted like the one in Isaiah's song (Isaiah 5:1–7). The tenants beat and kill the servants the owner sends. “He had one other to send, a beloved son” (12:6), and they kill him too. “What [then] will the owner of the vineyard do? He will come, put the tenants to death, and give the vineyard to others” (12:9). Then Jesus quotes Psalm 118:22–23. The leaders know the parable is aimed at them (12:12).
Why fulfilled
The beloved son is the one the Father's voice named at the Baptism and the Transfiguration (1:11; 9:7). He was rejected and killed, and God raised him to be the cornerstone; Peter preached this verse to the Sanhedrin (Acts 4:11; 1 Peter 2:7). The Catechism names the rejected stone made cornerstone among the images of the Church built on Christ (CCC 756).
Note
The judgment falls on the tenants, the leaders who refused the son, not on the vineyard. The vineyard is kept and given to others: the apostles and the Church gathered from Israel and the nations. Paul insists that God has not rejected his people, for his gifts and call are irrevocable (Romans 11:1–2, 29).
Mark 13:1–2ProphecyFulfilled
“There will not be one stone left upon another that will not be thrown down.”
Reading
A disciple marvels at the Temple's stones; Jesus foretells their fall, fulfilled in AD 70. At his trial false witnesses twisted his words about the Temple (14:58), and the Catechism links his prophecy of the Temple's destruction to the last days, which begin with his own Passover (CCC 585). See The Temple of His Body and the fuller reading on the Matthew page.
Mark 13:5–13ProphecyUnfolding
“But the gospel must first be preached to all nations.”
Reading
Wars, earthquakes and famines are “the beginnings of the labor pains” (13:8), and “it will not yet be the end” (13:7). Disciples will be handed over to councils and governors, and Jesus promises that the Holy Spirit will speak for them (13:11). The heart of the passage is Mark's own short sentence: before the end, the Gospel goes to all nations.
Why unfolding
The mission is still under way, and so is the trial. The promise holds with it: “the one who perseveres to the end will be saved” (13:13). Compare Matthew 24:4–14.
Mark 13:14–23ApocalypseFulfilledMystery
“When you see the desolating abomination standing where he should not (let the reader understand), then those in Judea must flee to the mountains.”
Reading
Jesus takes up Daniel's image (Daniel 9:27; 12:11). Mark's aside, “let the reader understand,” is a nudge to those who know Daniel. The warning to flee fits the war of AD 66–70, and Luke makes the reference plain (Luke 21:20). Mark's grammar is unusual: “standing where he should not.” Some interpreters take this as pointing to a person, and many Fathers saw in the abomination a figure of the final Antichrist (2 Thessalonians 2:4) (St. Irenaeus, Against Heresies 5.25.2; St. Hippolytus, On Christ and Antichrist 62–63).
Why two labels
Fulfilled in the Temple's profanation and fall; whether it also points to a last trial is not settled by the Church. The full discussion is on the Matthew page. The comfort is Mark's too: for the sake of the elect, the Lord “did shorten the days” (13:20).
Mark 13:24–27ApocalypseFuture
“And then they will see ‘the Son of Man coming in the clouds’ with great power and glory.”
Reading
After the darkened sun and falling stars of prophetic language comes Daniel's Son of Man (Daniel 7:13). He sends the angels to gather his elect “from the end of the earth to the end of the sky” (13:27). The Church professes this coming in the Creed (CCC 668–682). See Matthew 24:29–31 for other readings of these verses.
Mark 13:28–31ProphecyFulfilledMystery
“Amen, I say to you, this generation will not pass away until all these things have taken place.”
Reading
“Learn a lesson from the fig tree.” When its branch grows tender and puts out leaves, summer is near. “In the same way, when you see these things happening, know that he is near, at the gates” (13:29). Mark has already told of a fig tree in leaf but without fruit, cursed and withered on either side of the cleansing of the Temple (11:12–14, 20–21). Now a fig tree in leaf is a sign of hope: summer is coming. The NABRE note reads the discourse as placing the destruction it describes before the end of the first Christian generation, while the time of the end itself is known to the Father alone (13:32).
Why two labels
Jerusalem fell within that generation, in AD 70. How far “all these things” reaches beyond it, the Church has not settled, and the verse that follows next but one rules out any calculation. What holds in every generation is the promise: “Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away” (13:31). See the fuller card at Matthew 24:32–35.
Mark 13:32–37ProphecyFuture
“But of that day or hour, no one knows, neither the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father.”
Reading
Mark's discourse ends not with a timetable but with a short parable: a man goes on a journey and tells the gatekeeper to stay awake, since the master may come “in the evening, or at midnight, or at cockcrow, or in the morning” (13:35). The Catechism cites this verse for the rule that the day belongs to the Father alone (CCC 673), and the call to watch for the present time of trial (CCC 672). On the Son's words about not knowing, see CCC 474.
For now
The last word is for everyone: “What I say to you, I say to all: ‘Watch!’” (13:37). Watching is not fear; it is the readiness of a servant who loves the master.
Mark 14:3–9ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“Amen, I say to you, wherever the gospel is proclaimed to the whole world, what she has done will be told in memory of her.”
Reading
At Bethany, two days before Passover, a woman breaks a jar of costly perfumed oil and pours it on Jesus' head. Some call it waste. Jesus defends her: “Let her alone” (14:6). “She has done what she could. She has anticipated anointing my body for burial” (14:8). Then he makes a prophecy about the Gospel itself: it will go to the whole world, and her deed with it. Mark does not name her; John tells of an anointing at Bethany by Mary, the sister of Lazarus (John 12:1–8).
Why two labels
It came true as soon as the Gospels were written, and it comes true again each time the passage is read, as it is in the Passion on Palm Sunday. Here too Jesus takes for granted that the Gospel will go to all nations (13:10).
Mark 14:61–62ProphecyFulfilledFuture
“I am; and ‘you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of the Power and coming with the clouds of heaven.’”
Reading
Asked whether he is the Messiah, Jesus answers plainly for the first time in Mark, joining Psalm 110:1 to Daniel 7:13. Throughout the Gospel he has kept his identity hidden; here, before the court that condemns him, he reveals it.
Why two labels
The reign at the right hand began with the Resurrection and Ascension, from which Christ is Lord of the cosmos and of history (CCC 668). The coming with the clouds awaits his return in glory.
Mark 16:15–20PromiseUnfolding
“Go into the whole world and proclaim the gospel to every creature.”
Reading
In the longer ending of Mark, the risen Lord sends the eleven to the whole world. Whoever believes and is baptized will be saved (16:16), a text the Catechism cites in teaching that Baptism is the first and chief sacrament of the forgiveness of sins (CCC 977). He adds a promise: “These signs will accompany those who believe:” they will drive out demons, speak new languages, and lay hands on the sick, who will recover (16:17–18). The Church sees her ministry of healing in this promise (CCC 1507). Then he is taken up into heaven and takes his seat at the right hand of God (16:19).
Why unfolding
The last verse is the Church's story ever since: “But they went forth and preached everywhere, while the Lord worked with them” and confirmed the word by the signs that followed (16:20).
Note
These verses are missing from some of the oldest manuscripts. The NABRE note explains that early Church writers knew them, that their vocabulary and style suggest an author other than Mark, and that they have traditionally been accepted as part of the canon, defined as such by the Council of Trent. The Church reads them as inspired Scripture.
