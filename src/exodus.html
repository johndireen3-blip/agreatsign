Prophecy in Exodus

God hears the cry of slaves, breaks the power of Pharaoh, leads his people through the sea and comes to live among them in a tent. The New Testament reads the whole story again in Christ, the Lamb whose blood saves, and Revelation ends with its last word: God's dwelling is with the human race.

The book at a glance

Exodus tells how Israel, enslaved in Egypt, is set free: the call of Moses, the plagues, the Passover and the crossing of the sea (chapters 1–15); the journey through the desert to Sinai (15–18); the covenant and its law (19–24, with more law in 25–31 and 34); and the making of the tabernacle, where the glory of the Lord comes to dwell (25–40). Jewish and Christian tradition associate it with Moses; scholars differ on the date of the events and on how the book took its final shape over a long history. The Church reads it as inspired Scripture that teaches the truth about God through the literary forms of its time (CCC 337, 390), and celebrates its central event every year at Easter.

Like Genesis, Exodus is not a book of prophets, and it holds almost no predictions in the strict sense. It looks forward through types, events and institutions that the New Testament sees fulfilled in Christ and the sacraments (the lamb, the sea, the manna, the rock, the blood of the covenant, the tabernacle), and through images that Revelation takes up again (the plagues, the song by the sea, the ark). Jesus' own death is called his “exodus” (Luke 9:31). Each card below names which.

The passages

Exodus 1:15–2:10TypeFulfilled
“Throw into the Nile every boy that is born, but you may let all the girls live.” (1:22)
Reading
Pharaoh, afraid of a people growing too strong for him, orders the Hebrew midwives to kill the boys at birth. “The midwives, however, feared God” (1:17) and let them live, so the king commands that every newborn boy be thrown into the river. One mother hides her son for three months, then sets him in a basket among the reeds of the Nile, where Pharaoh's own daughter finds him: “She named him Moses; for she said, ‘I drew him out of the water’” (2:10). The child marked for death becomes the deliverer of his people.
Fulfilled
Matthew tells the infancy of Jesus as a new Moses story. A king kills the boys of Bethlehem (Matthew 2:16; see Rachel weeping), the child is hidden in Egypt, and the angel's word to Joseph, “for those who sought the child's life are dead” (Matthew 2:20), echoes God's word to Moses in Midian: “Return to Egypt, for all those who sought your life are dead” (Exodus 4:19). The one who will lead the new exodus is himself first rescued from a tyrant. Hebrews remembers the faith of the parents: “By faith Moses was hidden by his parents for three months after his birth” (Hebrews 11:23).
See also
Revelation shows the same pattern at the heart of history: the dragon stands before the woman to devour her child as soon as he is born, and the child is caught up to God (Revelation 12:4–5). The midwives Shiphrah and Puah, who obeyed God rather than the king, are among the first in Scripture to protect human life at its beginning.
Exodus 3:1–15ImageFulfilled
“God replied to Moses: I am who I am. Then he added: This is what you will tell the Israelites: I AM has sent me to you.” (3:14)
Reading
God calls Moses from a bush that burns without being consumed and reveals his name, a name that is both a gift and a mystery. The Catechism reads it as God saying who he is and promising to be with his people, the God who is faithful and who alone truly IS (CCC 205–207, 211, 213). He is also the God of promise: “the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob” (3:6), now coming to keep what he swore in Genesis 15:13–14.
Fulfilled
Jesus takes the name on his own lips: “before Abraham came to be, I AM” (John 8:58; compare 8:24, 28). Revelation opens with greetings “from him who is and who was and who is to come,” and the Lord God says, “I am the Alpha and the Omega” (Revelation 1:4, 8): the name of the bush, unfolded across all time.
See also
The Fathers saw in the bush that burns but is not consumed a figure of the Virgin who bore God without losing her virginity; St. Gregory of Nyssa develops it in his Life of Moses.
Exodus 7–11ImageUnfoldingMystery
“All Egyptians will know that I am the LORD, when I stretch out my hand against Egypt and bring the Israelites out of their midst.” (7:5)
Reading
The plagues are God's judgment on an empire that enslaves and on the idols it serves: “executing judgment on all the gods of Egypt—I, the LORD!” (12:12). Their aim is not destruction but knowledge of God and the freedom of his people, and Israel in Goshen is spared (9:26).
Unfolding
Revelation's trumpets and bowls are drawn from the same palette: water turned to blood (Revelation 8:8; 16:3–4; Exodus 7:20), festering sores (16:2; Exodus 9:10), frogs (16:13; Exodus 8:2), hail (8:7; 16:21; Exodus 9:23), locusts (9:3; Exodus 10:12) and darkness (16:10; Exodus 10:22). As in Egypt, God's servants are sealed and protected (Revelation 7:3; 9:4), and the judgments end in a new song of deliverance (15:3).
Why mystery
Catholic interpreters differ on whether the trumpets and bowls describe patterns that recur throughout history, the trials of the last days, or both. They are symbols, not a timetable, and no one should attach them to dates or to particular living persons. Their message is the one Pharaoh refused to hear: turn to God while there is time.
Exodus 12:1–14, 46TypeFulfilledUnfolding
“Your lamb must be a year-old male and without blemish.” (12:5) “Seeing the blood, I will pass over you.” (12:13)
Reading
Each household sacrifices a lamb, marks its doorposts with the blood and eats the meat in haste, ready to leave. Death passes over the houses marked by the blood. Of the lamb, “You shall not break any of its bones” (12:46).
Fulfilled
“For our paschal lamb, Christ, has been sacrificed” (1 Corinthians 5:7). St. John sets the crucifixion on the “preparation day for Passover” (John 19:14), notes the sprig of hyssop (19:29; compare Exodus 12:22) and sees the legs of Jesus left unbroken so that the scripture might be fulfilled: “Not a bone of it will be broken” (John 19:36). The Catechism calls Jesus the Paschal Lamb, “the symbol of Israel's redemption at the first Passover” (CCC 608). In Revelation he stands at the center of heaven, “a Lamb that seemed to have been slain” (Revelation 5:6).
Unfolding
The Passover was to be “a day of remembrance” (12:14). Jesus celebrated the Last Supper within the Passover and gave it its definitive meaning; his own passing over to the Father, “the new Passover,” is celebrated in the Eucharist (CCC 1340; Matthew 26:2).
Exodus 13:21–22; 14:19–20ImageFulfilledFuture
“Neither the column of cloud by day nor the column of fire by night ever left its place in front of the people.” (13:22)
Reading
“The LORD preceded them, in the daytime by means of a column of cloud to show them the way” (13:21), and at night by a column of fire. At the sea the cloud moves behind Israel and stands between them and the army of Egypt (14:19–20). Later it settles on the tent of meeting, and when it lifts, the people set out (40:34–38). God goes before his people and behind them, and they travel by his presence.
Fulfilled
St. Paul writes that “our ancestors were all under the cloud and all passed through the sea” (1 Corinthians 10:1). The Catechism counts the cloud among the symbols of the Holy Spirit: in the Old Testament it reveals the living God while veiling his glory, in the desert as on Sinai; at the Transfiguration it overshadows Jesus and the disciples; at the Ascension it takes Jesus from their sight (CCC 697). On the mountain, as Jesus speaks with Moses and Elijah of “his exodus that he was going to accomplish in Jerusalem” (Luke 9:31), “a cloud came and cast a shadow over them” (Luke 9:34).
Future
The Catechism adds that the same cloud will reveal the Son of Man in glory on the day of his final coming (CCC 697). Revelation sees a mighty angel “wrapped in a cloud,” whose “feet were like pillars of fire” (Revelation 10:1), and promises a city where “Night will be no more” (22:5), because God himself is its light.
Exodus 14:10–31TypeFulfilled
“The LORD will fight for you; you have only to keep still.” (14:14)
Reading
Trapped between the army and the sea, Israel is told not to fear. The waters are split, “so that the Israelites entered into the midst of the sea on dry land” (14:22), and the people come out on the far shore free. “They believed in the LORD and in Moses his servant” (14:31).
Fulfilled
St. Paul writes that our ancestors “all passed through the sea, and all of them were baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea” (1 Corinthians 10:1–2). The Church sees above all in the crossing of the Red Sea, the liberation of Israel from slavery, a figure of the liberation Baptism brings (CCC 1221), and sings of it at the Easter Vigil before the baptismal water is blessed. Paul adds that these things were written “as a warning to us, upon whom the end of the ages has come” (1 Corinthians 10:11): the baptized can still look back toward Egypt.
Exodus 15:1–21ImageFuture
“Then Moses and the Israelites sang this song to the LORD…” Its last line: “May the LORD reign forever and ever!” (15:1, 18)
Reading
On the far shore Moses, Miriam and the people sing of the LORD who has thrown horse and chariot into the sea, their strength and their savior, glorious in holiness and working wonders, who will lead them to his holy dwelling. It is the first great hymn of the Bible, and it is sung by the saved.
Future
Revelation sees the victors standing by “something like a sea of glass mingled with fire,” and “they sang the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb” (Revelation 15:2–3). Their song praises God's works as great and his ways as just, and foresees all the nations coming to worship him (15:3–4). The Exodus song becomes the song of the whole Church, delivered at last.
Exodus 16TypeFulfilledFuture
“I am going to rain down bread from heaven for you.” (16:4)
Reading
In the desert God feeds his people each day with manna, “like coriander seed, white” (16:31), for forty years. A jar of it is kept “before the LORD” for future generations (16:33), and Hebrews later places it in the ark (Hebrews 9:4). The Catechism notes that the manna always reminded Israel that it lives by the bread of the Word of God (CCC 1334).
Fulfilled
Jesus answers the crowd who recall the manna: “it was not Moses who gave the bread from heaven; my Father gives you the true bread from heaven” (John 6:32). “I am the living bread that came down from heaven; whoever eats this bread will live forever” (6:51). The fathers ate manna and died (6:49); the Eucharist is the bread of eternal life.
Future
To the faithful of Pergamum the risen Christ promises: “To the victor I shall give some of the hidden manna” (Revelation 2:17), the food of the world to come, hidden now and given at the end.
Exodus 17:1–7TypeFulfilled
“Strike the rock, and the water will flow from it for the people to drink.” (17:6)
Reading
The thirsty people quarrel with Moses and test God, asking, “Is the LORD in our midst or not?” (17:7). God answers their doubt with water from the rock.
Fulfilled
St. Paul writes that “they drank from a spiritual rock that followed them, and the rock was the Christ” (1 Corinthians 10:4). From the pierced side of Christ on the Cross flow blood and water (John 19:34), which the Fathers read as the sacraments of the Church, and Jesus promises rivers of living water to all who believe (John 7:37–39). Revelation ends with the river of life-giving water flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb (Revelation 22:1).
Exodus 17:8–16TypeFulfilled
“As long as Moses kept his hands raised up, Israel had the better of the fight…” (17:11)
Reading
Amalek attacks Israel in the desert. Joshua leads the fight in the valley while Moses stands on the hilltop with the staff of God. When his arms grow tired, Aaron and Hur sit him on a stone and hold up his hands, “so that his hands remained steady until sunset” (17:12). The victory is won by prayer as much as by the sword, and “Moses built an altar there, which he named Yahweh-nissi” (17:15). The Catechism names this battle among the moments when Moses interceded for his people: “He does not pray for himself but for the people whom God made his own” (CCC 2577), and it sees in his prayer a figure of Christ, the one mediator (CCC 2574).
The Fathers' reading
St. Justin Martyr saw in the outstretched arms a figure of the Cross. Israel did not prevail simply because Moses prayed, he argues, but because, while the one who bore the name of Jesus led the battle, Moses “himself made the sign of the cross” (Dialogue with Trypho 90). In Greek, Joshua's name is the same as Jesus' (Dialogue 75). This is a spiritual reading, not the literal sense of the story, and it became a favorite of Christian preaching.
Note
The chapter ends with a vow of war against Amalek through the generations (17:14–16). It belongs to the harsh history of its time. Amalek is not a name for any people today, and the New Testament knows only one war for Christians: against sin and the powers of evil, fought with prayer and with the weapons of God (Ephesians 6:10–18).
Exodus 19:3–6PromiseFulfilledUnfolding
“You will be to me a kingdom of priests, a holy nation.” (19:6)
Reading
At Sinai God reminds Israel “how I bore you up on eagles' wings and brought you to myself” (19:4), and promises that if they keep his covenant they will be his “treasured possession among all peoples” (19:5). Israel as a whole is to stand before God for the world. The Catechism begins its teaching on Holy Orders here: the chosen people was constituted by God as “a kingdom of priests and a holy nation” (CCC 1539).
Fulfilled
St. Peter gives the same titles to the baptized: “a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people of his own” (1 Peter 2:9). Revelation praises Christ, “who has made us into a kingdom, priests for his God and Father” (Revelation 1:6; compare 5:10), words the Catechism applies to the whole Church (CCC 1546).
Unfolding
The priestly people is still being gathered from every nation, offering “spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ” (1 Peter 2:5). The eagles' wings return in Revelation, where the woman is given “the two wings of the great eagle” to fly to her place in the desert, far from the serpent (Revelation 12:14).
Exodus 20:1–17TypeFulfilledUnfolding
“I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery.” (20:2)
Reading
“Then God spoke all these words” (20:1). The Ten Commandments begin not with a demand but with what God has done: he has set his people free, and the commandments show them how to live as free people. “The Decalogue must first be understood in the context of the Exodus, God's great liberating event at the center of the Old Covenant” (CCC 2057). The words are written on two tablets of stone, called the Testimony and kept in the ark (CCC 2058), and they carry a promise: “showing love down to the thousandth generation of those who love me and keep my commandments” (20:6).
Fulfilled
Jesus says, “I have come not to abolish but to fulfill” (Matthew 5:17), and to the young man who asks about eternal life, “If you wish to enter into life, keep the commandments” (Matthew 19:17). The Catechism teaches that the Ten Commandments receive their full meaning in the New Covenant in Jesus Christ (CCC 2056). The tablets of stone point to the promise of Jeremiah, a law written on the heart (Jeremiah 31:33), and St. Paul calls the Church a letter written “not on tablets of stone but on tablets that are hearts of flesh” (2 Corinthians 3:3).
Unfolding
In Revelation the children of the woman are “those who keep God's commandments and bear witness to Jesus” (Revelation 12:17), and the ark that held the tablets is seen in the temple of heaven (11:19). The ten words are still the shape of a free life, kept now by the grace of the Spirit.
Exodus 24:3–11TypeFulfilled
“This is the blood of the covenant which the LORD has made with you according to all these words.” (24:8)
Reading
The people promise, “All that the LORD has said, we will hear and do” (24:7). Moses splashes the blood of the sacrifice on the altar and on the people, sealing the covenant. Then the elders go up the mountain: “They saw God, and they ate and drank” (24:11), a covenant meal in God's presence.
Fulfilled
At the Last Supper Jesus takes the cup and repeats Moses' words in a new key: “this is my blood of the covenant, which will be shed on behalf of many for the forgiveness of sins” (Matthew 26:28). The Letter to the Hebrews quotes the Sinai rite and shows that Christ entered the sanctuary “not with the blood of goats and calves but with his own blood” (Hebrews 9:12, 18–22). What the elders tasted on Sinai, seeing God and eating in his presence, the Church receives in the Eucharist and awaits in the wedding feast of the Lamb (Revelation 19:9).
Exodus 25:8–9; 40:34–38TypeFulfilledFuture
“They are to make a sanctuary for me, that I may dwell in their midst.” (25:8)
Reading
Nearly half of Exodus is about a tent. Moses is to build it exactly “according to the pattern shown you on the mountain” (25:40), and when it is finished, “the cloud covered the tent of meeting, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle” (40:34). The God who freed his people now travels with them.
Fulfilled
Hebrews says the tabernacle was “a copy and shadow of the heavenly sanctuary” (Hebrews 8:5) and that Christ passed “through the greater and more perfect tabernacle not made by hands” (9:11). St. John writes that the Word “made his dwelling among us, and we saw his glory” (John 1:14); the Greek verb means that he pitched his tent. Jesus' own body is the true temple (John 2:21). See The Temple of His Body.
Future
Revelation sees “the temple that is the heavenly tent of testimony” opened (Revelation 15:5) and, at the end, hears the promise of the tabernacle fulfilled for all: “Behold, God's dwelling is with the human race” (21:3). In the new Jerusalem there is no temple, “for its temple is the Lord God almighty and the Lamb” (21:22).
Exodus 25:10–22TypeFulfilledMystery
“There I will meet you and there, from above the cover, between the two cherubim on the ark of the covenant, I will tell you all that I command you regarding the Israelites.” (25:22)
Reading
The ark, a chest of acacia wood covered with gold, holds the tablets of the covenant; Hebrews adds the jar of manna and Aaron's staff (Hebrews 9:4). Above its golden cover, between the cherubim, is the place where God meets his people.
Fulfilled
When God's temple in heaven is opened, “the ark of his covenant could be seen in the temple” (Revelation 11:19), with lightning and thunder as at Sinai. At once “a great sign appeared in the sky, a woman clothed with the sun” (Revelation 12:1).
A Marian reading
This is a reading of the Fathers and the liturgy, not something Exodus itself states. As the cloud “settled down upon” the tent and the glory filled it (40:35), the power of the Most High will “overshadow” Mary (Luke 1:35). Elizabeth's “how does this happen to me, that the mother of my Lord should come to me?” (1:43) echoes David's question before the ark (2 Samuel 6:9), and as the ark stayed three months in the hill country (2 Samuel 6:11), Mary stays with Elizabeth “about three months” (Luke 1:56). The Catechism calls Mary “the daughter of Zion in person, the ark of the covenant, the place where the glory of the Lord dwells” (CCC 2676), and the Litany of Loreto invokes her as Ark of the Covenant.
Why mystery
What became of the historical ark is unknown. 2 Maccabees 2:4–8 tells that Jeremiah hid it until God gathers his people again. Revelation answers not with a location but with a vision, and the Church has never encouraged a search.
Exodus 25:23–40TypeFulfilledUnfolding
“On the table you shall always keep showbread set before me.” (25:30)
Reading
In the Holy Place, before the veil, stand a gold-covered table for the bread and a lampstand: “You shall make a menorah of pure beaten gold” (25:31), with seven lamps set up “that they give their light on the space in front of the menorah” (25:37). Twelve loaves, one for each tribe, are always before the LORD and renewed every sabbath (Leviticus 24:5–9), and the lamps are kept burning (27:20–21). Hebrews remembers the furnishings of the first tent: “the lampstand, the table, and the bread of offering” (Hebrews 9:2).
Fulfilled
Jesus recalls how David “went into the house of God and ate the bread of offering” and adds, “something greater than the temple is here” (Matthew 12:4, 6). Christian tradition has seen in the bread kept always before God a figure of the Eucharist, the bread in which Christ himself is present, and in the lamp that never goes out a figure of Christ, the light of the world (John 8:12). This is the Church's spiritual reading of the tabernacle, offered alongside its first sense.
Unfolding
John turns to see who is speaking to him, “and when I turned, I saw seven gold lampstands,” with one like a son of man in their midst, and he is told that “the seven lampstands are the seven churches” (Revelation 1:12–13, 20). The menorah of the Holy Place has become the Church, where Christ walks among his people. To Ephesus he warns that a lampstand can be removed unless the church returns to the love it had at first (2:4–5).
Exodus 28TypeFulfilledFuture
“You shall also make a plate of pure gold and engrave on it, as on a seal engraving, ‘Sacred to the LORD.’” (28:36)
Reading
Aaron's vestments are a sermon in cloth and stone. On the breastpiece are twelve stones, “each stone engraved like a seal with the name of one of the twelve” tribes (28:21), so that “Whenever Aaron enters the sanctuary” (28:29) he carries Israel's names over his heart. On his turban is a gold plate, and “this plate must always be over his forehead” (28:38), so that the people's gifts may be acceptable before the LORD.
Fulfilled
Hebrews proclaims “a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, Jesus, the Son of God” (Hebrews 4:14; see Prophecy in Hebrews). John sees the risen Christ among the lampstands “wearing an ankle-length robe, with a gold sash around his chest” (Revelation 1:13), dressed as a priest. He carries the names of his people on his heart as Aaron did, and he is himself holy to the LORD.
Future
The new Jerusalem has twelve gates bearing the names of the tribes and foundations adorned with precious stones (Revelation 21:12–14, 19–20). What was engraved on the high priest's forehead alone will be written on all the redeemed: “They will look upon his face, and his name will be on their foreheads” (Revelation 22:4). In the end the whole people is a priestly people, sacred to the LORD (compare Ezekiel 9:4).
Exodus 32TypeFulfilled
“Now if you would only forgive their sin! But if you will not, then blot me out of the book that you have written.” (32:32)
Reading
While Moses is on the mountain, the people make a golden calf and cry, “These are your gods, Israel, who brought you up from the land of Egypt” (32:4). “Then they sat down to eat and drink, and rose up to revel” (32:6). The covenant is broken almost as soon as it is sealed, and Moses smashes the tablets (32:19). “But Moses implored the LORD” (32:11), and “So the LORD changed his mind about the punishment he had threatened to inflict on his people” (32:14). Then Moses offers himself in their place. The Catechism describes this prayer: Moses “stands in the breach” for the people, and “He does not pray for himself but for the people whom God made his own” (CCC 2577).
Fulfilled
St. Paul knew the same love: “For I could wish that I myself were accursed and separated from Christ for the sake of my brothers” (Romans 9:3). What Moses and Paul could only offer, Christ did: he took the curse upon himself (Galatians 3:13) and lives forever to intercede for us (Hebrews 7:25). Paul also quotes the scene as a warning to the baptized: “The people sat down to eat and drink, and rose up to revel” (1 Corinthians 10:7). God answered Moses by renewing the covenant and proclaiming his name: “The LORD, the LORD, a God gracious and merciful, slow to anger and abounding in love and fidelity” (34:6).
See also
The book God has written becomes in Revelation the book of life. To the faithful of Sardis the risen Christ promises, “I will never erase his name from the book of life” (Revelation 3:5), and at the judgment “another scroll was opened, the book of life” (20:12).
Exodus 34:29–35TypeFulfilledFuture
“…he did not know that the skin of his face had become radiant while he spoke with the LORD.” (34:29)
Reading
Moses comes down from Sinai with the new tablets, and his face shines from speaking with God. Aaron and the people “were afraid to come near him” (34:30). “When Moses finished speaking with them, he put a veil over his face” (34:33), taking it off whenever he went in to speak with the LORD. The Hebrew word for “radiant” is related to the word for a horn, and the Latin Vulgate translated it “horned,” which is why medieval artists and Michelangelo gave Moses horns.
Fulfilled
At the Transfiguration Moses stands beside Jesus, whose face “shone like the sun” (Matthew 17:2): not a borrowed brightness but his own. St. Paul writes that “the Israelites could not look intently at the face of Moses” (2 Corinthians 3:7), “but whenever a person turns to the Lord the veil is removed” (3:16), and “All of us, gazing with unveiled face on the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image” (3:18; see Prophecy in 2 Corinthians). As St. John puts it, “the law was given through Moses, grace and truth came through Jesus Christ” (John 1:17).
Future
Moses was told that no one could see God's face and live (33:20). John sees the risen Christ, “and his face shone like the sun at its brightest” (Revelation 1:16), and he is promised for all God's servants: “They will look upon his face” (22:4).

Exodus and Revelation: the new exodus

Revelation tells the end of history as a final Exodus: a people freed by the blood of the Lamb, brought through judgment and the sea, to the place where God dwells with them.
