Prophecy in Hosea

A prophet told to love an unfaithful wife, so that Israel could see how God loves her. Hosea is the Bible's great book of wounded, faithful love: God the husband who will not let his bride go, the father who taught his child to walk, the Lord who leads his people back into the wilderness to speak to her heart.

The book at a glance

Hosea, son of Beeri, prophesied in the northern kingdom of Israel in the eighth century before Christ, from the prosperous last years of Jeroboam II to the troubled decades before Samaria fell to Assyria in 722 (1:1). He is the only writing prophet who came from the north and spoke to it. His words were evidently treasured in Judah after the north fell, and many scholars see a Judean hand in the book's present form; its Hebrew is among the hardest in the Old Testament, and translations differ more than usual. The Church receives the whole as inspired Scripture either way. It stands first among the twelve “minor” prophets, minor only in length.

Hosea's prophecy is less about events than about a love. Israel's worship of the Baals is adultery; her alliances with Egypt and Assyria are prostitution; and still God will not give her up. Out of this come promises the New Testament quotes again and again: the people called “Not-My-People” called God's children, the son called out of Egypt, mercy desired more than sacrifice, death robbed of its sting. A note on numbering: the NABRE's Hosea 2:1–25 is 1:10–2:23 in many other Bibles, and its 14:2–10 is 14:1–9.

The passages

Hosea 1–3ImageFulfilledUnfolding
“Go, love a woman who is loved by her spouse but commits adultery; Just as the LORD loves the Israelites…” (3:1)
Reading
The book opens with a command that is hard to read: “Go, get for yourself a woman of prostitution and children of prostitution, for the land prostitutes itself, turning away from the LORD” (1:2). Hosea marries Gomer, and their children's names become sermons: Jezreel, Not-Pitied, Not-My-People (1:4–9). When she leaves him, he is told to love her again, and he buys her back (3:1–2). Whether these chapters tell of one marriage or two, and how literally, is debated; their meaning is not. Hosea's own heartbreak becomes the window through which Israel sees God's.
Fulfilled
The Catechism teaches that the prophets, seeing God's covenant with Israel “in the image of exclusive and faithful married love,” prepared the people to understand marriage as God understands it, and it points to Hosea 1–3 (CCC 1611). The image is completed when Christ gives himself up for the Church as a husband for his bride (Ephesians 5:25–32), and John the Baptist calls Jesus “the bridegroom” (John 3:29).
Why unfolding
Hosea ends this section with a look far ahead: “Afterward the Israelites will turn back and seek the LORD, their God, and David, their king; They will come trembling to the LORD and to his bounty, in the last days” (3:5). A king of David's line in the last days is a messianic hope, and the return it describes is still going on in every heart that comes home.
Hosea 2:16–25PromiseFulfilledUnfoldingFuture
“I will betroth you to me forever: I will betroth you to me with justice and with judgment, with loyalty and with compassion; I will betroth you to me with fidelity, and you shall know the LORD.” (2:21–22)
Reading
After the charges of the courtroom (“Accuse your mother, accuse!” 2:4), the punishment God chooses is a second honeymoon: “I will allure her now; I will lead her into the wilderness and speak persuasively to her” (2:16). The valley of trouble becomes “the valley of Achor as a door of hope” (2:17), and Israel will answer “as in the days of her youth” (2:17). She will call him “My husband,” and never again “My baal” (2:18). Even creation is drawn in: “Bow and sword and warfare I will destroy from the land” (2:20). Three times God says “I will betroth you,” and the gift he promises is not a thing but a knowing: “you shall know the LORD.”
Fulfilled
The new covenant is this betrothal. Jeremiah promises that all will know the LORD (Jeremiah 31:34), and the Church is the bride Christ has made his own.
Future
Revelation sees the wedding itself: “For the wedding day of the Lamb has come, his bride has made herself ready” (Revelation 19:7), and the holy city comes down “prepared as a bride adorned for her husband” (21:2). “He will dwell with them and they will be his people” (21:3) is Hosea's promise kept forever.
Note
In many other Bibles these verses are 2:14–23, so “Hosea 2:19–20” elsewhere is the NABRE's 2:21–22.
Hosea 2:1, 25; 1:9ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“I will have pity on Not-Pitied. I will say to Not-My-People, ‘You are my people,’ and he will say, ‘My God!’” (2:25)
Reading
The third child's name was the covenant reversed: “for you are not my people, and I am not ‘I am’ for you” (1:9), an echo of the divine name given to Moses (Exodus 3:14). But God does not leave the names as they are. “Instead of being told, ‘You are Not-My-People,’ They will be called, ‘Children of the living God’” (2:1). The names of judgment are renamed in mercy.
Fulfilled
St. Paul hears in these verses the calling of the Gentiles alongside the Jews: “Those who were not my people I will call ‘my people,’” and “there they shall be called children of the living God” (Romans 9:25–26). St. Peter writes to the baptized, “Once you were ‘no people’ but now you are God's people; you ‘had not received mercy’ but now you have received mercy” (1 Peter 2:10). St. Augustine reads Hosea the same way (City of God 18.28).
Why unfolding
The renaming happens again whenever someone far off is baptized and receives the name of a child of God.
Note
The NABRE's 2:1 is 1:10 in many other Bibles, and its 2:25 is 2:23.
Hosea 6:1–3ProphecyFulfilledMystery
“He will revive us after two days; on the third day he will raise us up, to live in his presence.” (6:2)
Reading
The people say to one another, “Come, let us return to the LORD, For it is he who has torn, but he will heal us; he has struck down, but he will bind our wounds” (6:1). In its setting this is Israel's own song of repentance, and God's reply shows it was shallow: “Your loyalty is like morning mist” (6:4). The “two days” and “third day” mean a short time; recovery will come soon.
The Fathers' reading
The Fathers heard more. St. Augustine says Hosea here foretold Christ's resurrection on the third day, and that we rise with him (City of God 18.28; compare Colossians 3:1). St. Paul says Christ “was raised on the third day in accordance with the scriptures” (1 Corinthians 15:4) without naming which scriptures; Hosea 6:2, with Jonah's three days (Matthew 12:40), is the text Christians have most often proposed.
Why mystery
No New Testament writer quotes this verse, and in context the speakers are not yet sincere. The Fathers' reading is ancient and reverent, but it is a reading, not a direct citation; this page marks it so. What is certain is the hope it carries: the God who wounds in order to heal.
Hosea 6:4–6ProphecyUnfolding
“For it is loyalty that I desire, not sacrifice, and knowledge of God rather than burnt offerings.” (6:6)
Reading
Israel's love is like dew that is gone by morning, and offerings cannot cover for it. What God wants is hesed, the steadfast covenant love the NABRE translates “loyalty,” and a real knowledge of him. Like Micah 6:8, this is prophecy in its first sense: God's word for now.
In the Gospel
Jesus quotes this verse twice in Matthew, both times to defend mercy. When he eats with tax collectors and sinners: “Go and learn the meaning of the words, ‘I desire mercy, not sacrifice’” (Matthew 9:13). When his hungry disciples pick grain on the sabbath: “If you knew what this meant, ‘I desire mercy, not sacrifice,’ you would not have condemned these innocent men” (Matthew 12:7). The Greek Old Testament that Matthew follows reads “mercy,” where the NABRE's Hosea reads “loyalty.”
Why unfolding
Jesus does not abolish sacrifice; he offers the one sacrifice that is wholly love. The word still judges every religion of outward forms without a heart, including our own.
Hosea 11:1–9TypeFulfilled
“When Israel was a child I loved him, out of Egypt I called my son.” (11:1)
Reading
Here God is not husband but father. He remembers the Exodus as a parent remembers a toddler: “Yet it was I who taught Ephraim to walk, who took them in my arms” (11:3); “I drew them with human cords, with bands of love” (11:4). The son ran from him, and judgment looms, and then God's heart turns within him: “My heart is overwhelmed, my pity is stirred” (11:8). The reason he will not destroy is his very holiness: “For I am God and not a man, the Holy One present among you” (11:9). The Catechism cites this chapter when it teaches that God's love for Israel is compared to a father's love for his son (CCC 219).
Fulfilled
Hosea looks back, not forward. But St. Matthew sees that the history of God's son Israel is relived in God's only Son. When the Holy Family returns from Egypt after Herod's death, Matthew writes that this happened “that what the Lord had said through the prophet might be fulfilled, ‘Out of Egypt I called my son’” (Matthew 2:15). This is fulfillment by type: what Israel was, Jesus is, and where Israel failed, he is faithful.

Exodus 4:22; Prophecy in Matthew.
Hosea 13:14ProphecyFulfilledFuture
“Shall I deliver them from the power of Sheol? shall I redeem them from death? Where are your plagues, O death! where is your sting, Sheol!”
Reading
This is one of the most debated verses in Hosea. The NABRE reads it as a threat: God will not rescue unrepentant Ephraim, and he summons death and Sheol to send their plagues, for “Compassion is hidden from my eyes.” Its note adds that St. Paul uses the text differently, as a cry of victory over death. Other translations read the first lines as promises, that God will ransom them from Sheol and redeem them from death. The Hebrew allows both.
Fulfilled
St. Paul joins Hosea to Isaiah 25:8 in the great hymn of the resurrection: “Death is swallowed up in victory. Where, O death, is your victory? Where, O death, is your sting?” (1 Corinthians 15:54–55). In Christ's resurrection the question Hosea asked against Israel is turned, once for all, against death itself.
Future
Death is defeated but not yet gone. Its end comes with the resurrection of the body, when “there shall be no more death” (Revelation 21:4).
Hosea 14:2–9PromiseUnfolding
“I will heal their apostasy, I will love them freely; for my anger is turned away from them.” (14:5)
Reading
The book ends as it has longed to end, with an invitation: “Return, Israel, to the LORD, your God; you have stumbled because of your iniquity” (14:2). God even gives the words to say, a prayer that renounces Assyria, warhorses and idols. Then he answers with a promise of springtime: “I will be like the dew for Israel: he will blossom like the lily” (14:6), rooted like the cedars of Lebanon, fragrant, fruitful, shaded. Hosea's last word is not judgment but healing.
Why unfolding
This is the shape of every conversion: God calls, gives the words of repentance, and loves “freely,” before we deserve it. The Church makes these words her own whenever a sinner comes home, above all in the sacrament of Penance.
Note
The NABRE's 14:2–10 is 14:1–9 in many other Bibles.
