St. Teresa Benedicta of the Cross (Edith Stein) on the Marriage of the Lamb

A Jewish philosopher who found the truth in Christ, a Carmelite who took her name from the cross, and a martyr of Auschwitz. In the first years of the Second World War, Edith Stein turned her sisters' eyes to the Book of Revelation: to the Lamb who was slain, to the bride he is making ready, and to the cross as the one hope that does not fail.

At a glance

WhoEdith Stein (1891–1942), born to a Jewish family in Breslau, philosopher and pupil of Edmund Husserl, baptized in 1922, a Discalced Carmelite from 1933 as Sister Teresa Benedicta of the Cross; murdered at Auschwitz in August 1942
StandingSaint and martyr; co-patroness of Europe beatified in Cologne on May 1, 1987; canonized by Pope St. John Paul II on October 11, 1998; proclaimed co-patroness of Europe on October 1, 1999. Feast: August 9.
The worksTwo short meditations written for her community's renewal of vows on the feast of the Exaltation of the Cross: Ave Crux, Spes Unica (September 14, 1939) and The Marriage of the Lamb (September 14, 1940); her letter to Pope Pius XI (April 1933)
WeightA saint's spiritual writing, not a teaching of the Magisterium. The Church holds up her life and death as a witness of holiness; her meditations are a Carmelite's prayer over Scripture, offered to her sisters. Her English texts are under copyright (ICS Publications), so they are summarized here, not quoted.

Her life

Edith Stein was born in Breslau on October 12, 1891, the youngest of eleven children of a devout Jewish family. As a young woman she gave up the practice of faith. She studied philosophy at Göttingen under Edmund Husserl, the founder of phenomenology, became his assistant, and in 1917 received her doctorate summa cum laude at Freiburg for a thesis on the problem of empathy.

In the summer of 1921 she read the autobiography of St. Teresa of Avila in a single night. “When I had finished the book, I said to myself: This is the truth” (Vatican biography). She was baptized on January 1, 1922. For more than a decade she taught and lectured, until the Nazi racial laws of 1933 made it impossible for her to continue. On October 14, 1933 she entered the Carmel of Cologne and received the name Teresa Benedicta of the Cross.

After the pogrom of November 1938 her community sent her across the border to the Carmel of Echt in the Netherlands, where she arrived on New Year's Eve 1938. On August 2, 1942, after the Dutch bishops had publicly protested the deportation of the Jews, the Gestapo arrested her together with her sister Rosa, also a convert. Her last words heard in Echt were to Rosa: “Come, we are going for our people” (Vatican biography). She was deported to Auschwitz and, as the Vatican biography carefully says, probably died in the gas chambers on August 9, 1942, the date the Church keeps as her feast.

Why Edith Stein matters here

Revelation was written for a Church under persecution, and Edith Stein read it as a member of a people being hunted to death. She did not use the book to decode her times or to predict their end. She used it to pray. Her meditations take two of its great images, the Lamb who was slain and the wedding feast of the Lamb, and set them beside the cross, which she loved as the only hope. That is the reading this site tries to follow: the visions of Revelation as consolation and a call to faithfulness, not a timetable.

Her own life became a commentary on the book. The woman of Revelation 12 is pursued by the dragon, who goes off “to wage war against the rest of her offspring, those who keep God's commandments and bear witness to Jesus” (Revelation 12:17). Stein was one of that offspring, and she bore her witness to the end.

Her meditations and her letter

Ave Crux, Spes Unica · Exaltation of the Cross, September 14, 1939Spiritual meditationUnfolding
The occasion
Each year the Carmelites renew their vows on the feast of the Exaltation of the Cross. In 1939 the feast fell two weeks after the German invasion of Poland, and Stein wrote a meditation for her sisters at Echt. Its title echoes a line of the ancient Passiontide hymn Vexilla Regis, O Crux, ave, spes unica: “Hail, O Cross, our only hope.”
Teaching (paraphrase)
She sets the renewal of vows against a world now at war, a world as if on fire, and points her sisters to the Crucified. He asks whether they will remain faithful to him; their vows bind them to his cross, and through that union their prayer and sacrifice can reach wherever there is suffering. The cross is not defeat but the one hope that holds when every other hope collapses.
Her conviction
The biography the Holy See published for her canonization gives these words as the conviction of her heart: “‘Ave, Crux, Spes unica’ (I welcome you, Cross, our only hope)” (Vatican biography).
Why “Unfolding”
The Lamb who conquers by being slain (Revelation 5:6) is the pattern of every age of the Church. Stein reads the war of her own day in that light, without claiming that it was the last.
The Marriage of the Lamb · For September 14, 1940Spiritual meditationUnfoldingFuture
“For the wedding day of the Lamb has come, his bride has made herself ready.” (Revelation 19:7)
The occasion
A year later, with the Netherlands now under German occupation, the prioress at Echt again asked Stein for a meditation for the renewal of vows. She took her theme from Revelation 19:7–9, the song of the multitude in heaven and the angel's word: “Blessed are those who have been called to the wedding feast of the Lamb.”
The Lamb and the bride (paraphrase)
The bride of the Lamb is the Church, which comes down from heaven: she is born of grace and bound to Christ forever. Stein traces the wedding back to the Incarnation, when the eternal Son united himself to our nature in the womb of the Virgin, and sees Mary as the mother of all the redeemed. At the cross, when Jesus gave his Mother to John, this hidden union came into the open. The Lamb who is the Bridegroom is “the Lamb that was slain” (Revelation 5:12).
Not yet the feast
The Church is already the bride, Stein says, but she has not yet reached her fullness. The wedding feast comes only when the dragon has been overthrown and the last of the redeemed have fought their battle through. Until then the bride makes herself ready, and the vows of religious life are one way of doing so.
Counsel to her sisters (paraphrase)
She asks her community to examine itself honestly. Are their prayers for peace truly pure, or mixed with self-interest? They must not judge others. And those who find, after many years in the convent, that they are still far from holiness should not lose heart: the grace of the Redeemer is still offered to them, as it was to the good thief on Golgotha. She ends with the cry of the bride at the close of the Bible, “Come, Lord Jesus” (Revelation 22:17, 20).
Why two labels
The Church is being made ready in every age; the wedding feast itself is still to come (CCC 677).
Letter to Pope Pius XI · April 1933LetterFulfilled
Responsibility falls also “on those who keep silent in the face of such happenings.”
The letter
In April 1933, weeks after the Nazis came to power and soon after the national boycott of Jewish businesses, Stein wrote to Pope Pius XI from Münster, where she was teaching. She wrote as a daughter of the Jewish people who by God's grace had become a daughter of the Church, and she begged the Holy Father to speak. The letter is undated; it reached the Pope through Archabbot Raphael Walzer of Beuron, her spiritual director, whose covering letter is dated April 12, 1933.
Its plea (paraphrase)
She warned that what was being done to the Jews in Germany came from a government that called itself Christian, and asked whether the effort to destroy Jewish blood was not an abuse of the most holy humanity of our Savior, of the Virgin and of the apostles. She feared the worst for the prestige of the Church if the silence continued.
Its publication
The letter was kept in the Vatican archives and released to the public on February 15, 2003, when the Holy See opened further sections of its archives for the pontificate of Pius XI. The English wording quoted above is the translation published in Inside the Vatican (March 2003), reprinted at Catholic Culture.
Why “Fulfilled”
She foresaw that the persecution would grow worse, and it did. She did not prophesy as a seer; she read the signs of her time with open eyes.
Canonization homily · St. John Paul II, October 11, 1998HomilyUnfolding
“Ever blossoming, the tree the Cross continues to bear new fruits of salvation.”
The bride under the cross
John Paul II opened the homily with St. Paul: “Far be it from me to glory except in the Cross of our Lord Jesus Christ” (Galatians 6:14). He quoted her own words: “Beneath the Cross I understood the destiny of God's People,” and “today I know far better what it means to be the Lord's bride under the sign of the Cross.” The bride of her meditation is the bride she became.
Truth and love
She “did not only write profound pages about the ‘science of the Cross’,” the Pope said; she was “thoroughly trained in the school of the Cross.” And he gave her message in one line: “Do not accept anything as the truth if it lacks love. And do not accept anything as love which lacks truth!” (Homily, October 11, 1998).
Co-patroness of Europe
A year later he named her, with St. Bridget of Sweden and St. Catherine of Siena, a co-patroness of Europe. He wrote that she felt that “in the systematic extermination of the Jews the Cross of Christ was being laid on her people,” and that her encounter with Christianity “did not lead her to reject her Jewish roots” (Spes Aedificandi 9). See also John Paul II on the woman and the dragon.

Edith Stein and the Book of Revelation

  • The Lamb who was slain. At the center of heaven John sees a Lamb that has been slain yet stands (Revelation 5:6). For Stein the cross is not a stage Christ passed through but the very form of his victory: “Worthy is the Lamb that was slain” (Revelation 5:12).
  • The wedding feast. “Blessed are those who have been called to the wedding feast of the Lamb” (Revelation 19:9). The Church is the bride; every Mass and every faithful life is part of her making ready. The Catechism likewise speaks of the Church's final perfection as the coming of the Bride (CCC 677, 1045).
  • The woman and the dragon. The feast comes only after the dragon is overthrown (Revelation 12; 20:10). Stein lived the war of the dragon against the woman's offspring (Revelation 12:17) in her own flesh. See Revelation 12.
  • The cross as the sign of hope. Not a sign of fear but of the love that has already won. “Hail, O Cross, our only hope.”
Read with careEdith Stein named no date for the end and made no prophecies about it. Her meditations are prayer, not a commentary claiming to unlock Revelation's timetable. Being canonized means the Church holds up her holiness and her martyrdom, not that every line she wrote is doctrine. Her English texts belong to ICS Publications; read them whole in The Hidden Life.

For reflection

Am I making myself ready?

The bride “has made herself ready” (Revelation 19:7). Edith Stein asked her sisters whether their prayer was pure and whether they had kept their first love. What would it mean for me to welcome the cross I am carrying now as a sign of hope rather than defeat?
