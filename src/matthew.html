Prophecy in Matthew

Matthew is the Gospel of fulfillment. Again and again he writes that something happened “to fulfill what the Lord had said through the prophet.” Then, on the Mount of Olives, Jesus himself becomes the prophet and speaks of the Temple's fall, the life of the Church and his return.

The book at a glance

Tradition attributes the first Gospel to the apostle Matthew, the tax collector called by Jesus (9:9). Written for a community steeped in the Jewish Scriptures, it presents Jesus as the Messiah, the Son of David, and the new Moses who teaches from the mountain. Its five great discourses end with the Olivet Discourse (chapters 24–25), the longest teaching in the Gospels on the last things.

Matthew looks back and forward. Looking back, it shows Old Testament prophecy fulfilled in Christ, using a formula more than ten times. Looking forward, it records Jesus' own prophecy and, in chapter 24, a discourse written in the apocalyptic style of Daniel. On this page, Jesus' prophecies are where the four categories matter most.

The passages

Matthew 1:22–23; 2:5–6, 15ProphecyFulfilled
“Behold, the virgin shall be with child and bear a son, and they shall name him Emmanuel,” which means “God is with us.”
Reading
Matthew opens with three fulfillments: Isaiah 7:14, the virgin's son (1:23); Micah 5:1, the ruler from Bethlehem (2:6); and Hosea 11:1, “Out of Egypt I called my son” (2:15). Hosea spoke of Israel's exodus; Matthew sees Jesus living Israel's story again, faithfully.
Note
These show the “more than one fulfillment” pattern. Isaiah's sign spoke first to King Ahaz; Hosea's words looked back before Matthew saw them look forward. See how each book is read.
See also
Matthew's last words echo his first: “I am with you always, until the end of the age” (28:20). Emmanuel, God with us, from beginning to end.
Matthew 5:3–12PromiseUnfoldingFuture
“Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.”
Reading
The Sermon on the Mount opens with the Beatitudes, and the first and the last carry the same promise in the present tense: “theirs is the kingdom of heaven” (5:3, 10). The ones between speak in the future: the mourners will be comforted, the meek will inherit the land, the clean of heart will see God. The Catechism calls the Beatitudes the heart of Jesus' preaching and “the paradoxical promises that sustain hope in the midst of tribulations” (CCC 1716–1717).
Why two labels
The kingdom already belongs to the poor in spirit and to those persecuted for righteousness. Its full reward is still ahead: “Rejoice and be glad, for your reward will be great in heaven” (5:12). Seeing God face to face waits for the end (Revelation 22:4).
Matthew 10:16–23ProphecyUnfoldingMystery
“You will be hated by all because of my name, but whoever endures to the end will be saved.”
Reading
Sending out the Twelve, Jesus warns them: “Behold, I am sending you like sheep in the midst of wolves” (10:16). They will be handed over to courts, scourged in synagogues and led before governors and kings, and the Spirit of their Father will speak through them. This reaches beyond the first mission in Galilee. It describes the Church's whole road, as Acts shows from its early chapters, and Jesus repeats it on the Mount of Olives (24:9–13).
Why two labels
The trial is unfolding wherever disciples suffer for his name. The last line is harder: “Amen, I say to you, you will not finish the towns of Israel before the Son of Man comes” (10:23). The NABRE note says this cannot mean the final return, admits that it is difficult to know what Matthew meant, and suggests either an anticipated coming of the risen Christ (28:16–20) or the judgment of AD 70. The Church has not fixed a reading.
Matthew 12:38–41ProphecyFulfilled
“Just as Jonah was in the belly of the whale three days and three nights, so will the Son of Man be in the heart of the earth three days and three nights.”
Reading
Asked for a sign, Jesus gives only one, the same he gives in the Temple in John 2: his death and resurrection. Fulfilled on Easter morning.
See also
The Temple of His Body; Matthew 16:4; 26:61.
Matthew 13:24–50ProphecyUnfoldingFuture
“Just as weeds are collected and burned [up] with fire, so will it be at the end of the age.”
Reading
In the parable of the weeds, an enemy sows weeds among the wheat, and the householder forbids his servants to pull them up early. Both are to grow until the harvest, “but gather the wheat into my barn” (13:30). Jesus explains: “The harvest is the end of the age, and the harvesters are angels” (13:39). The parable of the net (13:47–50) teaches the same. The Catechism uses the image for history itself: good and evil grow up together, like the wheat and the tares, until Christ comes at the end to achieve the definitive triumph of good over evil (CCC 681).
Why two labels
The field is the world now, where good and evil grow side by side, and disciples are told not to tear up what only the Lord can judge. The sorting belongs to the harvest. Then “the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father” (13:43).
See also
The harvest of the earth in Revelation 14:14–16.
Matthew 16:18–19ProphecyUnfolding
“You are Peter, and upon this rock I will build my church, and the gates of the netherworld shall not prevail against it.”
Reading
Jesus speaks in the future tense: I will build. The building begins with Peter and the apostles and goes on through the centuries (CCC 552–553, 881). It is the same building Paul describes, the temple “growing” in the Lord (Ephesians 2:21–22), and the promise holds: the powers of death will not overcome it.
Why unfolding
The Church is still being built, and still under assault. Revelation shows the same Church as the woman kept safe in the desert (12:6, 14) and the city whose foundations bear the names of the twelve apostles (21:14).
Matthew 16:27–28ProphecyFulfilledMystery
“There are some standing here who will not taste death until they see the Son of Man coming in his kingdom.”
Reading
The Fathers most often saw this fulfilled six days later in the Transfiguration (17:1–8), where three apostles saw the kingdom's glory (St. John Chrysostom, Homilies on Matthew 56; Pope St. Leo the Great, Sermon 51.2). Others point to the Resurrection, Pentecost, or the judgment on Jerusalem in AD 70.
Why mystery
The Church has not fixed one reading. All agree that Jesus was not mistaken about a timetable for the end; he expressly said no one knows it (24:36).
Matthew 17:10–13ProphecyFulfilledMystery
“Elijah will indeed come and restore all things; but I tell you that Elijah has already come, and they did not recognize him but did to him whatever they pleased.”
Reading
Coming down from the mountain where they have just seen Elijah with Moses, the disciples ask why the scribes say that Elijah must come first. They have in mind Malachi's promise that God would send Elijah before the day of the Lord (Malachi 3:23–24). Jesus answers that Elijah has come already, and that the Son of Man will suffer as he did. “Then the disciples understood that he was speaking to them of John the Baptist” (17:13). Citing this passage, the Catechism names John the Elijah who was to come (CCC 718; see also Matthew 11:14).
Why two labels
In John the promise is fulfilled: he went before the Lord “in the spirit and power of Elijah” (Luke 1:17). But Jesus' words “will indeed come and restore all things” led many Fathers to expect Elijah himself before the end as well. St. Augustine reports it as the common belief of the faithful that Elijah will come before the judgment and lead the Jews to faith in Christ by explaining the law (City of God 20.29), and St. Hippolytus named Elijah, with Enoch, as the two prophets of the last days (On Christ and Antichrist 43; compare Revelation 11:3–12). The Church has not defined this.
See also
The parallel in Mark, which joins Elijah's sufferings to the Son of Man's: Mark 9:11–13.
Matthew 24:1–2ProphecyFulfilled
“There will not be left here a stone upon another stone that will not be thrown down.”
Reading
As the disciples admire Herod's Temple, Jesus foretells its destruction. Forty years later, in AD 70, Roman armies under Titus burned the Temple and razed the city, as the historian Josephus records in The Jewish War. The disciples' question that follows (24:3) joins two things: the Temple's fall and “the end of the age.” Jesus' answer moves between them, and that is the key to the whole discourse.
Matthew 24:4–14ProphecyUnfolding
“And this gospel of the kingdom will be preached throughout the world as a witness to all nations, and then the end will come.”
Reading
False messiahs, wars, famines and earthquakes are “the beginning of the labor pains” (24:8), not signs that the end is here. Persecution, betrayal and lawlessness will cause “the love of many” to grow cold (24:12), a warning the Church links to the final trial and apostasy (CCC 675). Meanwhile the Gospel goes out to all nations.
Why unfolding
These things have marked every century. The preaching of the Gospel to all nations is still under way.
See also
The four horsemen (Revelation 6:1–8) follow the same order: conquest, war, famine, death.
Matthew 24:15–28ApocalypseFulfilledMystery
“When you see the desolating abomination spoken of through Daniel the prophet standing in the holy place…”
Reading
Jesus takes up Daniel's image (Daniel 9:27; 11:31; 12:11), first fulfilled when Antiochus IV set up a pagan altar in the Temple in 167 BC (1 Maccabees 1:54). Jesus applies it to the coming profanation and destruction of the Temple. Luke's version makes this explicit: “When you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies” (Luke 21:20). Many Fathers also saw in it a figure of the final Antichrist, who will “seat himself in the temple of God” (2 Thessalonians 2:4) (St. Irenaeus, Against Heresies 5.25.2; St. Hippolytus, On Christ and Antichrist 62–63).
Why two labels
Fulfilled in the events of AD 66–70. Whether the passage also points to a last trial still to come is held by many Fathers but not settled by the Church.
Matthew 24:29–31ApocalypseFuture
“They will see the Son of Man coming upon the clouds of heaven with power and great glory.”
Reading
The darkened sun and falling stars are the language of Old Testament apocalyptic (Isaiah 13:10; Joel 2:10), and the Son of Man on the clouds is Daniel's vision (Daniel 7:13–14). His angels gather the elect “from the four winds.” The Church professes this return in the Creed: he “will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead.”
Note
Some interpreters read these verses as symbolic language for the judgment of AD 70. The Church's faith in a real, visible return of Christ does not depend on settling that question: “every eye will see him” (Revelation 1:7; CCC 668–682).
Matthew 24:30, 32–35ProphecyFulfilledMystery
“Amen, I say to you, this generation will not pass away until all these things have taken place.”
Reading
Matthew alone says that before the Son of Man is seen, “the sign of the Son of Man will appear in heaven” (24:30). The NABRE note suggests the sign may be his glorious appearance itself. Many Fathers saw the Cross: St. Cyril of Jerusalem taught that Christ's true sign is the Cross, which will go before the King, shining (Catechetical Lectures 15.22), and St. John Chrysostom read it the same way (Homilies on Matthew 76). Then comes a parable: “Learn a lesson from the fig tree.” When its branch turns tender, summer is near. “In the same way, when you see all these things, know that he is near, at the gates” (24:33).
Why two labels
“This generation” is the hard saying. The NABRE note suggests that for Matthew it most likely meant the generation to which he and his community belonged, and the Temple did fall within it, in AD 70. How much of “all these things” belongs to that generation and how much to the end, the Church has not settled. Jesus joins the saying at once to his warning that no one knows the day (24:36), so it is no timetable. What stands firm is the next verse: “Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not pass away” (24:35).
See also
Mark 13:28–31; Luke 21:29–33.
Matthew 24:36–25:13ProphecyFuture
“But of that day and hour no one knows, neither the angels of heaven, nor the Son, but the Father alone.”
Reading
The rule for every reader of prophecy, and the reason this site sets no dates (CCC 673). Jesus follows it with images of readiness: the days of Noah, the thief in the night, the faithful servant, and the ten virgins with their lamps. The point is not to calculate but to be ready.
Matthew 25:14–30ProphecyFuture
“Come, share your master’s joy.”
Reading
A man going on a journey entrusts his possessions to his servants: five talents, two, and one. “After a long time the master of those servants came back and settled accounts with them” (25:19). Two have traded and doubled what they received; the third buried his in fear. The parable stands between the ten virgins and the Last Judgment and has the same subject: the Lord's return, and what his servants do while he is away. The Catechism counts it among the parables by which Jesus calls for a decision, in deeds and not in words alone (CCC 546).
For now
“After a long time” allows for delay, and the master asks for faithfulness, not success. The reward is not a wage but a share in his own joy. Luke tells a similar parable with its own accent: the minas (Luke 19:11–27).
Matthew 25:31–46ProphecyFuture
“Amen, I say to you, whatever you did for one of these least brothers of mine, you did for me.”
Reading
The Son of Man sits on his glorious throne and all the nations are gathered before him. The judgment turns on love shown or refused to the least (CCC 678, 1038–1041). Revelation's great white throne (20:11–15) shows the same scene.
For now
The future judgment is decided by present choices: every act of mercy is a stone in the building.
Matthew 26:63–64ProphecyFulfilledFuture
“From now on you will see ‘the Son of Man seated at the right hand of the Power’ and ‘coming on the clouds of heaven.’”
Reading
The high priest puts Jesus under oath: “I order you to tell us under oath before the living God whether you are the Messiah, the Son of God” (26:63). Jesus answers, “You have said so,” and joins Psalm 110:1 to Daniel 7:13. Mark's account does not have the words “from now on”; with them Matthew shows the exaltation beginning in the Passion itself. The Catechism teaches that being seated at the Father's right hand signifies the inauguration of the Messiah's kingdom, the fulfillment of Daniel's vision of the Son of Man (CCC 664).
Why two labels
The reign at the right hand began with the Resurrection and Ascension. The coming on the clouds awaits his return (24:30). See the parallel at Mark 14:61–62.
Matthew 28:18–20PromiseUnfolding
“Go, therefore, and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the holy Spirit…”
Reading
On a mountain in Galilee the risen Jesus says, “All power in heaven and on earth has been given to me” (28:18), the dominion Daniel saw given to the Son of Man (Daniel 7:14). Then he sends the eleven to all nations. The Catechism finds here the Church's missionary mandate (CCC 849) and teaches that the kingdom, first announced to Israel, is intended for people of all nations (CCC 543).
Why unfolding
The command and the promise run together “until the end of the age” (28:20), the same horizon as 24:14, where the Gospel goes to all nations before the end comes. Matthew ends not with the return but with the presence: “And behold, I am with you always, until the end of the age.” Emmanuel, God with us (1:23).
