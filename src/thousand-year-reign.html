What Is the Thousand-Year Reign?

The thousand-year reign, or millennium, is the reign of Christ with his saints in Revelation 20. The Catholic Church, following St. Augustine, reads it as the present age of the Church, from Christ's first coming to his return. It is not a future earthly kingdom: that hope, called millenarianism, the Church has set aside.

The short answerRevelation 20 describes Satan bound for a thousand years while the faithful reign with Christ. The Church's common reading since St. Augustine is that this is now: the kingdom of Christ is “already present in mystery” in the Church (CCC 669), and the saints reign with him until he comes in glory. At the end Satan is loosed for a short time, the final trial comes, and Christ returns to judge the living and the dead. The Church does not expect a political reign of Christ on earth before that day (CCC 676).

What Scripture says

The only place the Bible speaks of a thousand-year reign is Revelation 20:1–10. An angel comes down with the key to the abyss. “He seized the dragon, the ancient serpent, which is the Devil or Satan, and tied it up for a thousand years” (Revelation 20:2). Then John sees thrones and the souls of those beheaded for their witness to Jesus, who had not worshiped the beast: “They came to life and they reigned with Christ for a thousand years” (Revelation 20:4). “Blessed and holy is the one who shares in the first resurrection. The second death has no power over these; they will be priests of God and of Christ, and they will reign with him for [the] thousand years” (Revelation 20:6).

Afterward Satan is released, gathers “Gog and Magog,” and surrounds “the camp of the holy ones and the beloved city,” until fire comes down from heaven and the Devil is thrown into the pool of fire (Revelation 20:7–10). Then comes the Last Judgment (Revelation 20:11–15; see the Revelation page). In Revelation numbers are images, and a thousand, ten times ten times ten, is a vast and complete span. The question is what the image means.

The three Christian views

Christians who love Scripture read Revelation 20 in three main ways.

ViewWhat it holdsWho holds it
Amillennial (“no literal millennium”)The thousand years are the age of the Church, between Christ's two comings. Satan is bound by Christ's victory; the saints reign with him now. One visible return closes history with the resurrection of the dead and the judgment.The common reading of the Catholic Church since St. Augustine, and of the Orthodox; also of many Lutherans, Reformed and Anglicans.
PremillennialChrist returns before the millennium and reigns on earth with the risen just for a thousand years, before the final judgment. Historic premillennialism is close to the hope of some early Fathers. Dispensational premillennialism, taught by John Nelson Darby and spread by the Scofield Reference Bible, adds a rapture of the Church before a seven-year tribulation and sees the millennium as the fulfillment of God's promises to the nation of Israel.Many evangelical and Pentecostal Christians; dispensationalism is widely known through popular books and films such as the Left Behind series.
PostmillennialChrist returns after the millennium, a long age in which the Gospel spreads and the world is largely Christianized. The thousand years are a golden age of the Church before the end.Many Puritans and nineteenth-century Protestants; some Reformed Christians today.

Each view grows from a real conviction: premillennialists take God's promises of a renewed earth seriously, and postmillennialists trust the Gospel's power to change the world. The Catholic reading keeps both truths but places their fulfillment where the Catechism does: the kingdom will come “not by a historic triumph of the Church through a progressive ascendancy, but only by God's victory over the final unleashing of evil,” at the Last Judgment, and then in the new heavens and new earth (CCC 677). What the Fathers said

Some of the earliest Christian writers were millenarians, or chiliasts (from the Greek for “thousand”). Papias of Hierapolis, “the hearer of John, and a companion of Polycarp,” handed on sayings about the fruitfulness of the coming kingdom, as St. Irenaeus reports (Against Heresies 5.33.4). St. Justin Martyr expected “a thousand years in Jerusalem, which will then be built, adorned, and enlarged,” yet he admitted that “many who belong to the pure and pious faith, and are true Christians, think otherwise” (Dialogue with Trypho 80). St. Irenaeus held that the just ought “first to receive the promise of the inheritance” in the very creation where they had suffered (5.32.1; see St. Irenaeus on the kingdom). St. Victorinus of Pettau held the same, and St. Jerome later revised his commentary to remove it (Victorinus on the thousand years).

These were saints and martyrs, but on this point they were not the voice of the whole Church, as Justin admits. St. Augustine once shared their hope: “for I myself, too, once held this opinion.” Reading Revelation with the Gospels and St. Paul, he came to see that John “used the thousand years as an equivalent for the whole duration of this world” (City of God 20.7). His conclusion became the common reading of the Church: “Therefore the Church even now is the kingdom of Christ, and the kingdom of heaven,” and “even now His saints reign with Him, though otherwise than as they shall reign hereafter” (20.9). The whole of Book XX is read on St. Augustine on the end.

What the Church teaches

The Catechism teaches that Christ already reigns through his Church: the kingdom of Christ is “already present in mystery,” and the Church is “on earth, the seed and the beginning of the kingdom” (CCC 669). The Church “is the Reign of Christ already present in mystery” (CCC 763).

That is why she does not wait for another, earthly kingdom before the end. Speaking of the deception that offers within history the hope that can only be fulfilled beyond it, the Catechism says that “the Church has rejected even modified forms of this falsification of the kingdom to come under the name of millenarianism” (CCC 676). Its note cites Denzinger (DS 3839), and in 1944 the Holy Office judged that even a mitigated millenarianism cannot safely be taught. The Church has not defined how the thousand years must be counted; what she has set aside is the expectation of an earthly messianic reign before the end.

Three phrases, read with the Church

Satan bound
Jesus described his own work this way: “How can anyone enter a strong man's house and steal his property, unless he first ties up the strong man?” (Matthew 12:29). Augustine read Revelation 20:2–3 in that light. The binding does not mean Satan does nothing; it means “his being prevented from the exercise of his whole power to seduce men” (City of God 20.8). He can tempt, but he cannot stop the Gospel from reaching the nations.
The first resurrection
“Amen, amen, I say to you, the hour is coming and is now here when the dead will hear the voice of the Son of God, and those who hear will live” (John 5:25). Augustine took the first resurrection as the soul's rising from sin to new life in Christ: “Now this resurrection regards not the body, but the soul” (20.6). Everyone baptized into Christ shares in it now; the resurrection of the body comes at the end (Two resurrections).
Gog and Magog
The names come from Ezekiel 38–39. Augustine refused to map them onto particular peoples: the camp they surround “is nothing else than the Church of Christ extending over the whole world” (20.11). The loosing of Satan is the final trial of the Church, which no one can date (CCC 675–677; see Who is the Antichrist? and What is the great apostasy?).

How to live it

If the thousand years are now, the reign of Christ is not something to wait for idly. It is lived in every Baptism, every Mass, every act of mercy, every martyr's witness, and we share it by prayer, by refusing the beast's “mark” in our own choices, and by loving our neighbor. The question is less “When will Christ reign?” than “Does he reign in me?”

A sober noteThis site sets no dates and calculates no timetable: “of that day and hour no one knows” (Matthew 24:36; CCC 673). It names no living person as a fulfillment of prophecy. Private revelations, even approved ones, do not add to the faith or reveal the calendar of the end (CCC 67). See also Are we in the end times?
