Prophecy in Romans

Romans is Paul's long look at the whole plan of God, from Adam to the end. It opens with a Gospel “promised previously through his prophets” and closes with the serpent crushed. In between, creation groans in labor and Israel's story is not over.

The book at a glance

Paul wrote to the Christians of Rome, a church he had not yet visited, probably from Corinth in the late 50s. It is the longest of his letters and the most carefully argued: all have sinned (chapters 1–3), all are justified by faith in Christ (3–5), the baptized live a new life in the Spirit (6–8), God remains faithful to Israel (9–11), and the Church lives that faith in love (12–16).

Romans is not a book of visions. It looks forward in Paul's own way: through promises he shows kept in Christ, through a great type, Adam as the figure of the one to come, and through a few clear words about what is still ahead: the freeing of creation, the salvation of “all Israel,” and the day that is “at hand.” Each card below names which.

The passages

Romans 1:1–4PromiseFulfilled
“…the gospel of God, which he promised previously through his prophets in the holy scriptures, the gospel about his Son, descended from David according to the flesh…”
Reading
Paul's first sentence is a key to the whole Bible. The Gospel is not a new idea; it is a promise kept. The Son of David foretold by the prophets (2 Samuel 7:12–16; Isaiah 11:1) is “established as Son of God in power” through his resurrection (1:4).
See also
Matthew makes the same claim in story form, opening with “the son of David, the son of Abraham” (Matthew 1:1). See Prophecy in Matthew.
Romans 1:16–17PromiseFulfilled
“For in it is revealed the righteousness of God from faith to faith; as it is written, ‘The one who is righteous by faith will live.’”
Reading
These two verses are the theme of the whole letter. The Gospel “is the power of God for the salvation of everyone who believes: for Jew first, and then Greek” (1:16), and Paul grounds it in a prophet. Habakkuk received his word while waiting for a vision that was slow to come: the righteous will live by faith (Habakkuk 2:4). Paul hears in it the way of life God has now opened in Christ to Jew and Gentile alike. The NABRE note describes this faith as itself a gift of the Holy Spirit, by which a person accepts salvation as God's righteousness.
Why fulfilled
What Habakkuk was told to wait for, Paul says, is now “revealed.” The same verse is quoted again in Galatians 3:11 and Hebrews 10:38, where it is joined to the promise that “he who is to come shall come.”
See also
Habakkuk 2:4 on this site; Hebrews 10:35–37.
Romans 2:5–16ProphecyFuture
“…on the day when, according to my gospel, God will judge people's hidden works through Christ Jesus.”
Reading
Paul warns against the heart that refuses to repent and so stores up wrath “for the day of wrath and revelation of the just judgment of God, who will repay everyone according to his works” (2:5–6): “eternal life to those who seek glory, honor, and immortality through perseverance in good works” (2:7). “There is no partiality with God” (2:11). Even Gentiles who never had the law of Moses show “that the demands of the law are written in their hearts” (2:15); the Catechism teaches on conscience from these verses (CCC 1777). And it echoes Paul on the judgment: when Christ comes to judge the living and the dead, he “will reveal the secret disposition of hearts and will render to each man according to his works, and according to his acceptance or refusal of grace” (CCC 682).
Why future
The day is still ahead. Its judge is named: Christ Jesus, the same one Paul preaches as Savior.
For now
Paul calls this judgment part of “my gospel.” The next chapters explain why: all have sinned, and all are justified freely by God's grace through Christ (3:23–24). The judgment is not a threat set beside the Gospel but the truth the Gospel answers.
Romans 4:16–18PromiseFulfilledUnfolding
“He believed, hoping against hope, that he would become ‘the father of many nations,’ according to what was said, ‘Thus shall your descendants be.’”
Reading
God promised Abraham descendants as many as the stars (Genesis 15:5) and changed his name because “I have made you father of many nations” (4:17; Genesis 17:5). Paul reads the promise as wider than one people. Abraham is “the father of all of us” (4:16), of all who share his faith, and the promise is “that he would inherit the world” (4:13). He believed in the God “who gives life to the dead and calls into being what does not exist” (4:17), the God of the Resurrection. The Catechism presents Abraham as the model of faith who, “strong in his faith,” became the “father of all who believe” (CCC 146).
Why two labels
The promise is kept in Christ, the offspring of Abraham (Galatians 3:16), and in the nations who come to faith in him. It is still being kept: “it was not for him alone that it was written,” Paul says, “it was also for us” (4:23–24), and every new believer adds a star to Abraham's sky.
See also
The blessing of the nations in Genesis; father of many nations.
Romans 5:12–19TypeFulfilled
Adam, “who is the type of the one who was to come.”
Reading
Paul sets two men side by side. Through the first, sin and death entered the world; through the second, grace overflows “for the many” (5:15). “For just as through the disobedience of one person the many were made sinners,” so through the obedience of one the many will be made righteous (5:19). The Church teaches original sin and the New Adam from this passage (CCC 402, 411).
Note
Adam is a type by contrast: Christ undoes what Adam did. The Christian tradition reads Genesis 3:15 in the same light, as an announcement of the New Adam and, with many Fathers, of Mary as the new Eve (CCC 410–411). See Prophecy in Genesis.
See also
1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45.
Romans 8:18–25ProphecyFuture
“For creation awaits with eager expectation the revelation of the children of God…”
Reading
Paul widens hope beyond the soul. Creation itself will “be set free from slavery to corruption” (8:21); meanwhile “all creation is groaning in labor pains even until now” (8:22), and so do we, awaiting the redemption of our bodies. The Catechism reads these verses of the “common destiny” of the world and man, and of the new heavens and new earth (CCC 1046).
Why future
The labor has begun, and the Spirit is already given as “firstfruits” (8:23), but the birth is still to come. Revelation shows its end: “Behold, I make all things new” (Revelation 21:5).
For now
“For in hope we were saved” (8:24). The sufferings of the present, Paul says, are “as nothing compared with the glory to be revealed for us” (8:18).
Romans 8:28–30PromiseUnfolding
“And those he predestined he also called; and those he called he also justified; and those he justified he also glorified.”
Reading
God works “all things” for good for those who love him, and his purpose is that they be “conformed to the image of his Son” (8:29). The Catechism opens its teaching on Christian holiness with these verses (CCC 2012). Paul writes “glorified” in the past tense, as if already done: so certain is the promise.
Why unfolding
The calling and justifying happen now, in every generation of the Church. The glory is pledged, but its fullness is still ahead (8:18).
Romans 11:1–24ImageUnfolding
“But if some of the branches were broken off, and you, a wild olive shoot, were grafted in their place and have come to share in the rich root of the olive tree, do not boast against the branches.”
Reading
“I ask, then, has God rejected his people? Of course not!” (11:1). Paul is himself an Israelite, and as in Elijah's day, “at the present time there is a remnant, chosen by grace” (11:5). Israel's stumbling is not a fall: “through their transgression salvation has come to the Gentiles, so as to make them jealous” (11:11). Then the image. Israel is a cultivated olive tree, as the prophets had called it (Jeremiah 11:16). Some branches have been broken off and wild shoots grafted in, but “the root supports you” (11:18), and the broken branches “will be grafted in, for God is able to graft them in again” (11:23). The Catechism calls Abraham's people “the root on to which the Gentiles would be grafted, once they came to believe” (CCC 60), and among the images of the Church names the ancient olive tree “whose holy roots were the prophets” (CCC 755).
Why unfolding
The grafting of the Gentiles goes on in every baptism. The regrafting of the natural branches Paul calls a “mystery” still to come; see the next card.
For now
The image forbids contempt. The Second Vatican Council takes it up to say that the Church “draws sustenance from the root of that well-cultivated olive tree” onto which the Gentiles have been grafted (Nostra Aetate 4). A Christian who boasts against the Jewish people has forgotten what holds him up.
Romans 9–11; 11:25–27ProphecyFutureMystery
“…a hardening has come upon Israel in part, until the full number of the Gentiles comes in, and thus all Israel will be saved…”
Reading
Chapters 9–11 are Paul's anguish and hope for his own people. He begins with grief (9:1–5), insists that “God has not rejected his people whom he foreknew” (11:2), and ends with a “mystery”: Israel's partial hardening lasts until the Gentiles come in, and then “all Israel will be saved,” for “the gifts and the call of God are irrevocable” (11:29). He asks, “what will their acceptance be but life from the dead?” (11:15).
Church teaching
The Catechism links this passage to Christ's return: his coming is suspended until his recognition by “all Israel,” and the “full inclusion” of the Jews will follow “the full number of the Gentiles” (CCC 674, citing 11:12, 15, 25). The Church honors the Jewish people as “the first to hear the Word of God” (CCC 839, citing 9:4–5 and 11:29), and the Second Vatican Council, citing 11:28–29, teaches that God holds them most dear (Nostra Aetate 4).
Why two labels
That “all Israel will be saved” is taught as still to come. How and when it happens is not settled. Interpreters also differ on whom “all Israel” means: most read it as the Jewish people as a whole, while others, including some Fathers, read it as the whole people of God, Jew and Gentile together. Paul himself ends in wonder: “How inscrutable are his judgments and how unsearchable his ways!” (11:33). This page sets no timetable for it.
Romans 13:11–12ProphecyUnfolding
“For our salvation is nearer now than when we first believed; the night is advanced, the day is at hand.”
Reading
Paul calls the Church to wake up, throw off the works of darkness and “put on the armor of light.” Since the Ascension the world has entered “the last hour” (CCC 670): Christ's coming is near at every moment, though no one knows when (CCC 673).
Why unfolding
Every generation of Christians lives in this “advanced” night. The verse is not a countdown but a call to live now as people of the day.
Romans 15:8–13ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“The root of Jesse shall come, raised up to rule the Gentiles; in him shall the Gentiles hope.”
Reading
Near the end of the letter Paul sums up his argument: “Christ became a minister of the circumcised to show God's truthfulness, to confirm the promises to the patriarchs, but so that the Gentiles might glorify God for his mercy” (15:8–9). He proves it with four texts drawn from the Law, the Prophets and the Psalms (Psalm 18:50; Deuteronomy 32:43; Psalm 117:1; Isaiah 11:10), and ends with Isaiah's shoot from the stump of Jesse, David's father. The Messiah of Israel is the hope of the nations.
Why two labels
The root of Jesse has come and been “raised up,” and the Gentiles have begun to hope in him. Revelation still calls him “the root of David” (Revelation 5:5; compare 22:16), and the praise of the nations is not yet complete.
For now
Paul turns the prophecy into a prayer: “May the God of hope fill you with all joy and peace in believing, so that you may abound in hope by the power of the holy Spirit” (15:13).
See also
The shoot from the stump of Jesse in Isaiah.
Romans 16:20PromiseUnfoldingFuture
“…then the God of peace will quickly crush Satan under your feet.”
Reading
In his closing greetings Paul echoes the first promise in the Bible, the offspring of the woman who strikes the serpent's head (Genesis 3:15; the NABRE gives it as a cross-reference here). The striking is done by God, but it happens “under your feet”: the Church shares in Christ's victory.
Why two labels
The serpent is already defeated at the Cross, and his defeat goes on in the life of the Church (Luke 10:19). The final crushing is still ahead, when the devil is thrown into the pool of fire (Revelation 20:10).
See also
Prophecy in Genesis; Revelation 12.
Romans 16:25–27PromiseFulfilledUnfolding
“…according to the revelation of the mystery kept secret for long ages but now manifested through the prophetic writings and, according to the command of the eternal God, made known to all nations to bring about the obedience of faith…”
Reading
The letter's last sentence returns to its first. Romans began with a Gospel “promised previously through his prophets” (1:2) and ends with a mystery hidden for ages and now opened “through the prophetic writings.” The mystery is God's plan in Christ, embracing Jews and Gentiles in one people (Ephesians 3:4–6; Colossians 1:26). The Catechism takes from these verses and from 1:5 the phrase “the obedience of faith,” the whole person's assent to God who reveals (CCC 143).
Note
The manuscripts place this doxology after chapter 14, 15 or 16, and some omit it, so the NABRE prints it in brackets. Its note judges that, whoever composed it, it is a fitting close for the letter here. The Church receives it as Scripture.
Why two labels
The mystery is already “manifested.” Its being “made known to all nations” is the Church's mission, still under way.
