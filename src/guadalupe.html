Pillar II · Apparitions · Mexico, 1531

Our Lady of Guadalupe

Ten years after the fall of Tenochtitlan, on a hill outside what is now Mexico City, a Lady spoke to a poor Indigenous man in his own language and called him her littlest son. She asked for a church, and when the bishop asked for a sign, she left her image on his cloak.

At a glance

PlaceThe hill of Tepeyac, north of Mexico City, Mexico
DatesFour apparitions to Juan Diego, December 9–12, 1531, and one to his uncle, Juan Bernardino, who was healed. The image appeared on Juan Diego's tilma (cloak) on December 12, before the bishop-elect of Mexico, Fray Juan de Zumárraga.
SeerJuan Diego, a Nahua layman and recent convert, then in his fifties. He spent the rest of his life beside the chapel at Tepeyac and died in 1548. St. John Paul II beatified him at the Basilica on May 6, 1990, and canonized him there on July 31, 2002.
Church judgmentNo episcopal decree of the modern kind exists. The Church received Guadalupe by stages: a canonical inquiry in Mexico in 1665–1666; Pope Benedict XIV's bull Non est equidem (May 25, 1754), naming her patroness of New Spain, with a proper Mass and Office for December 12; the canonical coronation of the image, decreed by Leo XIII and carried out on October 12, 1895; and the canonization of Juan Diego in 2002. Pius XII hailed her as “Empress of America and Queen of Mexico” (radio message, October 12, 1945; our translation). Later popes honored her as patroness of Latin America and of all the Americas. All of this came long before the 2024 norms.
FeastDecember 12, Our Lady of Guadalupe (optional memorial in the universal calendar; a feast in the United States; in Ecclesia in America 11 St. John Paul II welcomed the Synod Fathers' proposal that it be celebrated throughout the continent). St. Juan Diego, December 9.
PilgrimageThe Basilica of Our Lady of Guadalupe at the foot of Tepeyac, where the tilma hangs today, one of the most visited Marian shrines in the world.
WeightPrivate revelation received by the Church: Catholics may accept it with prudent devotion but are not bound to believe it (CCC 67).

What happened

The story is told in the Nican Mopohua (“Here it is told”), a Nahuatl account traditionally attributed to the Indigenous scholar Antonio Valeriano and first printed in 1649 by Luis Laso de la Vega, the vicar of Tepeyac. Early on Saturday, December 9, 1531, Juan Diego was walking to Mass when he heard birdsong on Tepeyac and a voice calling him by name. At the top of the hill he met a young Lady, radiant as the sun, who said she was the ever-virgin Mary, Mother of the true God, and asked him to go to the bishop and ask that a church be built there.

Fray Juan de Zumárraga listened kindly but did not believe. Juan Diego went back and begged the Lady to send someone more important, calling himself, in Pope Francis's quotation of the account (Nican Mopohua 55), a “leather strap, a back frame, a tail, a wing, oppressed by another's burden.” She sent him again. The bishop asked for a sign. Then Juan Diego's uncle fell gravely ill, and on December 12 he went around the hill to avoid her, hurrying for a priest. She met him on the road anyway, told him his uncle was already healed, and sent him to the hilltop, where he found flowers blooming in December. She arranged them in his tilma herself. When he opened it before the bishop, the flowers fell to the floor, and on the cloth was her image. Juan Bernardino, healed that same day, said the Lady had appeared to him too and had given the name by which her image should be known: Holy Mary of Guadalupe.

The bishop built a chapel at Tepeyac. In the years that followed, the Gospel spread among the peoples of Mexico with a speed the missionaries had not known before. St. John Paul II saw in Guadalupe's mestiza face “a perfectly inculturated evangelization” (Ecclesia in America 11).

What Our Lady asked

Guadalupe has no long list of messages. Its heart is a mother's tenderness toward a man who thought himself of no account.

A house for her Son

She asked for a church at Tepeyac, where she would show her love, compassion, help and protection to all who came to her. (Paraphrased from the Nican Mopohua.)

Go to the bishop

She did not bypass the Church. She sent Juan Diego to the bishop again and again, and gave the sign for the bishop's sake.

Trust a mother's care

When he was afraid for his uncle, she asked: “What, my most precious little one, saddens your heart?” (Nican Mopohua 107).

Do not be afraid

“Yet am I not here with you, who have the honour of being your mother?” (Nican Mopohua 119). Her words come before any sign: first she comforts, then she heals.

The quotations above are from the Nican Mopohua as cited, with verse numbers, in the Vatican's English text of Pope Francis's homily at the Basilica of Guadalupe (February 13, 2016). Other lines are paraphrased.

The image on the tilma

The image is not a portrait for admirers but a message for a people who read pictures. Catholic commentators have long noted how much it says in the language of the Nahua world, while the Church has never tied her acceptance of Guadalupe to any single reading of its details.

The sun, the moon and the stars
What it shows
The Lady stands before a blaze of golden rays, on a dark crescent moon held up by an angel. Her blue-green mantle is scattered with gold stars.
Reading
She is greater than the sun and moon her viewers had honored, yet her head is bowed and her hands are joined in prayer. She is not a goddess. She worships someone greater than herself.
The black sash
What it shows
A dark ribbon is tied high above her waist, and over her womb is a small four-petaled flower.
Reading
The sash is widely read as “an indication of pregnancy in the native culture,” as the Catholic News Agency puts it. The Lady of Tepeyac is with child. The one she carries, not herself, is the center of the picture. This is why she is invoked as patroness of the unborn.
The face
What it shows
A young woman's face, neither European nor wholly Indigenous, looking down with gentleness.
Reading
She came as a mother to both peoples at a time of conquest and grief. Pius XII spoke of the image as left by “brushes that were not from here below” (radio message, October 12, 1945; our translation from the Spanish).

Guadalupe and the Book of Revelation

Guadalupe does not interpret Scripture for us. It echoes it, and the echo is unmistakable.

  • The woman clothed with the sun. “A great sign appeared in the sky, a woman clothed with the sun, with the moon under her feet, and on her head a crown of twelve stars” (Revelation 12:1). At Tepeyac the rays surround her, the moon is beneath her, and the stars are on her mantle.
  • With child. “She was with child” (Revelation 12:2). The sash says the same thing in another language: the woman is about to give the world her Son.
  • The child at the center. In Revelation everything turns on the son “destined to rule all the nations” (Revelation 12:5). At Guadalupe the small flower over her womb draws the eye to him.
  • The woman of Genesis. The enmity between the woman and the serpent (Genesis 3:15) is the ancient struggle Revelation 12 shows at its height. At Tepeyac the woman comes not with a sword but with a child, and the victory over the serpent begins, as it always does, with the Son she carries.
  • All ages shall call her blessed. “Behold, from now on will all ages call me blessed” (Luke 1:48). A continent has done so for five centuries.
Read with careMany claims circulate about the tilma: figures said to be reflected in her eyes, star patterns said to match the sky of December 1531, a steady body temperature, a name said to mean “she who crushes the serpent.” The Church has made no judgment on these, and her reception of Guadalupe does not rest on them. Some historians have also questioned the late date of the written accounts and even whether Juan Diego existed. Before his canonization, a historical commission working with the Congregation for the Causes of Saints studied the question and presented its findings in Rome in 1998; the Church then proceeded to canonize him. Guadalupe sets no dates and makes no forecasts. It asks for trust.

For reflection

Where do I feel too small to be sent?

Juan Diego asked to be replaced by someone more important. Our Lady chose him anyway, and sent him back. Where is God asking me to go again, trusting that I am not alone?
