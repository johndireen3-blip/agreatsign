The New Testament · The Letters of St. Paul · Book 64 of 73

Philemon

Paul's shortest letter, a single chapter about one man, Onesimus. It contains no prophecy, and this page says so plainly. What it does show is the Gospel at work inside one household.

Read Philemon (NABRE)

Home › The Bible › Philemon

The book at a glance

Paul writes from prison (verse 1) to Philemon, a Christian in whose house a church met (verse 2). Onesimus, a slave of Philemon's, has come to Paul and been converted: “my child Onesimus, whose father I have become in my imprisonment” (verse 10). Paul sends him back with this letter, asking Philemon to receive him “no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a brother” (verse 16). Its authorship by Paul is not disputed.

Why no prophecy

Philemon has no oracle, no vision, no promise about the last things and no type that the New Testament or the Fathers develop. This site does not read prophecy into a text that does not claim it, so this page has no passage cards.

What it shows of the Church being built

The letter still belongs to the story told elsewhere on this site. Ephesians speaks of strangers made “members of the household of God” (Ephesians 2:19); Philemon shows that happening in one real household. Paul asks Philemon to take back Onesimus as “a brother, beloved especially to me, but even more so to you, as a man and in the Lord” (verse 16). Elsewhere he writes that in Christ “there is neither slave nor free person” (Galatians 3:28).

Paul hints that God may have been at work in the separation itself: “Perhaps this is why he was away from you for a while, that you might have him back forever” (verse 15). That is not a prophecy. It is a reading of providence, offered tentatively by Paul himself with a “perhaps.”

The Catechism draws on this letter in its teaching on the seventh commandment, which forbids acts that lead to the enslavement of human beings, or to their being bought, sold and exchanged like merchandise, in disregard for their personal dignity. There it recalls that St. Paul directed a Christian master to treat his Christian slave “no longer as a slave but more than a slave, as a beloved brother” (CCC 2414, citing verse 16 in a different translation).
