Prophecy in James

A letter of practical wisdom: faith that works, a tongue held in check, the poor honored. Under its plain counsel runs a steady sense of the end. The crown of life is promised, the rich are warned, and the Church is told to be patient like a farmer, because “the Judge is standing before the gates.”

The book at a glance

The letter comes from “James, a slave of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ” to “the twelve tribes in the dispersion” (1:1). Tradition identifies him with James, “the brother of the Lord” (Galatians 1:19), who led the church in Jerusalem (Acts 15:13–21). Many modern scholars think the letter was written later by someone writing in his name; others defend his authorship. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture either way, and it is the first of the seven Catholic Letters, so called because most are addressed to the Church at large.

James is a book of wisdom more than of prophecy. Its forward look is mostly promise and warning: a reward for those who endure, judgment for those who exploit, and the Lord's coming as the reason for patience now. One passage, the anointing of the sick, is included here because it is a promise the Church still sees kept in a sacrament.

The passages

James 1:12PromiseFuture
“Blessed is the man who perseveres in temptation, for when he has been proved he will receive the crown of life that he promised to those who love him.”
Reading
The word for temptation is the same as the “trials” of 1:2, as the NABRE note observes, and the crown is the wreath given in the ancient world to victors and honored citizens. “Life” here is eternal life. The promise is made not to the strong but “to those who love him.” Perseverance is love that holds on.
Why future
The crown is given “when he has been proved,” at the end of the testing. The risen Christ makes the same promise to the church at Smyrna: “Remain faithful until death, and I will give you the crown of life” (Revelation 2:10).
See also
1 Peter 5:4; 2 Timothy 4:8.
James 5:1–6ProphecyFutureUnfolding
“Come now, you rich, weep and wail over your impending miseries.”
Reading
James speaks in the voice of the old prophets against those who grow rich by injustice. Their wealth is already rotting: “your gold and silver have corroded,” and the corrosion “will devour your flesh like a fire. You have stored up treasure for the last days” (5:3). The withheld wages of the laborers cry out, and their cries “have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts” (5:4). The irony is sharp: they have been saving, but what they have saved is evidence against themselves.
Why two labels
The judgment belongs to the last days. Yet the prophet's point is that the decay has already begun, and the cry of the poor is heard now. Jesus gives the same warning in the parable of the rich fool (Luke 12:16–21).
For now
This is not a curse on the rich as a class but a call to conversion while there is time. The Church teaches the same in plain words: to refuse or withhold a just wage “can be a grave injustice” (CCC 2434).
James 5:7–9ProphecyFuture
“You too must be patient. Make your hearts firm, because the coming of the Lord is at hand.”
Reading
To people oppressed by the unjust rich, James gives the image of the farmer who “waits for the precious fruit of the earth, being patient with it until it receives the early and the late rains” (5:7). The harvest cannot be hurried, but it is certain. Then a warning turned inward: “Do not complain, brothers, about one another, that you may not be judged. Behold, the Judge is standing before the gates” (5:9). Jesus used the same picture: “know that he is near, at the gates” (Matthew 24:33).
Note
“At hand” is not a date. The Catechism teaches that since the Ascension Christ's coming in glory could happen at any moment, while its time is known to the Father alone (CCC 673; Acts 1:7). Every generation of Christians has been told to live as if the Judge were at the door, because in one sense he always is.
For now
The nearness of the Judge is a reason for patience with suffering and gentleness with one another, not for anxiety.
James 5:14–15PromiseUnfolding
“…and the prayer of faith will save the sick person, and the Lord will raise him up. If he has committed any sins, he will be forgiven.”
Reading
“Is anyone among you sick? He should summon the presbyters of the church, and they should pray over him and anoint [him] with oil in the name of the Lord” (5:14). This is not a prediction but a promise attached to an action of the Church: the Lord himself will save, raise up and forgive. The Twelve had already anointed the sick on their mission (Mark 6:13).
Why it is here
It is included because it is a promise the Church still sees kept. The Catechism teaches that the apostolic Church had its own rite for the sick, attested by St. James in these verses, and that Tradition has recognized it as one of the seven sacraments (CCC 1510). The NABRE note recalls that the Council of Trent (Session 14) declared this anointing a sacrament instituted by Christ and promulgated by James. The Catechism also calls it a preparation for the final journey, completing our conformity to Christ's death and Resurrection (CCC 1523).
Why unfolding
The promise is kept wherever the sick are anointed. Its last word, “the Lord will raise him up,” reaches beyond bodily healing toward the resurrection.
