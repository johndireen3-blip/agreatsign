Prophecy in Ecclesiastes

“Vanity of vanities, says Qoheleth, all things are vanity!” (12:8) Ecclesiastes holds no prophecy, and this page says so plainly. What it does is name, with unflinching honesty, the emptiness of life “under the sun.” In doing so it prepares the heart for a hope only God can give.

The book at a glance

The book presents itself as “The words of David's son, Qoheleth, king in Jerusalem” (1:1). Qoheleth means something like “one who gathers an assembly”; the Greek Ecclesiastes translates it. Tradition linked the book with Solomon, but most scholars, Catholic scholars among them, read the royal voice as a literary device and date the book late, around the third century before Christ, from its Hebrew and its ideas. A brief epilogue (12:9–14), probably from a disciple, praises the master and sums up his teaching. The Church receives the whole as inspired Scripture.

Ecclesiastes is not prophecy. It has no oracle, no promise of a Messiah and no vision of the end, and this site does not read prediction into it. It is included here because it does something prophecy needs: it tells the truth about the world without God's final answer. It looks at toil, pleasure, wisdom and death “under the sun” and finds them all a breath. The New Testament takes up that honesty (Romans 8:20) and answers it with hope. The passages

Ecclesiastes 1:2–14ImageUnfoldingFuture
“Vanity of vanities, says Qoheleth, vanity of vanities! All things are vanity!” (1:2)
Reading
The Hebrew word, hebel, means breath or vapor. The NABRE's note calls “vanity of vanities” a Hebrew superlative expressing the supreme degree of futility and emptiness. Qoheleth asks, “What profit have we from all the toil which we toil at under the sun?” (1:3), and concludes, “Nothing is new under the sun!” (1:9). The book ends where it began (12:8).
Unfolding
St. Paul names the same futility and gives it a reason and a horizon: “creation was made subject to futility, not of its own accord but because of the one who subjected it, in hope that creation itself would be set free from slavery to corruption” (Romans 8:20–21). Qoheleth sees the futility; Paul sees that it is not the last word. Creation “awaits with eager expectation the revelation of the children of God” (Romans 8:19).
Future
Against “nothing is new under the sun” stands the last promise of the Bible: “Behold, I make all things new” (Revelation 21:5).
Ecclesiastes 3:1–8ImageFulfilled
“There is an appointed time for everything, and a time for every affair under the heavens.” (3:1)
Reading
Fourteen pairs follow: “A time to give birth, and a time to die” (3:2), “A time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance” (3:4), down to “a time of war, and a time of peace” (3:8). In its first sense the poem is about human limits: we do not set the seasons of our lives, and we cannot hold any of them.
Fulfilled
The New Testament speaks of a time God himself appointed: “when the fullness of time had come, God sent his Son, born of a woman” (Galatians 4:4). Jesus begins his preaching, “This is the time of fulfillment” (Mark 1:15). The times Qoheleth could only list are gathered into God's plan in Christ. This is a reading in the light of the Gospel, not a prophecy the poem makes.
Note
Of the final time, the Lord's return, Scripture is equally clear that no one knows the day or hour (Matthew 24:36). Qoheleth's humility before the seasons is a good guard against date-setting.
Ecclesiastes 3:11ImageUnfolding
“God has made everything appropriate to its time, but has put the timeless into their hearts so they cannot find out, from beginning to end, the work which God has done.”
Reading
The NABRE translates “the timeless,” and its note says others translate “eternity,” “the world” or “darkness”: the Hebrew is uncertain. In Qoheleth's own voice the verse is partly a complaint: God has given us a sense of the whole, yet hidden his work from us.
Unfolding
Read with the rest of Scripture, the verse names the desire for God written in the human heart. The Catechism teaches that this desire is written there because we are created by God and for God, and that God never ceases to draw us to himself (CCC 27). St. Augustine prayed, at the opening of his Confessions (1.1), that our heart is restless until it rests in God. Qoheleth's ache for what lies beyond time is answered in the one who gives eternal life (John 6:51).
Ecclesiastes 12:13–14PromiseFuture
“The last word, when all is heard: Fear God and keep his commandments, for this concerns all humankind; because God will bring to judgment every work, with all its hidden qualities, whether good or bad.”
Reading
The epilogue gathers the book into one line. After all its doubts, Ecclesiastes ends not in despair but with God, who sees everything, even what is hidden, and will judge it. Just before, Qoheleth describes death: “the dust returns to the earth as it once was, and the life breath returns to God who gave it” (12:7).
Future
The New Testament says who the judge will be: “we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each one may receive recompense, according to what he did in the body, whether good or evil” (2 Corinthians 5:10). The Catechism teaches that at the Last Judgment the truth of each person's relationship with God will be laid bare (CCC 1039). For those who love him, that is not a threat but a promise: nothing done in love is vanity, because God sees it.
See also
Matthew 25:31–46; Revelation 20:12.

Why this book belongs on a site about prophecy

Ecclesiastes says what the world looks like when we see only what is “under the sun.” It does not pretend. The prophets and the Gospel speak of a God who comes from above the sun, who enters time and makes the breath of human life into something that lasts. Qoheleth's honesty makes the hope sharper: the emptiness he names is a space that only God can fill.
