Prophecy in Ruth

After the darkness of Judges, a short story of faithfulness. A foreign widow follows her mother-in-law to Bethlehem, a kinsman redeems her, and their son becomes the grandfather of David. Ruth holds no prophecy, but it ends with a genealogy that leads, in Matthew's Gospel, to Jesus.

The book at a glance

Ruth is set “back in the time of the judges” (1:1). Famine drives Elimelech of Bethlehem, his wife Naomi and their two sons to Moab, where the sons marry Moabite women and all three men die. Naomi returns home with one daughter-in-law, Ruth, who gleans in the fields of Boaz, a relative; Boaz takes up the duty of redeemer, marries her, and their son Obed is the grandfather of David (chapters 1–4). The author is unknown. Jewish tradition ascribed the book to Samuel; scholars date it variously, from the early monarchy to after the exile, and some read it as a gentle answer to the laws that excluded foreigners. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture either way. In Jewish practice it is read at the feast of Weeks, Pentecost.

Ruth is a story, not a book of prophecy, and nothing in it predicts. It looks forward through types, above all Boaz the redeemer, which is a traditional Christian reading rather than the New Testament's own, and through a promise that runs underneath the story: the line that leads to David, and through David to Christ. Matthew names Ruth in the genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:5). Each card below names which kind of passage it is.

The passages

Ruth 1:16–17; 2:10–12TypeFulfilledUnfolding
“Wherever you go I will go, wherever you lodge I will lodge. Your people shall be my people and your God, my God.” (1:16)
Reading
Naomi urges her widowed daughters-in-law to go home. Orpah does; Ruth will not, and binds herself to Naomi, to Israel and to Israel's God even past death: “Where you die I will die, and there be buried” (1:17). She is a Moabite, and the Law said, “No Ammonite or Moabite may ever come into the assembly of the LORD” (Deuteronomy 23:4). In Bethlehem she asks Boaz, “Why should I, a foreigner, be favored with your attention?” (2:10), and he blesses her by the God of Israel, “under whose wings you have come for refuge” (2:12).
Fulfilled
The Fathers, St. Ambrose among them in his Commentary on Luke, saw in Ruth the Church gathered from the nations: a stranger who leaves her own land for love and is welcomed into the people of God. St. Paul says the same of the Gentiles in Christ, who are “members of the household of God” (Ephesians 2:19).
Unfolding
The welcome of the foreigner continues as the Gospel reaches every people, until the great multitude “from every nation, race, people, and tongue” stands before the Lamb (Revelation 7:9).
Ruth 3–4:10TypeFulfilled
“I am your servant Ruth. Spread the wing of your cloak over your servant, for you are a redeemer.” (3:9)
Reading
The Hebrew word is go'el, the near kinsman whose duty was to buy back a relative's land, free a relative from slavery, and keep a dead man's name alive in Israel (Leviticus 25:25, 47–49; Deuteronomy 25:5–6). Ruth asks Boaz to spread over her the “wing” of his cloak, the same word as the “wings” of God under which she had taken refuge (2:12). A closer kinsman has the first right, but declines because of the cost: “I cannot exercise my right of redemption for that would endanger my own estate. You do it in my place, for I cannot” (4:6). Boaz pays it freely, buys back Elimelech's land, and takes Ruth as his wife “so that the name of the deceased may not perish from his people” (4:10).
Fulfilled
This is a traditional Christian reading rather than one the New Testament spells out. Christ is the kinsman who became one of us so that he could redeem us, paying what no one else could pay: “God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law, to ransom those under the law, so that we might receive adoption” (Galatians 4:4–5). As Boaz takes the foreign widow as his bride, Christ takes the Church from the nations as his own, and Revelation ends with the wedding of the Lamb (Revelation 19:7).
Ruth 4:13–22PromiseFulfilled
“They named him Obed. He was the father of Jesse, the father of David.” (4:17)
Reading
When the child is born, the women of Bethlehem say to Naomi, “Blessed is the LORD who has not failed to provide you today with a redeemer” (4:14). The book closes with a genealogy from Perez, son of Judah, to David (4:18–22), and so the quiet story of one family turns out to be the story of Israel's royal line. The scepter promised to Judah (Genesis 49:10) passes through a Moabite woman. In the books of Samuel, God will promise David a house and a throne forever (2 Samuel 7).
Fulfilled
Matthew copies the line into the genealogy of Jesus: “Salmon the father of Boaz, whose mother was Rahab. Boaz became the father of Obed, whose mother was Ruth” (Matthew 1:5). Matthew names only four women before Mary: Tamar, Rahab, Ruth and the wife of Uriah, each with an irregular or foreign story. The Messiah's family tree already holds sinners and strangers, a sign of the mercy he comes to bring.

Ruth 1:1, 22; 4:11ImageFulfilled
“Prosper in Ephrathah! Bestow a name in Bethlehem!” (4:11)
Reading
The story begins with a famine in Bethlehem, whose name means “house of bread,” and turns when Naomi and Ruth arrive there “at the beginning of the barley harvest” (1:22). At the gate the elders bless Boaz and Ruth with a wish for Ephrathah and Bethlehem. The prophet Micah later names the same town as the birthplace of the coming ruler: “But you, Bethlehem-Ephrathah least among the clans of Judah, From you shall come forth for me one who is to be ruler in Israel” (Micah 5:1).
Fulfilled
Joseph goes up “to the city of David that is called Bethlehem, because he was of the house and family of David” (Luke 2:4), and there the angels announce “a savior … who is Messiah and Lord” (Luke 2:11). The house of bread becomes the birthplace of the one who says, “I am the bread of life” (John 6:35). The name the elders hoped for in Bethlehem was given there, far beyond what they asked.
