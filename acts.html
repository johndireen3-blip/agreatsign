Prophecy in Acts

Acts is the book of the Church's first steps between the Ascension and the return. It opens with a promise that Jesus will come back, shows Joel's prophecy fulfilled at Pentecost, and records Christian prophets whose words came true within the story itself.

The book at a glance

Tradition attributes Acts to Luke, the companion of Paul, as the second volume of his Gospel; both are addressed to Theophilus (Luke 1:3; Acts 1:1). It follows the Gospel from Jerusalem outward: the Ascension and Pentecost (chapters 1–2), the Church in Jerusalem and Judea under Peter (chapters 3–12), and Paul's missions, arrest and journey to Rome (chapters 13–28). The book ends with Paul in Rome, proclaiming the kingdom of God “without hindrance” (28:31).

Acts looks forward in three ways. It shows Old Testament prophecy fulfilled in the Church, above all Joel at Pentecost and Amos at the Council of Jerusalem. It records the promise of Christ's return and of the restoration of all things. And it shows the gift of prophecy at work: Agabus, Paul and an angel foretell what then happens. Many of these passages are fulfilled inside the book; others are still being fulfilled while the Gospel goes to “the ends of the earth.”

The passages

Acts 1:6–7ProphecyFuture
“It is not for you to know the times or seasons that the Father has established by his own authority.”
Reading
The disciples ask whether the kingdom will now be restored to Israel. Jesus does not deny that the kingdom will come; he refuses to give its date. The Catechism uses these words to teach that Christ's return is always imminent, yet no one may know when it will be (CCC 673).
Note
This is Matthew 24:36 again, at the very start of the Church's life, and the reason this site sets no dates.
Acts 1:8PromiseUnfolding
“But you will receive power when the holy Spirit comes upon you, and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, throughout Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth.”
Reading
Instead of a date, Jesus gives a task, and the verse is the outline of the whole book: Jerusalem (chapters 1–7), Judea and Samaria (chapter 8), then the nations, until Paul reaches Rome. The Church receives this as her missionary mandate to all peoples (CCC 849).
Why unfolding
The power came at Pentecost. The witness “to the ends of the earth” is still under way.
See also
Matthew 24:14; 28:19–20.
Acts 1:9–11PromiseFuture
“This Jesus who has been taken up from you into heaven will return in the same way as you have seen him going into heaven.”
Reading
Jesus is lifted up and “a cloud took him from their sight” (1:9). Two men in white tell the apostles to stop looking at the sky: he will come back as truly as he went. The Catechism cites this verse for Christ's return in glory (CCC 665), the return professed in the Creed.
Note
“In the same way” is read by the Church as a real, personal and visible return, not a hidden one. The cloud recalls Daniel 7:13 and Jesus' own words in Matthew 24:30.
Acts 2:16–21ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“‘It will come to pass in the last days,’ God says, ‘that I will pour out a portion of my spirit upon all flesh.’”
Reading
Peter explains Pentecost with Joel 3:1–5: “this is what was spoken through the prophet Joel” (2:16). Where Joel had said only “It shall come to pass,” Peter says “It will come to pass in the last days.” The Catechism teaches that Christ's Passover is completed in the outpouring of the Spirit (CCC 731), and that from that day the world has entered the “last days,” the time of the Church (CCC 732).
Why two labels
Fulfilled at Pentecost. The promise is “to you and to your children and to all those far off” (2:39), so the outpouring goes on, and “everyone shall be saved who calls on the name of the Lord” (2:21). Joel's signs in the sun and moon (2:19–20) Peter leaves without a date.
Acts 2:25–36ProphecyFulfilled
“Therefore let the whole house of Israel know for certain that God has made him both Lord and Messiah, this Jesus whom you crucified.”
Reading
In the same sermon Peter reads two psalms of David as speaking of Christ. In Psalm 16 David sings, “you will not abandon my soul to the netherworld, nor will you suffer your holy one to see corruption” (2:27). Yet David “died and was buried, and his tomb is in our midst to this day” (2:29). So, Peter says, David spoke as a prophet: “he foresaw and spoke of the resurrection of the Messiah” (2:31). Then Psalm 110: “The Lord said to my Lord, ‘Sit at my right hand until I make your enemies your footstool’” (2:34–35). David did not go up into heaven; his Son did. The Catechism cites these verses for Christ's body preserved from corruption (CCC 627), for his messianic kingship proclaimed after the Resurrection (CCC 440), and among the texts where Psalm 110 shows Jesus as Lord (CCC 447).
Why fulfilled
“God raised this Jesus; of this we are all witnesses” (2:32). The psalms are fulfilled in the Resurrection and the Ascension. The “until” of Psalm 110 is still running; see the Son seated until his enemies are made his footstool.
See also
Paul makes the same argument at Antioch: David “did see corruption,” but “the one whom God raised up did not see corruption” (13:36–37). Psalm 16 and Psalm 110 on this site.
Acts 3:19–21PromiseFuture
“…the Messiah already appointed for you, Jesus, whom heaven must receive until the times of universal restoration of which God spoke through the mouth of his holy prophets from of old.”
Reading
In the Temple, Peter calls his hearers to repent “that your sins may be wiped away” (3:19), so that the Lord may send “times of refreshment” and the Messiah. Christ remains in heaven until the restoration of all things. The Catechism reads this sermon as part of its teaching that the glorious coming of the Messiah awaits the recognition of Jesus by “all Israel” (CCC 674; Romans 11:25–26).
Note
The NABRE notes call the “times of refreshment” an apocalyptic name for the messianic age, and read the “universal restoration” in the same light. The Church does not set its timing; she keeps it as hope.
Acts 3:22–23ProphecyFulfilled
“A prophet like me will the Lord, your God, raise up for you from among your own kinsmen; to him you shall listen in all that he may say to you.”
Reading
Continuing his Temple sermon, Peter quotes the promise Moses gave Israel before his death (Deuteronomy 18:15–19). In Deuteronomy it assured Israel that God would keep speaking through prophets after Moses. By Jesus' time many were waiting for one prophet like Moses above all (John 1:21; 6:14). Peter says that prophet has come: Jesus, whom God raised up. Stephen repeats the same promise (7:37).
Why fulfilled
Like Moses, Jesus speaks God's word face to face, gives the law anew on a mountain, and leads his people out of slavery; Luke even calls his coming death in Jerusalem his “exodus” in the Greek of Luke 9:31. At the Transfiguration the Father's voice tells the disciples to listen to him (Mark 9:7), the very command of Deuteronomy.
See also
The prophet like Moses in Deuteronomy.
Acts 4:11–12ProphecyFulfilled
“He is ‘the stone rejected by you, the builders, which has become the cornerstone.’”
Reading
Arrested after healing a crippled man, Peter stands before the rulers and elders and applies Psalm 118:22 to Jesus, as Jesus had applied it to himself (Matthew 21:42). Peter adds two words to the psalm, “by you”: the builders are the leaders in front of him. Then he says, “There is no salvation through anyone else, nor is there any other name under heaven given to the human race by which we are to be saved” (4:12). The Catechism recalls that the Lord compared himself to the stone the builders rejected, made the cornerstone of God's building, the Church (CCC 756), and quotes 4:12 on the saving name of Jesus (CCC 432).
Why fulfilled
The rejection was the Cross; the raising was Easter. The building on that stone goes on, with believers as “living stones” (1 Peter 2:4–7; Ephesians 2:20). See The Temple of His Body and Psalm 118.
Acts 7:55–56ProphecyFulfilled
“Behold, I see the heavens opened and the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God.”
Reading
Stephen ends his speech to the Sanhedrin with the charge that their ancestors “put to death those who foretold the coming of the righteous one” (7:52). Then, “filled with the holy Spirit,” he looks up and sees Jesus at God's right hand. He uses the title Jesus used of himself, the Son of Man of Daniel 7:13. The NABRE note says that Stephen affirms to the Sanhedrin that the prophecy Jesus made before them (Mark 14:62) has been fulfilled.
Note
Elsewhere Christ is seated at the right hand (2:34). Why Stephen sees him standing is not settled: to welcome his first martyr, to stand as his witness and advocate, or to rise as judge. Each reading has defenders. Stephen dies as his Lord did, praying “Lord Jesus, receive my spirit” and “Lord, do not hold this sin against them” (7:59–60; compare Luke 23:34, 46). The Church calls martyrdom “the supreme witness given to the truth of the faith” (CCC 2473).
For now
Among the witnesses of his death stood “a young man named Saul” (7:58). Stephen's prayer was answered sooner than anyone could have guessed.
Acts 8:32–35ProphecyFulfilled
“Like a sheep he was led to the slaughter, and as a lamb before its shearer is silent, so he opened not his mouth.”
Reading
On the desert road to Gaza, an Ethiopian court official is reading Isaiah 53:7–8 aloud in his carriage. He asks Philip the question every reader of the Servant songs must ask: “I beg you, about whom is the prophet saying this? About himself, or about someone else?” (8:34). “Then Philip opened his mouth and, beginning with this scripture passage, he proclaimed Jesus to him” (8:35), and the man asked to be baptized. The Catechism teaches that Jesus' redemptive death fulfills Isaiah's prophecy of the suffering Servant, citing these verses (CCC 601).
Note
Acts quotes Isaiah in the Greek of the Septuagint, so its wording differs from the NABRE's translation of the Hebrew in Isaiah 53. Philip does what the risen Jesus did on the road to Emmaus, interpreting what referred to him in all the Scriptures (Luke 24:27). The silent lamb of Isaiah is the Lamb John sees standing in heaven (Revelation 5:6).
See also
The suffering Servant in Isaiah; John 1:29.
Acts 11:27–30ProphecyFulfilled
…one of them named Agabus stood up and predicted by the Spirit that there would be a severe famine all over the world, and it happened under Claudius.
Reading
Luke notes the fulfillment in the same sentence. Josephus records a great famine in Judea in these years, when Queen Helena of Adiabene bought grain in Egypt for the hungry (Antiquities 20.101). The church at Antioch answers the prophecy with charity, each sending relief “according to ability” to the brothers in Judea (11:29).
For now
The right response to a true prophecy is not alarm but practical love.
Acts 13:46–48ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“For so the Lord has commanded us, ‘I have made you a light to the Gentiles, that you may be an instrument of salvation to the ends of the earth.’”
Reading
At Antioch in Pisidia, when many in the synagogue reject their preaching, Paul and Barnabas say, “It was necessary that the word of God be spoken to you first” (13:46), and turn to the Gentiles. They give their reason from Isaiah 49:6, a word spoken of the Lord's Servant. Simeon had already sung it over the infant Jesus (Luke 2:32). Now the light of the Servant shines through his witnesses, and “the Gentiles were delighted when they heard this and glorified the word of the Lord” (13:48). The Second Vatican Council's teaching on the Church opens with the same image, Lumen Gentium, Latin for light of the nations, and applies it to Christ.
Note
The NABRE note is careful: the refusal frustrates God's plan for his chosen people, but “no adverse judgment is made here concerning their ultimate destiny.” See Romans 11.
Why two labels
Fulfilled when the Gentiles first received the word. The light is still traveling toward “the ends of the earth” (1:8).
Acts 15:14–18ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“After this I shall return and rebuild the fallen hut of David; from its ruins I shall rebuild it and raise it up again, so that the rest of humanity may seek out the Lord.”
Reading
At the Council of Jerusalem, James hears how God has acquired “from among the Gentiles a people for his name” (15:14), and finds it foretold in Amos 9:11–12. The house of David, fallen into ruin, is raised up in the risen Son of David, and the Gentiles come in. On that ground the Council does not burden the Gentiles who turn to God (15:19).
Why two labels
Fulfilled when the Gentiles were first received. The rebuilding of David's house, the Church of Jews and Gentiles, goes on; see The Temple of His Body on the temple still being built.
Acts 17:30–31ProphecyFuture
“…because he has established a day on which he will ‘judge the world with justice’ through a man he has appointed, and he has provided confirmation for all by raising him from the dead.”
Reading
On the Areopagus in Athens, Paul begins from an altar to an unknown god and from the Greeks' own poets, and ends with the day of judgment. “God has overlooked the times of ignorance, but now he demands that all people everywhere repent” (17:30). The words in single quotation marks are the Psalms' own hope that the Lord comes to set the world right (Psalm 96:13; 98:9). The judge is a man, and his credentials are his resurrection. The Catechism teaches that the Last Judgment will come when Christ returns in glory, and that only the Father knows its day and hour (CCC 1040).
Why future
God has fixed the day; he has not told it. Paul names no date, only the call to repent now.
For now
“When they heard about resurrection of the dead, some began to scoff,” while others said they would hear more (17:32). The judge of the world is the one who died for it, which is why his judgment is good news.
Acts 20:29–30ProphecyUnfolding
“I know that after my departure savage wolves will come among you, and they will not spare the flock.”
Reading
Paul's farewell to the elders of Ephesus at Miletus. Danger will come from outside, and also “from your own group,” men “perverting the truth to draw the disciples away after them” (20:30). His answer is pastoral: keep watch over the flock “in which you tend the church of God that he acquired with his own blood” (20:28), and be vigilant (20:31).
Why unfolding
Every generation of the Church has known false teachers. The Catechism speaks of a final trial that will include religious deception and apostasy (CCC 675). This page names no one: the warning is for shepherds to guard, not for readers to accuse.
Acts 21:10–11; 27:22–26ProphecyFulfilled
“This is the way the Jews will bind the owner of this belt in Jerusalem, and they will hand him over to the Gentiles.”
Reading
Agabus acts out his prophecy as the old prophets did, binding his own hands and feet with Paul's belt. Within days Paul is seized in the Temple and the Roman commander has him “secured with two chains” (21:33). Paul does not turn back: “I am prepared not only to be bound but even to die in Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus” (21:13). The Lord then tells him, “you must also bear witness in Rome” (23:11).
Note
On the voyage to Rome, in the storm, an angel tells Paul, “Do not be afraid,” and that God has granted safety to all who sail with him (27:24). Paul tells the crew, “not one of you will be lost, only the ship” (27:22). The ship runs aground, and “all reached shore safely” (27:44).
See also
Paul's own foreknowledge, given “in one city after another” by the Spirit (20:23).
Acts 28:23–31ProphecyFulfilledMystery
“Let it be known to you that this salvation of God has been sent to the Gentiles; they will listen.”
Reading
In Rome the Jewish leaders come to Paul's lodgings, and “from early morning until evening” he tries to convince them about Jesus “from the law of Moses and the prophets” (28:23). “Some were convinced by what he had said, while others did not believe” (28:24). Paul's last recorded words quote Isaiah 6:9–10, “Gross is the heart of this people; they will not hear with their ears” (28:27), the same prophecy Jesus applied to his own hearers (Matthew 13:14–15; John 12:40). The NABRE note explains that Paul's final words reflect a major concern of Luke's writings: the rejection by many leads to the proclamation among the Gentiles.
Why two labels
Isaiah's word came true again in Paul's day. But it is not the last word on Israel. Acts ends with an open door: Paul “received all who came to him” and proclaimed the kingdom “without hindrance” (28:30–31). And Paul himself had already written to Rome that Israel's hardening is “in part,” lasting until the full number of the Gentiles comes in (Romans 11:25–26). How that will come about is God's mystery. The Church teaches that “the Jews should not be presented as rejected or accursed by God” as if this followed from Scripture (Nostra Aetate 4).

Isaiah 6 on this site.
