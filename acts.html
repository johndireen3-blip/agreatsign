Prophecy in Acts

Acts is the book of the Church's first steps between the Ascension and the return. It opens with a promise that Jesus will come back, shows Joel's prophecy fulfilled at Pentecost, and records Christian prophets whose words came true within the story itself.

The book at a glance

Tradition attributes Acts to Luke, the companion of Paul, as the second volume of his Gospel; both are addressed to Theophilus (Luke 1:3; Acts 1:1). It follows the Gospel from Jerusalem outward: the Ascension and Pentecost (chapters 1–2), the Church in Jerusalem and Judea under Peter (chapters 3–12), and Paul's missions, arrest and journey to Rome (chapters 13–28). The book ends with Paul in Rome, proclaiming the kingdom of God “without hindrance” (28:31).

Acts looks forward in three ways. It shows Old Testament prophecy fulfilled in the Church, above all Joel at Pentecost and Amos at the Council of Jerusalem. It records the promise of Christ's return and of the restoration of all things. And it shows the gift of prophecy at work: Agabus, Paul and an angel foretell what then happens. Many of these passages are fulfilled inside the book; others are still being fulfilled while the Gospel goes to “the ends of the earth.”

The passages

Acts 1:6–7ProphecyFuture
“It is not for you to know the times or seasons that the Father has established by his own authority.”
Reading
The disciples ask whether the kingdom will now be restored to Israel. Jesus does not deny that the kingdom will come; he refuses to give its date. The Catechism uses these words to teach that Christ's return is always imminent, yet no one may know when it will be (CCC 673).
Note
This is Matthew 24:36 again, at the very start of the Church's life, and the reason this site sets no dates.
Acts 1:8PromiseUnfolding
“But you will receive power when the holy Spirit comes upon you, and you will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, throughout Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth.”
Reading
Instead of a date, Jesus gives a task, and the verse is the outline of the whole book: Jerusalem (chapters 1–7), Judea and Samaria (chapter 8), then the nations, until Paul reaches Rome. The Church receives this as her missionary mandate to all peoples (CCC 849).
Why unfolding
The power came at Pentecost. The witness “to the ends of the earth” is still under way.
See also
Matthew 24:14; 28:19–20.
Acts 1:9–11PromiseFuture
“This Jesus who has been taken up from you into heaven will return in the same way as you have seen him going into heaven.”
Reading
Jesus is lifted up and “a cloud took him from their sight” (1:9). Two men in white tell the apostles to stop looking at the sky: he will come back as truly as he went. The Catechism cites this verse for Christ's return in glory (CCC 665), the return professed in the Creed.
Note
“In the same way” is read by the Church as a real, personal and visible return, not a hidden one. The cloud recalls Daniel 7:13 and Jesus' own words in Matthew 24:30.
Acts 2:16–21ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“‘It will come to pass in the last days,’ God says, ‘that I will pour out a portion of my spirit upon all flesh.’”
Reading
Peter explains Pentecost with Joel 3:1–5: “this is what was spoken through the prophet Joel” (2:16). Where Joel had said only “It shall come to pass,” Peter says “It will come to pass in the last days.” The Catechism teaches that Christ's Passover is completed in the outpouring of the Spirit (CCC 731), and that from that day the world has entered the “last days,” the time of the Church (CCC 732).
Why two labels
Fulfilled at Pentecost. The promise is “to you and to your children and to all those far off” (2:39), so the outpouring goes on, and “everyone shall be saved who calls on the name of the Lord” (2:21). Joel's signs in the sun and moon (2:19–20) Peter leaves without a date.
Acts 3:19–21PromiseFuture
“…the Messiah already appointed for you, Jesus, whom heaven must receive until the times of universal restoration of which God spoke through the mouth of his holy prophets from of old.”
Reading
In the Temple, Peter calls his hearers to repent “that your sins may be wiped away” (3:19), so that the Lord may send “times of refreshment” and the Messiah. Christ remains in heaven until the restoration of all things. The Catechism reads this sermon as part of its teaching that the glorious coming of the Messiah awaits the recognition of Jesus by “all Israel” (CCC 674; Romans 11:25–26).
Note
The NABRE notes call the “times of refreshment” an apocalyptic name for the messianic age, and read the “universal restoration” in the same light. The Church does not set its timing; she keeps it as hope.
Acts 11:27–30ProphecyFulfilled
…one of them named Agabus stood up and predicted by the Spirit that there would be a severe famine all over the world, and it happened under Claudius.
Reading
Luke notes the fulfillment in the same sentence. Josephus records a great famine in Judea in these years, when Queen Helena of Adiabene bought grain in Egypt for the hungry (Antiquities 20.101). The church at Antioch answers the prophecy with charity, each sending relief “according to ability” to the brothers in Judea (11:29).
For now
The right response to a true prophecy is not alarm but practical love.
Acts 15:14–18ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“After this I shall return and rebuild the fallen hut of David; from its ruins I shall rebuild it and raise it up again, so that the rest of humanity may seek out the Lord.”
Reading
At the Council of Jerusalem, James hears how God has acquired “from among the Gentiles a people for his name” (15:14), and finds it foretold in Amos 9:11–12. The house of David, fallen into ruin, is raised up in the risen Son of David, and the Gentiles come in. On that ground the Council does not burden the Gentiles who turn to God (15:19).
Why two labels
Fulfilled when the Gentiles were first received. The rebuilding of David's house, the Church of Jews and Gentiles, goes on; see The Temple of His Body on the temple still being built.
Acts 20:29–30ProphecyUnfolding
“I know that after my departure savage wolves will come among you, and they will not spare the flock.”
Reading
Paul's farewell to the elders of Ephesus at Miletus. Danger will come from outside, and also “from your own group,” men “perverting the truth to draw the disciples away after them” (20:30). His answer is pastoral: keep watch over the flock “in which you tend the church of God that he acquired with his own blood” (20:28), and be vigilant (20:31).
Why unfolding
Every generation of the Church has known false teachers. The Catechism speaks of a final trial that will include religious deception and apostasy (CCC 675). This page names no one: the warning is for shepherds to guard, not for readers to accuse.
Acts 21:10–11; 27:22–26ProphecyFulfilled
“This is the way the Jews will bind the owner of this belt in Jerusalem, and they will hand him over to the Gentiles.”
Reading
Agabus acts out his prophecy as the old prophets did, binding his own hands and feet with Paul's belt. Within days Paul is seized in the Temple and the Roman commander has him “secured with two chains” (21:33). Paul does not turn back: “I am prepared not only to be bound but even to die in Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus” (21:13). The Lord then tells him, “you must also bear witness in Rome” (23:11).
Note
On the voyage to Rome, in the storm, an angel tells Paul, “Do not be afraid,” and that God has granted safety to all who sail with him (27:24). Paul tells the crew, “not one of you will be lost, only the ship” (27:22). The ship runs aground, and “all reached shore safely” (27:44).
See also
Paul's own foreknowledge, given “in one city after another” by the Spirit (20:23).
