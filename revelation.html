Prophecy in Revelation

The last book of the Bible is not a puzzle about the end of the world. Its first words give its title, “The revelation of Jesus Christ”: it is the unveiling of the Lamb who was slain and now reigns. It was written to strengthen persecuted churches, and it still does: evil is real, its time is short, and the One on the throne says, “Behold, I make all things new.”

The book at a glance

The book is written by a seer who calls himself simply “John, your brother,” exiled on the island of Patmos “because I proclaimed God's word and gave testimony to Jesus” (Revelation 1:9). Tradition from the second century identifies him with John the Apostle. Many modern scholars, Catholic scholars among them, think the seer was another John, a prophet of the churches of Asia; the Church receives the book as inspired Scripture either way. Most date it to the end of the reign of the emperor Domitian, about the year 95, following St. Irenaeus, who writes that the vision was seen “towards the end of Domitian's reign” (Against Heresies 5.30.3); a minority place it earlier, under or just after Nero.

Revelation is three things at once. It calls itself an apocalypse, an unveiling: “The revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave to him, to show his servants what must happen soon” (1:1). It calls itself a prophecy: “Blessed is the one who reads aloud and blessed are those who listen to this prophetic message” (1:3). And it is a letter: “John, to the seven churches in Asia” (1:4). Its visions are full of images and types drawn from the whole Old Testament, from Genesis and Exodus to Isaiah, Ezekiel, Daniel and Zechariah, but it almost never quotes them directly. It weaves them into one picture of Christ's victory.

The Church does not read Revelation as a coded timetable. She reads it as the unveiling of what Christ has already won and will bring to completion, and she reads it above all in the liturgy: the Catechism describes the heavenly liturgy of Revelation 4–5 and 7, the throne, the Lamb and the great multitude, and teaches that we share in it whenever we celebrate the sacraments (CCC 1137–1139). She also reads it with the Fathers (on chapter 12; on chapter 13), above all St. Victorinus of Pettau, who wrote the oldest surviving commentary, and St. Augustine. Readers have followed four main approaches. The historical (or preterist) reading sees John's own world: Rome, the emperor cult, the persecution of the churches. The ecclesial-historical reading sees the life of the Church through the ages. The futurist reading sees the events just before Christ's return. The spiritual (or idealist) reading sees the struggle of every age between the Lamb and the powers of evil. Catholic interpreters usually hold several of these together, since a symbol that spoke first to Smyrna and Pergamum can still speak to us and will be fulfilled at the end. What the Church never does is set dates: Christ's coming has been imminent since the Ascension, but “it is not for you to know times or seasons” (CCC 673).

The passages

Revelation 1:4–8ProphecyFuture
“Behold, he is coming amid the clouds, and every eye will see him, even those who pierced him.” (1:7)
Reading
In one verse John joins two prophets: the “One like a son of man” coming with the clouds of heaven (Daniel 7:13) and the one whom Jerusalem will look upon, whom they have pierced (Zechariah 12:10; John 19:37). The verse ends, “All the peoples of the earth will lament him. Yes. Amen.” God then speaks in his own name: “I am the Alpha and the Omega,” “the one who is and who was and who is to come, the almighty” (1:8).
Why future
This is the book's theme, announced at the start: Christ will come in glory, visibly, for all. The Church professes it in the Creed and waits for it in hope; the lament is the grief of repentance, and the “Amen” is the Church's own consent.

Revelation 1:9–20ApocalypseFulfilledUnfolding
“Do not be afraid. I am the first and the last, the one who lives. Once I was dead, but now I am alive forever and ever. I hold the keys to death and the netherworld.” (1:17–18)
Reading
“I was caught up in spirit on the Lord's day” (1:10): the vision begins on a Sunday, the day of the Resurrection. John sees “seven gold lampstands and in the midst of the lampstands one like a son of man” (1:12–13), robed as a priest, with seven stars in his right hand. The stars are the angels of the seven churches and the lampstands are the churches themselves (1:20). John falls “as though dead,” and the risen Christ lays his hand on him.
Fulfilled
Daniel's son of man is the risen Jesus, who has passed through death and holds its keys.
Unfolding
He is not far off. He walks among the lampstands now, in the midst of his churches, and his first word to a frightened disciple is “Do not be afraid.”

The Lamb's Victory on chapter 1.
Revelation 2–3ProphecyUnfolding
“Yet I hold this against you: you have lost the love you had at first.” (2:4)
Reading
Christ dictates seven letters to real churches in Asia Minor: Ephesus, Smyrna, Pergamum, Thyatira, Sardis, Philadelphia and Laodicea. He praises, warns and promises. To Ephesus, faithful in labor and in testing false teachers but cold in love: “Repent, and do the works you did at first” (2:5). To persecuted Smyrna: “Remain faithful until death, and I will give you the crown of life” (2:10). To lukewarm Laodicea, “neither hot nor cold” (3:16): “Behold, I stand at the door and knock” (3:20). Each letter ends with the same call, “Whoever has ears ought to hear what the Spirit says to the churches,” and a promise “to the victor,” from the tree of life (2:7) to a place on Christ's own throne (3:21).
Why unfolding
St. Victorinus noted that seven is the number of fullness: what Christ says to these seven churches he says to the whole Church in every age (Victorinus on the seven churches). Some later interpreters read the letters as seven eras of Church history, but the Church has never required that reading; above all they are a mirror for every parish and every soul. This site takes its own name from the first of them (How this site is made): knowledge of Scripture cannot take the place of the love we had at first.

The Lamb's Victory on chapter 2 and chapter 3.
Revelation 4–5ApocalypseFulfilledUnfolding
“Worthy is the Lamb that was slain to receive power and riches, wisdom and strength, honor and glory and blessing.” (5:12)
Reading
John sees “an open door to heaven” (4:1) and a throne. Four living creatures sing without ceasing, “Holy, holy, holy is the Lord God almighty, who was, and who is, and who is to come” (4:8), and twenty-four elders cast down their crowns. No one can open the sealed scroll of God's plan until he hears: “The lion of the tribe of Judah, the root of David, has triumphed” (5:5). He looks, and sees not a lion but “a Lamb that seemed to have been slain” (5:6). The Lamb takes the scroll, and heaven sings a new song: “for you were slain and with your blood you purchased for God those from every tribe and tongue, people and nation” (5:9).
Fulfilled
The center of the whole book is already accomplished. The Lamb has conquered by being slain; the Cross is the key to history.
Unfolding
This is the heavenly liturgy, and the Church joins it at every Mass (CCC 1137–1139). We sing the Sanctus with the living creatures and with Isaiah's seraphim (Isaiah 6:3). The elders hold “gold bowls filled with incense, which are the prayers of the holy ones” (5:8): our prayers rise there too.
See also
Victorinus on the four living creatures; The Lamb's Victory on chapter 4 and chapter 5.
Revelation 6:1–8:1ApocalypseUnfoldingFuture
“These are the ones who have survived the time of great distress; they have washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb.” (7:14)
Reading
The Lamb opens the seals one by one. Four horsemen ride out, the first on a white horse, “and its rider had a bow” (6:2), then war, famine and death, the sorrows Jesus foretold in Matthew 24:6–8. Under the altar the souls of the martyrs cry, “How long will it be, holy and true master, before you sit in judgment” (6:10). Before the end, the angels hold back harm “until we put the seal on the foreheads of the servants of our God” (7:3): “one hundred and forty-four thousand” from the tribes of Israel (7:4). Then John looks and sees “a great multitude, which no one could count, from every nation, race, people, and tongue” (7:9). At the seventh seal, “there was silence in heaven” (8:1).
Unfolding
The horsemen ride through every age; the seal is the mark of those who belong to God, and the Fathers saw in it the seal of Baptism. The number 144,000, twelve times twelve times a thousand, is the fullness of the people of God, and the multitude no one can count is the same people seen as God sees it, gathered from all the nations promised to Abraham (Genesis 12:3). The Catechism names this multitude among those who celebrate the heavenly liturgy (CCC 1138).
Future
The end of chapter 7 belongs to the end of all things: the Lamb will shepherd them “and God will wipe away every tear from their eyes” (7:17).

The Lamb's Victory on chapter 6 and chapter 7.
Revelation 8:2–11:19ApocalypseUnfoldingFutureMystery
“The kingdom of the world now belongs to our Lord and to his Anointed, and he will reign forever and ever.” (11:15)
Reading
Seven angels “were given seven trumpets” (8:2). First an angel offers incense with “the prayers of all the holy ones” on the gold altar (8:3): the trumpets that follow are God's answer to the martyrs' cry. Fire, blood, darkness and the locusts of the abyss recall the plagues of Egypt, and still the survivors do not repent (9:20–21). Then God says, “I will commission my two witnesses to prophesy for those twelve hundred and sixty days, wearing sackcloth” (11:3). They are killed and lie in the street of the great city, and after three and a half days they are raised and taken up. At the seventh trumpet loud voices proclaim the kingdom, and “the ark of his covenant could be seen in the temple” (11:19).
Unfolding
The trumpets are warnings, not yet the end, and their purpose is repentance. St. Victorinus taught that the trumpets and the bowls retell the same span of time from different sides (recapitulation), a reading many Catholic interpreters still follow.
Why mystery
Who the two witnesses are is not settled. Many Fathers named Enoch and Elijah, Victorinus named Elijah and Jeremiah (Victorinus on the two witnesses), and many readers see in them the prophetic witness of the whole Church. The twelve hundred and sixty days, three and a half years, are the broken half of seven: a time of trial that is real but limited.
Future
Heaven sings the song of 11:15 as if the kingdom had already come, though on earth its fullness is still awaited. It is certain because the Lamb has already won.
See also
The Lamb's Victory on chapter 8 and chapter 11.
Revelation 12ApocalypseFulfilledUnfoldingFuture
“A great sign appeared in the sky, a woman clothed with the sun, with the moon under her feet, and on her head a crown of twelve stars.” (12:1)
Reading
The ark seen in the temple gives way to a woman in labor, and a huge red dragon waits to devour her child. The child is caught up to God's throne, Michael and his angels cast down “the huge dragon, the ancient serpent, who is called the Devil and Satan, who deceived the whole world” (12:9), and the woman is fed in the desert. The Church reads the woman as Israel, as Mary the Mother of the Messiah, and as the Church, all at once.
Fulfilled
The child is born and enthroned: this is the Incarnation, Cross and Ascension, the promise of Genesis 3:15 kept.
Unfolding
The dragon, thrown down, wars against “the rest of her offspring” (12:17). But “They conquered him by the blood of the Lamb” (12:11).
Future
The dragon knows his time is short (12:12). His defeat, already won at the Cross, will be complete when Christ returns (20:10).

This site's full study, Revelation 12: The Woman and the Dragon; the Fathers on Revelation 12; The Lamb's Victory on chapter 12.
Revelation 13ApocalypseUnfoldingMystery
“Wisdom is needed here; one who understands can calculate the number of the beast, for it is a number that stands for a person. His number is six hundred and sixty-six.” (13:18)
Reading
The dragon stands on the shore and John sees “a beast come out of the sea with ten horns and seven heads” (13:1), a figure built from all four beasts of Daniel 7. It receives the dragon's power and is worshiped. A second beast rises from the land with “two horns like a lamb's” but speaking like a dragon (13:11); later the book calls it the false prophet (16:13). It makes people bear the beast's mark, a counterfeit of the seal of God (7:3). In John's day the beast wore the face of imperial Rome and its cult.
Unfolding
Every age meets some power that demands the worship due to God, and some voice that makes it look like a lamb. The answer is the same in every age: “Such is the faithful endurance of the holy ones” (13:10). The Church teaches that before Christ's coming she will pass through a final trial, and that the Antichrist is “the supreme religious deception” (CCC 675).
Why mystery
The Fathers offered names for 666 and said they were guessing; St. Irenaeus counseled waiting rather than guessing (Irenaeus on the number). Many scholars think John's first readers would have heard in it the name of a persecuting emperor of their own day. The Church has adopted no name, and this site names no one.
See also
The Fathers on Revelation 13; Newman on the Antichrist; The Lamb's Victory on chapter 13.
Revelation 14ApocalypseUnfoldingFuture
“Blessed are the dead who die in the Lord from now on.” (14:13)
Reading
After the beasts, John looks and “there was the Lamb standing on Mount Zion” (14:1) with the 144,000, who bear his name and his Father's name on their foreheads instead of the mark of the beast. They “follow the Lamb wherever he goes” (14:4). Angels proclaim the everlasting gospel to every nation and call all to worship the Creator. Then one like a son of man on a white cloud is told that “the earth's harvest is fully ripe” (14:15), and the earth is reaped.
Unfolding
The faithful already belong to the Lamb, and their dead already rest from their labors.
Future
The harvest is the image Jesus used for the end of the age (Matthew 13:39). It comes in God's time, when the grain is ripe.

The Lamb's Victory on chapter 14.
Revelation 15–16TypeUnfoldingFuture
“Behold, I am coming like a thief.” (16:15)
Reading
John sees “seven angels with the seven last plagues” (15:1). Beside a sea of glass the victors over the beast sing “the song of Moses” and “the song of the Lamb” (15:3), as Israel sang after crossing the Red Sea. The bowls are then poured out: sores, seas and rivers turned to blood, scorching sun, darkness, the drying of the Euphrates, and the kings gathered “in the place that is named Armageddon” (16:16). At the seventh bowl a voice from the throne says, “It is done” (16:17).
Type
The bowls are a new Exodus. Their plagues follow the plagues of Egypt (Exodus 7–11), and the song of Moses is sung by those whom the Lamb has set free (Exodus 15). As with Pharaoh, the plagues are also a call to conversion, and those struck “did not repent or give him glory” (16:9).
For now
In the middle of the bowls Christ himself speaks a beatitude for the watchful, echoing his own words in the Gospel (Matthew 24:43). Armageddon is the “Mountain of Megiddo,” a place of ancient battles; John uses it as a symbol of the last gathering of evil, not as a map.

The Lamb's Victory on chapter 15 and chapter 16.
Revelation 17–18ApocalypseFulfilledUnfoldingMystery
“Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great.” (18:2)
Reading
A woman rides the scarlet beast, and on her forehead is a name: “Babylon the great, the mother of harlots and of the abominations of the earth” (17:5). Her seven heads are “seven hills upon which the woman sits” (17:9), and readers in Asia would have thought at once of Rome. The kings make war on the Lamb, but “the Lamb will conquer them, for he is Lord of lords and king of kings” (17:14). A voice cries, “Depart from her, my people” (18:4), the merchants and sailors mourn her wealth, and an angel throws a stone like a huge millstone into the sea (18:21).
Fulfilled
The words are those of the prophets against the old Babylon (Jeremiah 50–51; Isaiah 21:9). The empire that persecuted the churches in John's day did fall.
Unfolding
Babylon is more than one city. She is every order of the world built on idolatry, luxury and the blood of the innocent, and the call to depart from her is a call to leave her ways.
Why mystery
John himself calls the name “a mystery” (17:5), and the seven kings of 17:10–11 have been counted many ways. This site does not identify Babylon with any present nation, city or church.

The Lamb's Victory on chapter 17 and chapter 18.
Revelation 19ImageUnfoldingFuture
“Blessed are those who have been called to the wedding feast of the Lamb.” (19:9)
Reading
Heaven answers Babylon's fall with Alleluias: “For the wedding day of the Lamb has come, his bride has made herself ready” (19:7). The harlot city gives way to the bride. Then heaven opens and a white horse appears, and its rider is called “Faithful and True” (19:11). His cloak is dipped in blood, “his name was called the Word of God” (19:13), and on his cloak and thigh is written “King of kings and Lord of lords” (19:16). He conquers by the sword that comes from his mouth, his word.
Unfolding
The wedding feast is already tasted. The Eucharist “anticipates the wedding feast of the Lamb in the heavenly Jerusalem” (CCC 1329), and before Communion the priest says, “Blessed are those called to the supper of the Lamb.”
Future
The rider is Christ coming in glory. The battle is won before it begins: there is no fight described, only the victory of the Word.

The Lamb's Victory on chapter 19.
Revelation 20:1–10ApocalypseUnfoldingMystery
“They came to life and they reigned with Christ for a thousand years.” (20:4)
Reading
An angel “seized the dragon, the ancient serpent, which is the Devil or Satan, and tied it up for a thousand years” (20:2). The martyrs and those who refused the beast reign with Christ; this is “the first resurrection.” At the end Satan is released for a short time, gathers Gog and Magog against the camp of the holy ones, and is thrown into the pool of fire for ever.
Unfolding
St. Augustine, who had once expected an earthly reign of a thousand years, came to read the thousand years as the present age of the Church, between Christ's first coming and his return: Satan is bound so that he cannot seduce the Church, and “even now His saints reign with Him” (City of God 20.7–9). This became the common reading of the Church in the West.
Why mystery
The Church has rejected millenarianism, the expectation of an earthly messianic kingdom before the end, “even modified forms” of it (CCC 676). Some early Fathers, St. Irenaeus among them, did hold a form of it (Irenaeus on the kingdom). But she has not defined how the thousand years must be counted, and the last loosing of Satan is the final trial no one can date (CCC 675, 677).
See also
Victorinus on the thousand years; The Lamb's Victory on chapter 20.
Revelation 20:11–15ProphecyFuture
“Next I saw a large white throne and the one who was sitting on it.” (20:11)
Reading
Earth and sky flee, and the dead, great and small, stand before the throne. Scrolls are opened, and the book of life. “The dead were judged according to their deeds, by what was written in the scrolls” (20:12). The sea and Death and Hades give up their dead, and “Then Death and Hades were thrown into the pool of fire” (20:14).
Why future
This is the Last Judgment the Church professes in the Creed. It will come when Christ returns in glory, after the resurrection of all the dead, and “the truth of each man's relationship with God will be laid bare” (CCC 1038–1041). Death itself, the last enemy, is destroyed (1 Corinthians 15:26).
For now
The message of the Last Judgment is a call to conversion while there is time (CCC 1041). The book of life is the Lamb's, and he wants every name written in it.
See also
St. Augustine on the Last Judgment; The Lamb's Victory on chapter 20.
Revelation 21:1–22:5PromiseFuture
“Behold, God's dwelling is with the human race.” (21:3)
Reading
“Then I saw a new heaven and a new earth” (21:1). The holy city, the new Jerusalem, comes down out of heaven “prepared as a bride” (21:2). “He will wipe every tear from their eyes” (21:4), and the one on the throne says, “Behold, I make all things new” (21:5). There is no temple: “I saw no temple in the city, for its temple is the Lord God almighty and the Lamb” (21:22), and “its lamp was the Lamb” (21:23). From the throne flows “the river of life-giving water, sparkling like crystal” (22:1); beside it grows the tree of life, and “the leaves of the trees serve as medicine for the nations” (22:2). “They will look upon his face” (22:4).
Why future
This is the hope of the whole Bible. The garden of Genesis 2 returns as a city, and the God who walked with Adam dwells with his people for ever. The Catechism teaches that at the end of time the Kingdom will come in its fullness and the universe itself will be renewed: “In this new universe, the heavenly Jerusalem, God will have his dwelling among men” (CCC 1042–1045).
See also
Irenaeus on the new Jerusalem; Isaiah's new heavens and new earth; The Lamb's Victory on chapter 21 and chapter 22.
Revelation 22:6–21ProphecyUnfoldingFuture
“I am the Alpha and the Omega, the first and the last, the beginning and the end.” (22:13)
Reading
The book closes as it opened, with a blessing for those who keep its prophetic message and the voice of Christ. He is the first and last letter of the alphabet, the one in whom all things begin and end, the root and offspring of David, “the bright morning star” (22:16). “The Spirit and the bride say, ‘Come’” (22:17), and the last words of the Bible are a promise and a prayer: “The one who gives this testimony says, ‘Yes, I am coming soon.’ Amen! Come, Lord Jesus!” (22:20).
Unfolding
The Church prays this prayer every day. In the Eucharist “she calls for his coming: ‘Marana tha!’ ‘Come, Lord Jesus!’” (CCC 1403; 1 Corinthians 16:22). He who is coming already comes to us at the altar.
Future
“Soon” is God's measure, not ours (2 Peter 3:8). The Bible ends not with a date but with a longing, and with grace: “The grace of the Lord Jesus be with all” (22:21).

The Lamb's Victory on chapter 22.

Revelation and Genesis: the Bible's bookends

The first book opens what the last book closes. Revelation answers Genesis point by point: what was lost in the garden is restored, and more, in the city.

Read with careRevelation has drawn more date-setting than any other book of the Bible, and every calendar built from it has failed. Its numbers, beasts and cities are symbols, not a timetable: seven is fullness, three and a half is a trial cut short, a thousand is the fullness of God's time. The Church sets no date, since “it is not for you to know times or seasons” (CCC 673), and this site names no living person as the beast, the false prophet or Babylon. Private revelations, however valuable, do not add to what Christ has revealed (CCC 67). The book was written to give the suffering faithful courage, and that is how it should be read.
