St. Jerome on the Antichrist

A pagan philosopher said the Book of Daniel was only history in disguise, and that its last enemy was a Syrian king long dead. St. Jerome answered him verse by verse. The tyrant of Daniel's day was real, he said, but he was a sketch. The full portrait is still to come: a man, not a demon, in whom Satan will dwell, and whom Christ will destroy.

At a glance

WhoSt. Jerome (c. 347–420), born at Stridon, priest, monk and translator of the Bible into Latin, who spent his last decades in Bethlehem near the Grotto of the Nativity
StandingFather of the Church Doctor of the Church
The worksCommentary on Daniel, written in Bethlehem around 407–408, a reply above all to the pagan Porphyry; Letter 121, to Algasia (406), question 11, on 2 Thessalonians 2:3; Letter 123, to Ageruchia (409)
TranslationsGleason L. Archer, Jr., Jerome's Commentary on Daniel (Baker, 1958), at tertullian.org (prologue through chapter 6), with the whole scanned book at the Internet Archive; W. H. Fremantle and others, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, Second Series, vol. 6, for the letters at New Advent
WeightThe greatest biblical scholar among the Latin Fathers, reporting what he calls the view of “all the commentators of the Christian Church.” On the core points he speaks for the Fathers; on several details he gives his own opinion or that of his school, and says so.

Why Jerome matters here

Jerome gave his life to the Bible. Pope Benedict XVI recalled his words, “Ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ” (General Audience, November 7, 2007). When he wrote on Daniel, he wrote as a scholar who knew the Hebrew, the Greek versions and the history of the Seleucid kings, and who still read the prophecy as pointing beyond its own century.

His commentary became the standard reading of Daniel in the West. St. Augustine, writing on Daniel 7 in The City of God, sent readers who wanted the details to Jerome (see St. Augustine on the Last Judgment). St. John Henry Newman quoted him as a witness to the universal tradition (see Newman on the Antichrist). And when St. John of Damascus wrote that the Antichrist is a man and not the devil made flesh, his English editors pointed to Jerome on Daniel 7 as the source (see St. John of Damascus).

The commentary, step by step

Prologue · Against PorphyryPatristic teachingFulfilled
“Porphyry wrote his twelfth book against the prophecy of Daniel…”
Teaching
The third-century philosopher Porphyry, an enemy of the Church, argued that Daniel was written not by the prophet but by “some individual living in Judaea at the time of the Antiochus who was surnamed Epiphanes,” and that “whatever is foretold concerning Antichrist at the end of the world was actually fulfilled in the reign of Antiochus Epiphanes.” Jerome turns the argument around: the prophecy matched the history so closely that the attack itself “testifies to Daniel's accuracy.”
Why fulfilled
Much of Daniel 11 does describe Antiochus IV, who profaned the Temple in 167 B.C.; Jerome agrees, and so do the NABRE notes. The question between Jerome and Porphyry was whether that is all the prophecy means. See Daniel 11 and 1 Maccabees.
On Daniel 7:8 · The little horn is a manPatristic teachingFuture
“Let us not follow the opinion of some commentators and suppose him to be either the Devil or some demon, but rather, one of the human race, in whom Satan will wholly take up his residence in bodily form.”
Teaching
Daniel sees ten horns on the fourth beast, and then “another, a little horn, sprang out of their midst… This horn had eyes like human eyes, and a mouth that spoke arrogantly” (Daniel 7:8). Porphyry said it was Antiochus. Jerome gives instead “the traditional interpretation of all the commentators of the Christian Church”: at the end of the world “there shall be ten kings who will partition the Roman world amongst themselves,” and “Then an insignificant eleventh king will arise, who will overcome three of the ten kings.”
The “human eyes” are the key. They show that the horn is not the devil but a man, and Jerome joins him at once to Paul: “For this is the man of sin, the son of perdition,” who will “seat himself in the temple of God” (2 Thessalonians 2:4). Newman's own translation of the passage, in his first lecture on the Antichrist, renders it: “he is one man, in whom Satan shall dwell bodily” (Newman Reader).
Why it matters
This is the point on which the Fathers agree and the Church still speaks: the Antichrist is a person, a creature, used by Satan but not Satan himself (CCC 675). The ten kings dividing the Roman world are Jerome's reading of the horns, shared by earlier Fathers, but the Church has not adopted it. See Daniel 7.
On Daniel 11:21–35 · Antiochus, a type of the AntichristPatristic teachingFulfilledFuture
“And so, just as the Savior had Solomon and the other saints as types of His advent, so also we should believe that the Antichrist very properly had as a type of himself the utterly wicked king, Antiochus, who persecuted the saints and defiled the Temple.”
Teaching
Here Jerome sets out his principle. Porphyry applies the rest of the chapter to Antiochus, while “those of our persuasion” believe “all these things are spoken prophetically of the Antichrist who is to arise in the end time.” Jerome does not deny that much of it fits Antiochus. Rather, Antiochus “is to be regarded as a type of the Antichrist, and those things which happened to him in a preliminary way are to be completely fulfilled in the case of the Antichrist.” The same holds for the profaning of the Temple (11:31): it happened under Antiochus “in a preliminary way as a mere type of the Antichrist, who is destined to seat himself in the Temple of God, and make himself out to be as God.”
Why two labels
This is how the Church reads much prophecy: a first fulfillment in history that points ahead to a fuller one. Jesus himself took Daniel's “desolating abomination” and applied it to a future day (Matthew 24:15). See Matthew 24:15–28.
On Daniel 11:36–45 · The king who exalts himself, and his endPatristic teachingFuture
“We too understand this to refer to the Antichrist.”
Teaching
At 11:36 the king does as he wills and exalts himself above every god. Jerome notes that on this verse Jewish and Christian interpreters agree: “The Jews believe that this passage has reference to the Antichrist. … We too understand this to refer to the Antichrist.” It is the same figure Paul describes, who “exalts himself above every so-called god and object of worship” (2 Thessalonians 2:4).
On the last verse, “he shall come to his end with none to help him” (Daniel 11:45), Jerome writes, “And no one shall be able to assist the Antichrist as the Lord vents his fury upon him,” and on Daniel 12:1 he says that it is “after the Antichrist is crushed and destroyed by the breath of the Savior's mouth” that the people written in God's book will be saved (compare 2 Thessalonians 2:8).
His own opinion
Jerome adds: “Our school of thought insists that Antichrist is going to perish in that spot from which the Lord ascended to heaven,” that is, the Mount of Olives. He offers this as the view of his school, not as revealed; the Church has not adopted it.
On Daniel 12:7–12 · The 1,290 and the 1,335 daysPatristic opinionMystery
“…the reason for the forty-five days of inaction after the slaying of the Antichrist is a matter which rests in the knowledge of God…”
Teaching
Daniel hears: “From the time that the daily sacrifice is abolished and the desolating abomination is set up, there shall be one thousand two hundred and ninety days. Blessed are they who have patience and persevere for the one thousand three hundred and thirty-five days” (Daniel 12:11–12). Jerome takes the “time, two times, and half a time” (7:25; 12:7) as three and a half years, the span in which the Antichrist “is going to persecute the saints for three and a half years.” Against Porphyry he notes that the Temple's profanation under Antiochus lasted three years, while “under the Antichrist it is not stated that the desolation and overthrow of the holy Temple shall endure for three years, but for three years and a half, that is, one thousand two hundred and ninety days.”
The extra forty-five days of verse 12 he reads as a promise: “He means that he is blessed who waits for forty-five days beyond the predetermined number,” for it is in that interval that the Lord comes in glory. Why the Lord waits after the Antichrist's fall, Jerome will not pretend to know. He offers only one suggestion: perhaps “the rule of the saints is delayed in order that their patience may be tested.”
Why mystery
Jerome uses the numbers to describe a short, limited trial, not to set a date, and he stops where Scripture stops. That restraint is the Church's own: “it is not for you to know times or seasons which the Father has fixed by his own authority” (CCC 673). See Daniel 12:4–13.

The letters: 2 Thessalonians 2 and the falling of Rome

Letter 121, to Algasia (406) · Question 11Patristic opinionMystery
The question
Algasia, a Christian woman of Gaul, sent Jerome eleven questions on hard passages of Scripture. The last asked the meaning of 2 Thessalonians 2:3: “unless the apostasy comes first and the lawless one is revealed, the one doomed to perdition.”
His answer, in summary
No complete English translation of Jerome's answers is freely available online; the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers gives only the list of questions. We therefore paraphrase from the Latin, and do not quote an English version. Jerome took the “apostasy” (in Latin, discessio, a departure) to mean that all the nations subject to the Roman Empire would depart from it (ut omnes gentes quae Romano imperio subjacent, recedant ab eis). He understood the power that “restrains” the lawless one (2 Thessalonians 2:6–7) to be the Roman Empire, which now holds all the nations (quod nunc universas gentes tenet): it must first withdraw and be taken out of the way, and then the Antichrist will come. He added that the “temple of God” where the lawless one sits may be Jerusalem, as some thought, or the Church, which he judged the truer reading (vel Jerosolymis, ut quidam putant, vel in Ecclesia, ut verius arbitramur). The Latin (Migne's text, with a French translation) is at remacle.org; the critical edition is Isidor Hilberg's (Corpus Scriptorum Ecclesiasticorum Latinorum, vol. 56). The Catholic commentator George Leo Haydock summarizes the letter the same way (Haydock on 2 Thessalonians 2).
Why mystery
St. Paul did not say what restrains the lawless one, and the Church has not settled it. St. Augustine, reviewing the same opinions, wrote, “I frankly confess I do not know what he means” (City of God 20.19). See 2 Thessalonians 2:6–7.
Letter 123, to Ageruchia (409) · 16ExhortationUnfolding
“He that lets is taken out of the way, and yet we do not realize that Antichrist is near.”
Teaching
Three years after the letter to Algasia, amid the barbarian invasions of the West, Jerome saw the restraining power crumbling before his eyes. “He that lets” is the old English for “he who restrains.” He went on: “Yes, Antichrist is near whom the Lord Jesus Christ ‘shall consume with the spirit of his mouth.’”
What has not come to pass
Rome was sacked the next year, and the Western Empire fell within the century, but the Antichrist did not come. Like other Fathers, Jerome read the troubles of his own day as the last ones. His mistake was about the hour, not the hope: the Church holds that Christ's coming has been “imminent” since the Ascension, yet that its time is the Father's alone (CCC 673).

Jerome's own restraintFor all his learning, Jerome refused to explain what God has not explained. On the forty-five days, the reason “rests in the knowledge of God.” He names no living ruler as the Antichrist and sets no date in the commentary. Where he gives the view of his school, he says so. This site follows him in that, and the Church goes further still: she names no one and sets no date (CCC 673–675).

For reflection

Can I wait forty-five days more?

Jerome read Daniel's blessing on those who persevere as a blessing on patience: the saints who keep faith even when the Lord seems to delay. Where in my life am I tempted to give up because God has not yet acted? What would it mean to wait for him there, with hope?
