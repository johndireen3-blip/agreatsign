The Temple of His Body

Forty-six years in building, three days to raise up. How the cleansing of the Temple points to the Immaculate Conception, the New Eve, the Woman of Revelation 12, and the risen Christ.

“Destroy this temple and in three days I will raise it up.” … “But he was speaking about the temple of his body.”John 2:19, 21
Begin the studyRead John 2:13–25

Home › The Temple of His Body

About this reflectionWhat follows is my own reflection on John 2. The dating it rests on is not mine. It follows the Jewish historian Josephus and the chronology of Rick Lanser (Associates for Biblical Research), who counts John's “forty-six years” from the start of Herod's work on the Temple in the fall of 20 BC to the Passover of AD 28, the first Passover of Christ's public ministry. What I add is the connection to Mary: Lanser writes only about the chronology, and the link between those forty-six years and her Immaculate Conception is mine. I incorporate his dating here because, read this way, it helps us see how God fulfilled Genesis 3:15 and how we are called to take part in building the Body of Christ. The reflection makes two claims:
  1. The new temple, the body of Christ, began to be built physically at the Immaculate Conception of the Blessed Virgin Mary.
  2. That temple, the Body of Christ, is still being built, and its building requires our participation.
Both will matter again when this site comes to the abomination of desolation (Daniel 9:27; Matthew 24:15).
Fr. John

The Temple of stone

John places the cleansing of the Temple at the start of Christ's ministry, at the first Passover (John 2:13). Jesus drives out the merchants and money-changers and forbids them to make his Father's house a marketplace (John 2:16). When the authorities demand a sign, he gives only one: “Destroy this temple and in three days I will raise it up” (John 2:19). John adds that the disciples understood this only after the Resurrection (John 2:22). The Catechism reads the scene the same way: Jesus identifies himself with the Temple as God's dwelling among us (CCC 583–586).

The Jewish historian Josephus records that Herod the Great began rebuilding the Temple in the eighteenth year of his reign, about 20/19 BC (Antiquities 15.380). Forty-six years later brings us to about AD 27/28, which fits the opening of Christ's public ministry. The number in John 2:20 is not decoration. It is a date.

The living temple

The New Eve and Genesis 3:15

After the Fall, God speaks to the serpent of enmity “between you and the woman” (Genesis 3:15). The Church calls this verse the Protoevangelium, the first announcement of the Gospel, and sees in “the woman” the Mother of the Redeemer (Catechism 410–411).

The Fathers of the Church named her the New Eve. Saint Justin Martyr and Saint Irenaeus taught that as the first Eve, still a virgin, brought death through disobedience, so the Virgin Mary brought life through her obedience. Irenaeus wrote that “the knot of Eve's disobedience was loosed by the obedience of Mary” (Against Heresies 3.22.4). The Second Vatican Council repeats this teaching (Lumen Gentium 56), and so does the Catechism (CCC 494).

The Immaculate Conception: when the New Eve began

The first Eve came from God's hand without sin. If the New Eve was to undo Eve's work, she too had to begin her life untouched by sin. This is the Immaculate Conception, defined by Blessed Pius IX in 1854: from the first instant of her conception, by a singular grace and in view of the merits of Christ, Mary was preserved free from every stain of original sin (Ineffabilis Deus; CCC 490–492). The Church celebrates it on December 8.

The Immaculate Conception is the moment Genesis 3:15 is set in motion. The “woman” who will stand in enmity against the serpent now exists, full of grace (Luke 1:28), and the serpent has no claim on her.

The Church's prayer has long given her the names of the place where God dwells: the Litany of Loreto calls her “Ark of the Covenant” and “House of Gold.”

A meditation on the timing

Here is the connection I propose. I count both temples from their first stone: the Temple of stone from the season Herod's builders began, and the living temple from the first instant of Mary's Immaculate Conception, not from her birth nine months later. Counted that way, the two temples are the same age.

Early Christian tradition holds that Mary was very young, perhaps about fifteen, at the Annunciation. The oldest witness is the second-century Protoevangelium of James, which gives her age when she conceived the Lord as fourteen to seventeen, depending on the manuscript (12.3). It is not Scripture, and the Church does not treat it as a historical record, but it preserves an early Christian memory of Mary's youth. Counted from her own conception, she was about sixteen when Christ was born. Saint Luke tells us that Jesus was “about thirty years of age” when he began his ministry (Luke 3:23), and the cleansing of the Temple is one of its first public acts. So at that Passover, counting each from its beginning:

  • Jesus was about 30;
  • Mary, counted from her Immaculate Conception, was about 16 + 30 = 46;
  • and the Temple of stone had been in building for forty-six years (John 2:20).

Count back forty-six years from about AD 27/28 and we arrive at about 20/19 BC. In the very season that Herod's builders laid the first stones of the Temple of stone, God was creating the New Eve in her Immaculate Conception, the living temple who would give the Son of God his body. The two temples were “in building” side by side for the same forty-six years. When Jesus stands in the Temple of stone and speaks of “the temple of his body” (John 2:21), he speaks of the flesh he received from her.

Was his Mother there? John tells us that just before this Passover she went down to Capernaum with Jesus, his brothers and his disciples (John 2:12). She was with him at Cana (John 2:1–5), she came looking for him during his ministry (Mark 3:31–35), and she stood by the Cross (John 19:25). She was presumably present for much, if not most, of his public ministry, and she may well have been in Jerusalem for this first Passover. The New Eve stands with the disciples who help us understand God's plan for our salvation.

A note on certaintyScripture and the Church do not give the year of Mary's conception or her exact age. This reflection builds on what others have established: the year Herod's builders began, from Josephus; the reckoning of John 2:20 to the Passover of AD 28, as Rick Lanser sets it out; and the Gospel's “about thirty.” To these I add the ancient tradition of Mary's youth (the Protoevangelium of James, which is not Scripture), and I count her age from her conception rather than her birth. That count, and the link between the two forty-six years, are mine; Lanser's article is about chronology alone and does not discuss Mary. It is offered as a reflection, not as Church teaching.

The new temple began in flesh

A temple is a place built for God to dwell in. The Temple of stone took forty-six years and the labor of thousands. The new temple, the body of Christ, would be built from the flesh of one woman. The Church teaches that the Word took flesh from the Virgin Mary at the Annunciation (Luke 1:35; CCC 484–486). My claim is that the building of that temple began earlier, as a house begins with its foundation: at the first instant of Mary's conception, when God formed her body and soul preserved from all sin, so that the flesh the Son would take from her would come from a dwelling entirely his. The Immaculate Conception is not only a privilege given to Mary. It is the first stone of the new temple.

That is why the old Temple's forty-six years matter to me. The stone house and the living house were rising side by side. One would be destroyed in AD 70. The other would be destroyed on the Cross and raised on the third day, never to fall again.

The temple is still being built

The building did not end at the Resurrection. Christ's risen body is complete, but his Body, the Church, is still under construction, and we are its stones.

  • “In him the whole structure is held together and grows into a temple sacred in the Lord; in him you also are being built together into a dwelling place of God in the Spirit” (Ephesians 2:21–22).
  • “Like living stones, let yourselves be built into a spiritual house” (1 Peter 2:5).
  • The gifts of Christ are given “for building up the body of Christ, until we all attain to the unity of faith” (Ephesians 4:12–13).
  • St. Paul even says he is “filling up what is lacking in the afflictions of Christ on behalf of his body, which is the church” (Colossians 1:24).

The Church herself uses this image of a building and a temple (Lumen Gentium 6; CCC 756, 797). God could build without us; he chooses not to. Every prayer, sacrament received, sin repented, sacrifice offered and person brought to Christ is a stone set in place. The building is finished only when the new Jerusalem comes down from heaven “prepared as a bride adorned for her husband” (Revelation 21:2), and then there is no temple in the city, because God and the Lamb are its temple (21:22).

From Cana to Calvary to the Woman of Revelation

The Temple scene comes right after Cana in the same chapter, where Jesus addresses his mother as “Woman” (John 2:4), the title of the woman of Genesis 3:15. He calls her “Woman” again from the Cross (John 19:26), the hour when the temple of his body is destroyed. In Revelation, God's temple in heaven is opened and the ark of his covenant is seen (Revelation 11:19), and at once “a great sign appeared in the sky, a woman clothed with the sun” (Revelation 12:1). The Ark, the Temple and the Woman are drawn together in one vision, and the ancient serpent of Genesis appears again as the dragon (Revelation 12:9).

Why this matters for reading Revelation

When I talk with people about Revelation, I often meet one of two responses.

“It has all happened already”

Some say everything in Revelation was fulfilled long ago, apart from Christ's return, so there is little point studying it. Much of it was fulfilled, and this site marks those passages plainly. But the temple shows why that is not the whole story: Christ's body was raised once for all, yet his Body is still being built. Revelation describes that building and the war waged against it, “against the rest of her offspring” (12:17), which means us. A book about the present life of the Church is not irrelevant to the Church.

“It is too hard to understand”

Others say Revelation matters but is too difficult, so there is nothing they can do with it. Yet the book begins with a promise to ordinary listeners: “Blessed is the one who reads aloud and blessed are those who listen to this prophetic message and heed what is written in it” (1:3). What it asks is not hard to grasp: worship God, repent, keep his commandments, hold to the testimony of Jesus, and endure (12:17; 14:12). You do not need to decode every symbol to be a living stone.

Between those two responses is the one Revelation asks for: to know that the victory is already won, and to take our place in the building that is still going on. For a first reading of the whole book, chapter by chapter, see The Lamb's Victory.

What has been fulfilled, and what remains

Fulfilled

The destruction and raising of the temple of Christ's body (John 2:19–22); the destruction of the Temple of stone in AD 70, foretold by Christ (Matthew 24:1–2).

Unfolding

The Church as the Body of Christ and the temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 3:16; Ephesians 2:19–22), still at war with the dragon, who wages war against “the rest of her offspring” (Revelation 12:17).

Future

The new Jerusalem, where God and the Lamb are themselves the temple (Revelation 21:22).
