Pillar II · Apparitions · Japan, 1973

Our Lady of Akita

In a small convent in the hills outside Akita, in northern Japan, a deaf religious sister said she heard a voice from a wooden statue of Mary. Three messages followed in 1973, calling for prayer and reparation, and over the next years the statue was seen to weep. The local bishop recognized the events in 1984; the Holy See has never given a final judgment.

At a glance

PlaceThe convent of the Handmaids of the Holy Eucharist (Seitai Hoshikai) at Yuzawadai, near Akita City, in the Diocese of Niigata, Japan
DatesThree messages, on July 6, August 3 and October 13, 1973. The statue was reported to weep 101 times, from January 4, 1975 to September 15, 1981.
SeerSr. Agnes Katsuko Sasagawa, a convert from a Buddhist family, who had become completely deaf. She died on August 15, 2024, the Solemnity of the Assumption, at 93.
Church judgmentRecognized by the Bishop of Niigata, John Shojiro Ito, in a pastoral letter of April 22, 1984. He recognized “the supernatural character of a series of mysterious events concerning the statue” and authorized its veneration in his diocese “while awaiting that the Holy See publishes definitive judgment on this matter.” The Holy See has published no such judgment. As of October 2026 the bishop's recognition has not been withdrawn, and the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith has issued no judgment on Akita under the 2024 norms. The details are below.
FeastNone in the universal or Japanese calendar
WeightPrivate revelation with a diocesan recognition only: Catholics may accept it with prudent devotion but are not bound to believe it (CCC 67). Bishop Ito said the same in his letter.

What happened

Agnes Sasagawa had joined the small community at Yuzawadai in 1973, already deaf. On June 12 she reported seeing brilliant rays coming from the tabernacle, and later that month a cross-shaped wound appeared on her hand and bled. On July 6, while praying before the convent's wooden statue of Mary, about three feet tall and carved from a single block of katsura wood, she said she heard a voice from the statue. The same day the sisters noticed drops of blood on the statue's right hand. Later that year they reported that the statue seemed to “sweat,” especially on the forehead and neck.

On January 4, 1975 the statue began to shed tears. Over nearly seven years this was reported 101 times, the last on September 15, 1981, and was seen by many witnesses. Sr. Agnes's own deafness was healed on May 30, 1982, Pentecost Sunday; a doctor's certificate found “no further anomaly in the two ears,” though the doctors themselves did not call the cure miraculous.

The details above follow the account of the community's chaplain, Fr. Teiji Yasuda, and Bishop Ito's 1984 letter. They are reported events, which the bishop weighed; they are not doctrine.

The three messages

The quotations below are from the English text of the messages given by EWTN, which follows the account of Sr. Agnes and Fr. Yasuda (published in English as Akita: The Tears and Message of Mary). There is no text of the messages published by the Holy See. Quotations are kept short.

First message: July 6, 1973Prayer
“Pray very much for the Pope, Bishops, and Priests.”
Content
The voice told Sr. Agnes that her deafness would be healed, and asked her to pray in reparation for the sins of men. It went over with her the community's prayer of offering to the Sacred Heart of Jesus, present in the Eucharist.
Reading
The first word of Akita is a request to pray for the Church's shepherds, and its center is the Eucharist.
Second message: August 3, 1973Reparation
“Many men in this world afflict the Lord.”
Content
The message asks for souls to console the Lord by prayer, sacrifice and offering their daily lives, and warns that the Father is preparing a great chastisement, which prayer and penance may soften.
Reading
This is the language of reparation familiar from Paray-le-Monial and Fatima: love that consoles, offered for others. Its weight falls on what we can do now, not on what will happen.
Third message: October 13, 1973WarningConditional
“As I told you, if men do not repent and better themselves, the Father will inflict a terrible punishment on all humanity.”
Content
The message describes a punishment greater than the flood, in which “fire will fall from the sky and will wipe out a great part of humanity.” It warns that “the work of the devil will infiltrate even into the Church,” with bishops and cardinals set against one another, and it ends with a remedy: “The only arms which will remain for you will be the Rosary and the Sign left by My Son,” and “Each day recite the prayers of the Rosary.”
Read soberly
This is what the message says, and it is serious. But it is a warning, not a forecast. It is conditional (“if men do not repent”), it gives no date, and it points at once to prayer, penance and the Cross. Bishop Ito judged that the messages contain nothing contrary to faith and morals; he did not, and could not, make their images a matter of faith. The Church has never taught that this warning describes a fixed event, and no one may set a date for it (Matthew 24:36).
Why conditional
Biblical prophecy of judgment is often a call to change the outcome. Jonah announced that Nineveh would be destroyed, and when the city repented God relented (Jonah 3). That is the hope Akita places in the Rosary.

What the Holy See did and did not say

Akita is often described as “approved by the Vatican.” That is not accurate, and the history deserves to be told exactly.

DateAct
1975–1976Bishop Ito consults the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith (CDF), which replies that judgment belongs first to the local bishop. A first commission of inquiry (1976) concludes that it is “not in a position to prove the supernatural events.”
1981–1983According to Bishop Ito's own letter, the CDF wrote in 1981 in terms “unfavorable to the events,” a letter he judged to contain “some misunderstandings.” He re-examined the facts and sent a fuller dossier, and in October 1983 three CDF officials decided it should remain under examination.
April 22, 1984Bishop Ito's pastoral letter recognizes the supernatural character of the events concerning the statue, finds nothing contrary to faith and morals, and authorizes veneration in the Diocese of Niigata, explicitly pending a definitive judgment of the Holy See.
June 1988Now retired, Bishop Ito meets Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger, Prefect of the CDF. Some later accounts say the Cardinal approved Akita or found its messages “reliable and worthy of belief.” No decree or document of the CDF exists. According to a UCA News report of September 13, 1988, as later quoted, Bishop Ito himself explained that the Cardinal's “limited remarks only indicated that he did not object to the pastoral letter.”
1990As quoted from 30 Days (April 1990), the Apostolic Nuncio in Japan, Archbishop William Aquin Carew, said that Cardinal Ratzinger “did not give any judgment on the reliability or credibility” of the messages, only that there was no objection to the conclusions of the pastoral letter. Later that year (July–August 1990) the same magazine quoted the president of the Japanese bishops' conference, Archbishop Peter Seiichi Shirayanagi of Tokyo: “The events of Akita are no longer to be taken seriously.”
1999According to the British magazine Christian Order (December 1999), the Apostolic Nuncio, Archbishop Ambrose De Paoli, answering its query at the request of the CDF, wrote: “The Holy See has never given any kind of approval to either the events or messages of Akita.”
Since 2024Under the 2024 norms any new judgment would require the approval of the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith. The Dicastery has published none on Akita. When Sr. Agnes died in August 2024, Catholic news agencies reported that the Vatican has not officially ruled on Akita.

Where things stand. Akita has a diocesan recognition, given in 1984 by the competent local bishop and never revoked by his successors or by Rome. It is not a recognition by the Holy See, which has neither confirmed nor rejected it, and the bishops of Japan have at times been reserved about it. Catholics may venerate Our Lady of Akita and pray with its messages; they should not claim for them more authority than the Church has given.

Akita and the Book of Revelation

Akita does not interpret the Book of Revelation, but several of its images echo it.

  • The woman and the war on her offspring. The great sign of Revelation 12:1 is followed by the dragon's war against “those who keep God's commandments and bear witness to Jesus” (Revelation 12:17). Akita's warning that the devil will work even within the Church is the same struggle seen from inside.
  • Prayers rising like incense. In Revelation an angel offers incense “along with the prayers of all the holy ones” (Revelation 8:3), and only then is the censer, filled with burning coals, “hurled … down to the earth” (Revelation 8:5). Akita, too, sets prayer before judgment and asks that it be offered first.
  • The sign left by her Son. Revelation's victors conquer “by the blood of the Lamb” (Revelation 12:11). Akita's “Sign left by My Son” is the Cross, and the community's prayer centers on his presence in the Eucharist.
  • The call to repent. “Repent, and do the works you did at first” (Revelation 2:5). Like the letters to the seven churches, Akita addresses believers first.
  • Tears wiped away. The statue's tears are not the end of the story: “God will wipe away every tear from their eyes” (Revelation 7:17).
Read with careAkita's third message is often quoted alone, sometimes with dates attached or with claims that it is the hidden content of the third secret of Fatima. Those claims rest on reported private conversations, not on any Church document, and the Holy See published the Fatima secret in full in 2000. Akita sets no dates. Its remedy is the one the Church always gives: prayer, penance, the Rosary and the Cross. Private revelation never adds to the faith (CCC 67).

For reflection

What does she ask of me?

To pray for the Pope, the bishops and priests; to offer my day in reparation; to pray the Rosary each day. A warning taken to heart leads to prayer, not to fear.
