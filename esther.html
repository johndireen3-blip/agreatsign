Prophecy in Esther

A decree goes out to destroy a whole people on a single day. A young queen risks her life to plead for them, and the day of death becomes a feast. Esther is the story of a great reversal, and in its Greek form it opens with a dream of two dragons that Revelation 12 seems to remember.

The book at a glance

Esther is set at the Persian court in Susa. The Jewish orphan Esther, raised by her cousin Mordecai, becomes queen. Haman, the king's chief minister, offended by Mordecai's refusal to bow to him, casts lots to choose a day and obtains a decree to destroy all the Jews in the empire. Esther goes to the king unbidden, exposes Haman, and the Jews are saved; the feast of Purim keeps the memory. The Church receives the book, like Tobit and Judith, as a story told to teach, whatever its exact relation to the events. The book has come down in two forms: a shorter Hebrew text, in which the name of God never appears, and a longer Greek text with six additional passages that speak of God openly and give the prayers of Mordecai and Esther. The Council of Trent received the whole book, Greek additions included, into the canon; the additions are deuterocanonical.

Esther holds no prophecy in the strict sense. It looks forward through type (the queen who intercedes for her people, read by the Church as a figure of Mary) and through image: in the Greek text, a dream of two dragons and a war of the nations against the just, which the book itself interprets and which Revelation echoes. Each card below names which, and says plainly where a reading is the Church's spiritual sense rather than the text's first meaning.

The Greek additions: chapters A–F

The NABRE translates the Hebrew text in the ordinary chapters 1–10 and prints the six Greek additions where they belong in the story, labeled with letters, so that verses are cited as A:1, C:14, F:3 and so on. Older Catholic Bibles followed St. Jerome, who translated the Hebrew and gathered the Greek passages at the end of the book, where they are numbered 10:4–16:24. The same text therefore carries different references in different Bibles; this page uses the NABRE's.

The passages

Esther A:4–10; F:1–6ImageFulfilledUnfolding
“Two great dragons advanced, both poised for combat.” (A:5)
Reading
“This was his dream. There was noise and tumult, thunder and earthquake—confusion upon the earth” (A:4). At the dragons' cry every nation prepares for war, “to fight against the nation of the just” (A:6). The just cry out to God, and from their cry, “as though from a tiny spring, a mighty river, a flood of water” (A:9). “The light of the sun broke forth; the lowly were exalted and they devoured the boastful” (A:10). At the end of the book Mordecai remembers the dream and explains it: “This is the work of God” (F:1). The tiny spring that grew into a river is Esther: “The river is Esther, whom the king married and made queen” (F:3). “The two dragons are myself and Haman” (F:4). “The Lord saved his people and delivered us from all these evils” (F:6).
Fulfilled
Within the book the dream is fulfilled in the deliverance it narrates. Read beside Revelation 12, it is one of the most striking anticipations in the Old Testament. There too “another sign appeared in the sky; it was a huge red dragon” (Revelation 12:3), and “war broke out in heaven” (12:7). There too a woman stands at the center, clothed with the sun, and a flood of water: “The serpent, however, spewed a torrent of water out of his mouth after the woman to sweep her away with the current,” but “the earth helped the woman” (12:15–16). In Esther the river is the woman who saves; in Revelation the river is the serpent's weapon against her, and it fails.
Unfolding
The dragon, foiled, goes off “to wage war against the rest of her offspring” (Revelation 12:17). The Greek Esther already names the pattern: the nations gathered “to fight against the nation of the just,” the cry of the lowly, and God's answer. The same dragon in Revelation is the ancient serpent, and in Esther's dream one dragon is Mordecai: the image is not yet fixed on Satan, which is why this page calls the link an echo and not a prophecy in the strict sense. See Revelation 12.
Esther 4:14–16; C:12–30TypeFulfilledUnfolding
“Who knows—perhaps it was for a time like this that you became queen?” (4:14)
Reading
Anyone who goes to the king unbidden may be put to death, and Esther hesitates. Mordecai answers that “relief and deliverance will come to the Jews from another source” (4:14) if she stays silent; it is the Hebrew text's quiet way of speaking of God without naming him. Esther asks the people to “fast on my behalf, all of you, not eating or drinking night or day for three days,” and decides: “If I perish, I perish!” (4:16). In the Greek addition she flees to the Lord and prays: “My Lord, you alone are our King. Help me, who am alone and have no help but you” (C:14). She asks, “Put in my mouth persuasive words in the presence of the lion” (C:24), and, “Save us by your power” (C:25). The king receives her: “What is it, Queen Esther? What is your request? Even if it is half of my kingdom, it shall be granted you” (5:3). The Church reads Esther's prayer at Mass on Thursday of the First Week of Lent, with the Gospel “Ask and it will be given to you” (Matthew 7:7).
Fulfilled
Christian tradition has long seen in Esther a figure of Mary: the daughter of God's people raised to be queen, who uses her place not for herself but to plead for her people before the king. At Cana Mary speaks for others before they ask: “They have no wine” (John 2:3). The Catechism names Esther among the holy women who prepared for Mary's mission (CCC 489) and kept alive the hope of Israel's salvation, of whom “the purest figure” is Mary (CCC 64). This is the Church's typological reading; the book itself speaks of Esther and of the Jews of Persia.
Unfolding
The difference is as important as the likeness. Esther feared the king and risked death; Mary intercedes before a Son who loves those she prays for, and her prayer, like that of all the saints, depends wholly on his one mediation (CCC 970). Her care for her children continues in heaven (CCC 969).
Esther 9TypeFulfilledFuture
On the day their enemies had hoped to overpower them, “the situation was reversed: the Jews overpowered those who hated them.” (9:1)
Reading
The day Haman chose by lot for destruction becomes the day of deliverance. Haman is impaled “on the stake he had set up for Mordecai” (7:10), and Mordecai is raised to Haman's place. The Jews keep the days as a feast, the month “which was turned for them from sorrow into joy, from mourning into celebration” (9:22). “And so these days have been named Purim after the word pur” (9:26), the lot Haman cast (3:7). “These days were to be commemorated and kept in every generation” (9:28). The fighting of chapter 9 belongs to its time; the lesson the book draws from it is that God does not abandon his people.
Fulfilled
The Gospel is the great reversal. Mary sings of God who “has thrown down the rulers from their thrones but lifted up the lowly” (Luke 1:52). On the Cross the enemy's plan to destroy becomes the means of salvation, and Jesus promises the disciples, “you will grieve, but your grief will become joy” (John 16:20).
Future
The last reversal is still to come: “He will wipe every tear from their eyes, and there shall be no more death or mourning, wailing or pain” (Revelation 21:4).
