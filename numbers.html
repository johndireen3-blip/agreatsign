Prophecy in Numbers

Forty years in the wilderness: a people who grumble, rebel and are fed anyway. In the middle of the journey stand a serpent lifted on a pole and a star seen far off, and Jesus and the Gospels take up both.

The book at a glance

Numbers takes its English name from the two censuses of Israel (chapters 1 and 26). It follows the people from Sinai (1–10) through the wilderness, where a whole generation refuses to enter the promised land and is sentenced to forty years of wandering (11–25), to the plains of Moab across the Jordan, where a new generation is counted and prepared (26–36). Jewish and Christian tradition associate it with Moses; scholars see in it priestly and older narrative material joined over a long history. The Church reads it as inspired Scripture either way.

Numbers holds one prophecy in the strict sense: Balaam's oracle of a star and a scepter (24:17). Most of what looks forward here is type: the manna, the water from the rock and the bronze serpent, which Jesus and St. Paul read as figures of Christ, and which the Catechism names among the Old Testament figures of salvation (CCC 1094, 2130). St. Paul says of the desert years: “These things happened to them as an example, and they have been written down as a warning to us” (1 Corinthians 10:11). Each card below names which.

The passages

Numbers 6:22–27PromiseFulfilledUnfolding
The LORD bless you and keep you! The LORD let his face shine upon you, and be gracious to you! The LORD look upon you kindly and give you peace! (6:24–26)
Reading
This is liturgy, not prediction: the blessing God gives Aaron and his sons to speak over Israel. It ends with a promise: “So shall they invoke my name upon the Israelites, and I will bless them” (6:27). The Church still prays it, and reads it at Mass on January 1, the Solemnity of Mary, Mother of God.
Fulfilled
The prayer that God's face would shine upon his people is answered in Christ. Paul speaks of “the knowledge of the glory of God on the face of [Jesus] Christ” (2 Corinthians 4:6), and the risen Lord's first word to his disciples is peace (John 20:19).
Unfolding
The blessing is still given, at the end of Mass and over people and homes. Its last fulfillment is the vision promised in Revelation: “They will look upon his face” (Revelation 22:4).
Numbers 11:4–9, 31–34TypeFulfilledFuture
“But now we are famished; we have nothing to look forward to but this manna.” (11:6)
Reading
The people remember the fish, the cucumbers and the melons of Egypt and despise the bread God gives them each morning. God sends quail, “a wind from the LORD that drove in quail from the sea” (11:31), but the meat they craved becomes a plague. The gift itself had never failed; the people's hearts had.
Fulfilled
Jesus reads the manna as a sign of himself: “it was not Moses who gave the bread from heaven; my Father gives you the true bread from heaven” (John 6:32). “Your ancestors ate the manna in the desert, but they died”; “I am the living bread that came down from heaven; whoever eats this bread will live forever” (6:49, 51). The Catechism teaches that the “manna in the desert prefigured the Eucharist” (CCC 1094).
Future
“To the victor I shall give some of the hidden manna” (Revelation 2:17).
For now
Numbers is honest that God's people can grow tired of his gifts. Paul warns the Corinthians against the same craving (1 Corinthians 10:6).
Numbers 11:24–29PromiseFulfilled
“Are you jealous for my sake? If only all the people of the LORD were prophets! If only the LORD would bestow his spirit on them!” (11:29)
Reading
God takes some of the spirit that was on Moses and shares it with seventy elders, and they prophesy. Two men who stayed in the camp, Eldad and Medad, prophesy too, and Joshua wants them stopped. Moses answers with a wish that reaches far beyond his own day.
Fulfilled
The prophet Joel turned Moses' wish into a promise (Joel 3:1–2), and at Pentecost Peter announces it kept: “‘that I will pour out a portion of my spirit upon all flesh. Your sons and your daughters shall prophesy’” (Acts 2:17). Jesus answers his own disciples much as Moses answered Joshua, when they try to stop a man driving out demons in his name (Mark 9:38–40).
Numbers 20:1–13TypeFulfilled
“Then, raising his hand, Moses struck the rock twice with his staff, and water came out in abundance…” (20:11)
Reading
At Meribah the people again have no water. God tells Moses to “command the rock to yield its waters” (20:8), but Moses strikes it twice in anger. Water flows anyway, and Moses and Aaron are told they will not lead the people into the land. A similar story is told in Exodus 17.
Fulfilled
St. Paul says that in the desert Israel “drank from a spiritual rock that followed them, and the rock was the Christ” (1 Corinthians 10:4). The Catechism: “Water from the rock was the figure of the spiritual gifts of Christ” (CCC 1094). Jesus himself offers the water: “Let anyone who thirsts come to me and drink” (John 7:37). From his side on the Cross, opened by a lance, flow blood and water (John 19:34), which the Fathers read as the sacraments of the Church.
See also
Revelation 22:1, “the river of life-giving water.”
Numbers 21:4–9TypeFulfilled
“Accordingly Moses made a bronze serpent and mounted it on a pole, and whenever the serpent bit someone, the person looked at the bronze serpent and recovered.” (21:9)
Reading
The people complain, “We are disgusted with this wretched food!” (21:5), and are bitten by “seraph serpents” (21:6). God's remedy is strange: the image of the very thing that is killing them, lifted up for all to see. The Book of Wisdom already understood that it was not the bronze that healed: the one who turned toward it “was saved, not by what was seen, but by you, the savior of all” (Wisdom 16:7). Centuries later King Hezekiah broke the serpent in pieces, because the people had begun to burn incense to it (2 Kings 18:4).
Fulfilled
Jesus himself gives the reading: “And just as Moses lifted up the serpent in the desert, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, so that everyone who believes in him may have eternal life” (John 3:14–15). On the Cross Christ, who knew no sin, is lifted up bearing the image of what destroys us, and whoever looks to him in faith lives. The Catechism names the bronze serpent among the images God permitted in the Old Covenant that pointed toward salvation by the incarnate Word (CCC 2130). The Church reads this passage on the feast of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross, September 14.
Numbers 24:15–19ProphecyFulfilledFuture
“I see him, though not now; I observe him, though not near: A star shall advance from Jacob, and a scepter shall rise from Israel…” (24:17)
Reading
Balaam, a foreign seer hired by the king of Moab to curse Israel, can only bless it. His fourth oracle looks far ahead to a ruler from Israel. In its first horizon it points to David, who conquered Moab and Edom (2 Samuel 8:2, 14). Jewish tradition before Christ already read it of the Messiah, and the Church has always read it so.
Fulfilled
The magi come from the east asking, “Where is the newborn king of the Jews? We saw his star at its rising and have come to do him homage” (Matthew 2:2), and the NABRE note on that verse points to Numbers 24:17. Origen connected the magi with Balaam's prophecy (Against Celsus 1.60): the first to follow the star were, like Balaam, seers from the east.
Future
At the end of the Bible the star speaks for himself: “I am the root and offspring of David, the bright morning star” (Revelation 22:16). He promises a share in his light to the faithful: “to him I will give the morning star” (2:28).
Numbers 22–25; 31:16ImageUnfolding
The Midianite women “on Balaam's advice were behind the Israelites' unfaithfulness to the LORD in the affair at Peor.” (31:16)
Reading
The man who spoke the star oracle did not end well. Chapter 25 tells how Israel was drawn into idolatry and immorality at Baal of Peor, and chapter 31 lays it at Balaam's door. He could not curse God's people, so he taught their enemies how to lead them astray.
Unfolding
The New Testament remembers Balaam as the type of the religious teacher for hire (2 Peter 2:15; Jude 11). The risen Christ warns the church at Pergamum: “You have some people there who hold to the teaching of Balaam, who instructed Balak to put a stumbling block before the Israelites: to eat food sacrificed to idols and to play the harlot” (Revelation 2:14). The danger is not a curse from outside but compromise from within, and it remains a warning for the Church in every age.

Numbers 27:15–23TypeFulfilled
“Take Joshua, son of Nun, a man of spirit, and lay your hand upon him.” (27:18)
Reading
Moses, told that he will not enter the land, asks God for a leader “that the LORD's community may not be like sheep without a shepherd” (27:17). God names Joshua, whose name Moses had given him (Hoshea, “salvation,” became Joshua, “the LORD saves”; 13:16). Moses lays hands on him before the priest and the people, and Joshua leads Israel into the promised land.
Fulfilled
In Greek, Joshua and Jesus are the same name, and Hebrews plays on it: “Now if Joshua had given them rest, he would not have spoken afterwards of another day” (Hebrews 4:8). The angel tells Joseph to name the child Jesus, “because he will save his people from their sins” (Matthew 1:21). Seeing the crowds, Jesus' heart “was moved with pity for them, for they were like sheep without a shepherd” (Mark 6:34). The Fathers saw in Joshua a figure of Jesus, who leads his people where Moses, the law, could not.
See also
The laying on of hands to hand on a charge is continued in the Church (Acts 6:6; 1 Timothy 4:14).
Numbers 35:9–34TypeFulfilledMystery
The one who killed unintentionally “will stay there until the death of the high priest who has been anointed with sacred oil.” (35:25)
Reading
Six cities are set apart as places of asylum, open to Israelites and to “resident or transient aliens” alike (35:15), where someone who has killed by accident can flee from the avenger of blood. The refugee is safe only inside the city, and goes free only when the high priest dies (35:28).
Fulfilled
Many Christian interpreters, from the Fathers on, have read the cities of refuge as a figure of Christ, in whom sinners find shelter, and have seen in the death of the high priest that sets the refugee free a figure of the death of Christ, the high priest. Hebrews may echo the image when it speaks of “we who have taken refuge” holding fast to the hope set before us, “an anchor of the soul, sure and firm” (Hebrews 6:18–19).
Why mystery
No New Testament text names the cities of refuge, and the echo in Hebrews is not certain. The type is a traditional Christian reading, offered here as such.

Numbers and Revelation

The desert journey of Numbers returns in the last book, where the Church is a pilgrim people on its way to the promised land.
