Prophecy in Luke

Luke's Gospel begins and ends with prophecy. Angels, Mary, Zechariah and Simeon speak in the voice of Israel's prophets, and the risen Jesus closes the book by opening the Scriptures: everything written about him must be fulfilled. In between, Luke alone names the armies around Jerusalem and the “times of the Gentiles.”

The book at a glance

Tradition attributes the third Gospel to Luke, the companion of Paul; Irenaeus writes that Luke set down in a book the Gospel Paul preached (Against Heresies 3.1.1). It is the first of two volumes, continued in Acts, and is addressed to Theophilus so that he may know “the certainty” of what he was taught (1:4). Most scholars hold that Luke drew on Mark; he also has material of his own, including the infancy narratives and many parables.

Luke's prophecy has a shape. The first two chapters are full of promises spoken over the child and his mother. In the middle, Jesus reads Isaiah and says it is fulfilled “today.” On the road and in the city, he weeps over Jerusalem and foretells its fall. At the end, he shows that the whole of Scripture led to his cross and resurrection, and that the mission to all nations will begin from Jerusalem.

Key to the labels

What kind of passage it is

Prophecy God's word through a prophet, or Christ, about what will come.

Promise A pledge God makes, kept over generations.

Type A person or event that foreshadows Christ or the Church.

Image A picture Scripture returns to and completes at the end.

Apocalypse A vision unveiling history from heaven's side, in symbols.

The passages

Luke 1:32–33PromiseFulfilledFuture
“…and he will rule over the house of Jacob forever, and of his kingdom there will be no end.”
Reading
Gabriel's words to Mary take up God's promise to David that his throne would stand forever (2 Samuel 7:12–16; Isaiah 9:6). The child will be “Son of the Most High” and receive “the throne of David his father” (1:32). When Jesus enters Jerusalem the Catechism names him the son of David (CCC 559), and the Annunciation as the beginning of the fulfillment of God's promises (CCC 484).
Why two labels
The King has come and reigns now from the right hand of the Father. His kingdom “without end” is fully revealed only at his return (CCC 671), the phrase the Creed itself repeats.
See also
The woman's son “destined to rule all the nations” in Revelation 12.
Luke 1:46–55ProphecyUnfolding
“For he has looked upon his handmaid’s lowliness; behold, from now on will all ages call me blessed.”
Reading
Mary's song is a prophecy in the line of Hannah's (1 Samuel 2:1–10). She speaks of God's mercy “from age to age” and of a great reversal: “He has thrown down the rulers from their thrones but lifted up the lowly” (1:52). The Catechism opens its teaching on devotion to Mary with this verse (CCC 971).
Why unfolding
“From now on” is still running. Each generation that calls Mary blessed, at every Hail Mary and every Magnificat sung at Vespers, adds to its fulfillment. The reversal she sings is the pattern of the kingdom (4:18; 6:20–26).
Luke 1:68–79ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“…because of the tender mercy of our God by which the daybreak from on high will visit us to shine on those who sit in darkness and death’s shadow…”
Reading
At his son's circumcision, “Zechariah his father, filled with the holy Spirit, prophesied” (1:67). The first half of his song blesses God, who “has raised up a horn for our salvation within the house of David his servant” (1:69), remembering the covenant and the oath sworn to Abraham. The second half turns to the child: “And you, child, will be called prophet of the Most High, for you will go before the Lord to prepare his ways” (1:76). John goes before; the one who comes after him is the daybreak. Citing this verse, the Catechism calls John the prophet of the Most High, the Lord's immediate precursor (CCC 523).
Why two labels
Fulfilled when John prepared the way and Christ, the light promised to a people in darkness (Isaiah 9:1), came. In the liturgy the Church sings the Benedictus every morning at Lauds, so that the daybreak from on high is welcomed again each day. Its last line is still a prayer for us: “to guide our feet into the path of peace” (1:79).
Luke 2:29–35ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“Behold, this child is destined for the fall and rise of many in Israel, and to be a sign that will be contradicted (and you yourself a sword will pierce).”
Reading
In the Temple, Simeon holds the child and sees “a light for revelation to the Gentiles, and glory for your people Israel” (2:32), words from Isaiah's Servant (Isaiah 42:6; 49:6). Then he turns to Mary. The Catechism reads the Presentation as Simeon and Anna's recognition of the Messiah and Simeon's words as foretelling the Cross and Mary's share in it (CCC 529).
Why two labels
The sword pierced at Calvary (John 19:25). The light to the Gentiles is still going out, and Jesus is still a sign that is contradicted (CCC 575).
See also
The woman in labor and the child opposed by the dragon in Revelation 12.
Luke 3:7–17ProphecyFulfilledFuture
“He will baptize you with the holy Spirit and fire.”
Reading
John's preaching is urgent: “Even now the ax lies at the root of the trees” (3:9). To the crowds, the tax collectors and the soldiers he gives plain counsel: share what you have, take no more than is owed, extort from no one. Then he points beyond himself to one mightier who is coming. The fire in his words is both gift and judgment. The Catechism, citing this verse, reads the fire as a symbol of the transforming energy of the Holy Spirit (CCC 696). John adds the image of the threshing floor: “His winnowing fan is in his hand to clear his threshing floor and to gather the wheat into his barn, but the chaff he will burn with unquenchable fire” (3:17).
Why two labels
The baptism with the Holy Spirit was given at Pentecost, when tongues as of fire rested on the disciples (Acts 1:5; 2:3). The winnowing belongs to the judgment at the end, which, as in the parable of the weeds, is left to the Lord (Matthew 13:30).
Luke 4:16–21ProphecyFulfilled
“Today this scripture passage is fulfilled in your hearing.”
Reading
In the synagogue at Nazareth Jesus reads Isaiah 61:1–2: “The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to bring glad tidings to the poor.” Then he sits down and says one sentence. The Anointed One of Isaiah is standing in the room. He stops before Isaiah's “day of vindication” and reads only the “year acceptable to the Lord.” The Catechism cites this text in teaching that the kingdom belongs to the poor and lowly (CCC 544).
Note
“Today” runs through Luke: to the shepherds (2:11), to Zacchaeus (19:9), to the thief on the cross (23:43).
Luke 10:17–20ApocalypseFulfilledUnfolding
“I have observed Satan fall like lightning from the sky.”
Reading
The seventy-two (some manuscripts read seventy) return from their mission rejoicing: “Lord, even the demons are subject to us because of your name” (10:17). Jesus answers with what he has seen. Their small victories are signs of a greater one: Satan's power is broken. The Catechism teaches that “The coming of God's kingdom means the defeat of Satan's,” a defeat won above all on the Cross (CCC 550; John 12:31).
Why two labels
The fall is real and has begun, but the struggle goes on until the end. Revelation shows the dragon thrown down from heaven and still waging war on the rest of the woman's offspring (Revelation 12:9, 17). And Jesus turns their joy to its true ground: “Nevertheless, do not rejoice because the spirits are subject to you, but rejoice because your names are written in heaven” (10:20).
See also
The Woman Clothed with the Sun.
Luke 13:22–30ProphecyUnfoldingFuture
“And people will come from the east and the west and from the north and the south and will recline at table in the kingdom of God.”
Reading
On the road to Jerusalem someone asks, “Lord, will only a few people be saved?” (13:23). Jesus gives no number. He gives a summons: “Strive to enter through the narrow door, for many, I tell you, will attempt to enter but will not be strong enough” (13:24). The door will be shut, and some who ate and drank in his company will be left outside, while Abraham, Isaac, Jacob and all the prophets are in the kingdom with guests from every direction. The Catechism teaches that everyone is called to enter the kingdom, which was announced first to Israel and is intended for people of all nations (CCC 543), and it reads the warning of the narrow gate as an urgent call to conversion (CCC 1036; Matthew 7:13–14).
Why two labels
The gathering from east and west is under way in the Church's mission. The feast itself is still ahead, the wedding feast of the Lamb (Revelation 19:9). And it holds a reversal: “For behold, some are last who will be first, and some are first who will be last” (13:30).
Luke 17:20–37ProphecyUnfoldingFuture
“For behold, the kingdom of God is among you.”
Reading
Asked when the kingdom will come, Jesus says it “cannot be observed” by calculation. It is already present in him. The NABRE note explains that the Greek can also mean “within you,” but prefers “among” in light of Luke's other sayings (10:9, 11; 11:20). He then speaks to the disciples of “the days of the Son of Man”: sudden as lightning, unlooked for as in the days of Noah and Lot. “But first he must suffer greatly and be rejected by this generation” (17:25).
Why two labels
The kingdom is present and growing now. The day the Son of Man is revealed (17:30) is still to come, and no one can point to it: “Look, here it is” is the cry Jesus tells his disciples not to follow (17:21; compare 17:23).
Note
Some read “one will be taken, the other left” (17:34–35) as a secret rapture before Christ's return. The Catholic reading, like most of the Christian tradition, sees one visible return of Christ at the end; the disciples' question “Where, Lord?” receives no map, only a proverb (17:37). See The Lamb's Victory on the rapture.
Luke 18:1–8ProphecyMystery
“But when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on earth?”
Reading
The parable of the widow and the unjust judge is “about the necessity for them to pray always without becoming weary” (18:1). If even a corrupt judge gives way to persistence, how much more will God secure the rights of his chosen ones who call out to him day and night. “I tell you, he will see to it that justice is done for them speedily” (18:8). Then comes a question, and no answer is given. The Catechism reads the parable as a lesson in praying always with the patience of faith, and repeats the question (CCC 2613). It also cites this verse in teaching that before Christ's second coming the Church must pass through a final trial that will shake the faith of many believers (CCC 675).
Why mystery
Jesus asks; he does not say how many will be found faithful. The question follows directly on his words about the day of the Son of Man (17:20–37), and it is put to each disciple. The answer is given in prayer.
Luke 19:11–27ProphecyFuture
“A nobleman went off to a distant country to obtain the kingship for himself and then to return.”
Reading
Luke says why Jesus told this parable: he was near Jerusalem “and they thought that the kingdom of God would appear there immediately” (19:11). So he tells of a nobleman who goes away to receive a kingdom and come back. He gives ten servants a gold coin each (in Greek, a mina): “Engage in trade with these until I return” (19:13). Some of his citizens send word after him that they do not want him as king. Luke's first hearers may have remembered Archelaus, son of Herod, who went to Rome to obtain his kingship while a delegation of his subjects opposed him (Josephus, Jewish Antiquities, book 17).
Why future
The King has gone to receive his kingdom, and his servants trade in his absence. His return and the reckoning are still ahead; the parable corrects the expectation that the end would come at once. Right after it, Jesus enters Jerusalem and is acclaimed as king (19:38). Compare the talents in Matthew 25:14–30.
Luke 19:41–44ProphecyFulfilled
“If this day you only knew what makes for peace—but now it is hidden from your eyes.”
Reading
As the crowd cheers his entry, Jesus sees the city and weeps. He foretells a siege wall, encirclement, and that the enemy “will not leave one stone upon another,” because the city “did not recognize the time of your visitation” (19:44). The Romans did exactly this in AD 70. The Catechism cites his tears over Jerusalem (CCC 558).
Note
This is a lament, not a curse. Jesus' last word over the city is grief. The same Gospel ends with the mission “beginning from Jerusalem” (24:47).
Luke 21:20–24ProphecyFulfilledMystery
“When you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies, know that its desolation is at hand.”
Reading
Where Matthew and Mark speak of “the desolating abomination,” Luke names what his readers would see: armies around the city. This is the key that unlocks the parallel; see the Matthew page on the desolating abomination. Those in Judea are to flee to the mountains. Eusebius records that the Jerusalem church, warned by a revelation, left the city before the war and settled at Pella beyond the Jordan (Church History 3.5.3).
Why two labels
The siege, fall and exile were fulfilled in AD 70. But Jesus adds: “Jerusalem will be trampled underfoot by the Gentiles until the times of the Gentiles are fulfilled” (21:24). The Catechism cites this verse in teaching that Christ's glorious coming awaits Israel's recognition of the Messiah, following “the full number of the Gentiles” (CCC 674; Romans 11:25). How long those times run, and how they end, the Church has not said.
Luke 21:25–28ApocalypseFuture
“But when these signs begin to happen, stand erect and raise your heads because your redemption is at hand.”
Reading
Signs in the sun, moon and stars, nations in dismay, the powers of heaven shaken: then “they will see the Son of Man coming in a cloud with power and great glory” (21:27). The Catechism cites this verse for Christ's reign revealed in glory at his return (CCC 671).
For now
Where others “die of fright” (21:26), disciples stand up and lift their heads. Luke's version of the discourse ends with the same counsel as Mark's: be vigilant and pray (21:36).
Luke 22:28–30PromiseUnfoldingFuture
“It is you who have stood by me in my trials; and I confer a kingdom on you, just as my Father has conferred one on me…”
Reading
At the Last Supper, right after the dispute over which of them is the greatest (22:24–27), Jesus gives the apostles a share in his own kingdom, “that you may eat and drink at my table in my kingdom; and you will sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel” (22:30). The Catechism cites these words in teaching that Jesus gave the Twelve a share in his authority and mission (CCC 551).
Why two labels
The apostles exercise this authority now in the Church built on them, and, as the Church reads it, the table is already set at every Eucharist. The thrones and the judgment are fully revealed at the end: the wall of the new Jerusalem has twelve foundations bearing the names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb (Revelation 21:14; compare Matthew 19:28).
Luke 22:31–32PromiseFulfilledUnfolding
“…but I have prayed that your own faith may not fail; and once you have turned back, you must strengthen your brothers.”
Reading
“Simon, Simon, behold Satan has demanded to sift all of you like wheat” (22:31). The NABRE note explains that “you” here is plural in Greek: the trial is for all the apostles. But Jesus has prayed for Peter, so that he can strengthen the others. Within hours Peter denies him three times (22:54–62), weeps, and turns back; on Easter the risen Lord appears to him (24:34; 1 Corinthians 15:5). The Catechism reads in this verse Peter's mission to keep the faith from every lapse and to strengthen his brothers in it (CCC 552).
Why two labels
Fulfilled in Peter's repentance and his leadership in Acts. The First Vatican Council cited the same verse for the ministry of Peter's successors (Pastor Aeternus, chapter 4), so the Church sees this prayer still at work.
Luke 24:25–27, 44–47ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“Everything written about me in the law of Moses and in the prophets and psalms must be fulfilled.”
Reading
On the road to Emmaus the risen Jesus asks, “Was it not necessary that the Messiah should suffer these things and enter into his glory?” (24:26), and “beginning with Moses and all the prophets” he interprets what referred to him. In the upper room he opens the disciples' minds to the whole of Scripture. The Catechism takes this as Jesus' own key to reading the Old Testament (CCC 572). It is the key this site uses.
Why two labels
The suffering and rising are fulfilled. The last line of the prophecy is still being fulfilled: repentance for the forgiveness of sins “would be preached in his name to all the nations, beginning from Jerusalem” (24:47). Its continuation is the Book of Acts.
Luke 24:49PromiseFulfilled
“And [behold] I am sending the promise of my Father upon you; but stay in the city until you are clothed with power from on high.”
Reading
The risen Jesus' last instruction in Luke: “You are witnesses of these things” (24:48), but first they must wait. “The promise of my Father” is the Holy Spirit, as Luke's second volume makes plain (Acts 1:4–5, 8). Then “he parted from them and was taken up to heaven” (24:51).
Why fulfilled
At Pentecost the Spirit came on the disciples gathered in Jerusalem (Acts 2:1–4), after days of prayer with Mary the mother of Jesus (Acts 1:14). The Catechism teaches that on that day Christ's Passover is fulfilled in the outpouring of the Holy Spirit (CCC 731).
