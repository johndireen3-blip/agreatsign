Questions people ask

Is There a Rapture?

Not in the sense many people mean. Catholics believe that when Christ returns in glory, the faithful will be “caught up together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air” (1 Thessalonians 4:17). But the Church does not teach a secret rapture that takes believers away before a time of tribulation. There is one return of Christ, visible to all, at the end.

The short answerYes, the faithful will be “caught up” to meet Christ, as St. Paul says. No, this is not a separate, hidden coming before the final trial. The Catholic Church teaches one glorious, public return of Christ at the end of history, with the resurrection of the dead and the judgment (CCC 681–682). The Church will not be removed before the last trial; she will pass through it with her Lord (CCC 675–677).

Where the word comes from

“Rapture” is not a word in English Bibles. It comes from the Latin Vulgate's rapiemur, “we shall be caught up,” in 1 Thessalonians 4:17. The NABRE's own note on that verse explains the origin of the term and observes that it has been joined to other passages of the Gospels and Revelation in a dispensationalist scheme of the end. So the question is not whether believers are “caught up,” but when.

Where the pre-tribulation rapture comes from

Many Evangelical Christians hold what is called the pre-tribulation rapture. In this view Christ comes first for his Church, unseen by the world, and takes believers to heaven. A tribulation of about seven years follows on earth, under the Antichrist. Then Christ returns visibly with his saints and reigns on earth for a thousand years. The framework is called dispensational premillennialism.

This reading took shape in the 1830s. Its best-known early teacher was John Nelson Darby (1800–1882), a former Anglican clergyman and leader among the Plymouth Brethren, who developed it in these years, when it was discussed at the prophecy conferences held at Powerscourt in Ireland (1831–1833), and spread it in many writings afterward. It spread in America through the Scofield Reference Bible, edited by C. I. Scofield and published by Oxford University Press in 1909, whose notes carried the scheme into millions of homes. Later it reached a wide public through Hal Lindsey's The Late Great Planet Earth (1970) and the Left Behind novels of Tim LaHaye and Jerry B. Jenkins (1995–2007).

Those who hold this view are fellow Christians who love Scripture and long for the Lord's coming, and that longing is good. But the view is fairly recent, and most Christians, Catholic, Orthodox and many Protestant, read these texts as describing one return of Christ.

What Scripture says

1 Thessalonians 4:16–17. Paul writes to a grieving church about its dead. “For the Lord himself, with a word of command, with the voice of an archangel and with the trumpet of God, will come down from heaven, and the dead in Christ will rise first. Then we who are alive, who are left, will be caught up together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air. Thus we shall always be with the Lord.” This is not a quiet scene: a command, an archangel's voice, God's trumpet, the dead rising. St. John Chrysostom explains the “meeting” with an image his hearers knew: “For when a king drives into a city, those who are in honor go out to meet him; but the condemned await the judge within.” And again: “as He descends, we go forth to meet Him” (Homilies on 1 Thessalonians, 8). See 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18.

1 Corinthians 15:51–52. “Behold, I tell you a mystery. We shall not all fall asleep, but we will all be changed, in an instant, in the blink of an eye, at the last trumpet.” The “last trumpet” is the same trumpet of 1 Thessalonians: the resurrection of the dead on the last day (CCC 1001). See 1 Corinthians 15:51–57.

Matthew 24:40–41. “Two men will be out in the field; one will be taken, and one will be left. Two women will be grinding at the mill; one will be taken, and one will be left.” These verses are often read as a secret rapture. Read in context, they describe the visible coming. Jesus has just said, “For just as lightning comes from the east and is seen as far as the west, so will the coming of the Son of Man be” (24:27), and “they will see the Son of Man coming upon the clouds of heaven with power and great glory” (24:30). The image that comes right before is Noah's flood: “They did not know until the flood came and carried them all away. So will it be [also] at the coming of the Son of Man” (24:39). Whether “taken” means taken to the Lord or taken in judgment, the point is suddenness, and the lesson follows at once: “Therefore, stay awake! For you do not know on which day your Lord will come” (24:42). See Matthew 24:29–31 and Luke 17:20–37.

The angels at the Ascension said the same: “This Jesus who has been taken up from you into heaven will return in the same way as you have seen him going into heaven” (Acts 1:11).

What the Church teaches

The Catechism treats Christ's return in paragraphs 668–682 (668–677, 678–679, 680–682). Christ already reigns, yet “Though already present in his Church, Christ's reign is nevertheless yet to be fulfilled ‘with power and great glory’” at his return (CCC 671). That return can come at any time: “Since the Ascension Christ's coming in glory has been imminent,” though its hour belongs to the Father (CCC 673).

Before it, “the Church must pass through a final trial that will shake the faith of many believers” (CCC 675). She is not lifted out of that trial: “The Church will enter the glory of the kingdom only through this final Passover, when she will follow her Lord in his death and Resurrection” (CCC 677). Then comes the one return of the Lord: “On Judgement Day at the end of the world, Christ will come in glory to achieve the definitive triumph of good over evil” (CCC 681). The resurrection belongs to that same day, for “the resurrection of the dead is closely associated with Christ's Parousia” (CCC 1001), and the Catechism quotes 1 Thessalonians 4:16 to show it.

So the Catholic answer keeps everything the rapture teaching rightly prizes, the meeting in the air and being “always with the Lord,” and places it where Paul does: at the glorious coming of Christ. St. John Henry Newman, gathering the teaching of the Fathers, described the last persecution as a trial the Church herself will endure (Newman on the Antichrist).

How to live it

The hope of being caught up to meet the Lord is meant to comfort, not to frighten. Paul ends the passage, “console one another with these words” (1 Thessalonians 4:18). He then asks for readiness rather than calculation: “let us stay alert and sober” (1 Thessalonians 5:6), because “God did not destine us for wrath, but to gain salvation through our Lord Jesus Christ” (1 Thessalonians 5:9). Jesus promises the same to those who endure the trial: “But the one who perseveres to the end will be saved” (Matthew 24:13).

The surest way to be ready to meet the Lord in the air is to meet him now, in the Eucharist, in his word, and in the least of his brothers and sisters (Matthew 25:40). On the final trial, see Who Is the Antichrist? and What Is the Great Apostasy?; for the thousand years that dispensationalism places after the return, see the thousand-year reign.

Where this site standsThis site sets no date for Christ's return, for “it is not for you to know times or seasons which the Father has fixed by his own authority” (CCC 673). It names no living person as a fulfillment of prophecy. Private revelations, however well known, are kept in their place: “It is not their role to improve or complete Christ's definitive Revelation” (CCC 67). Our hope is in the Lord who is coming, and who is already with us.
