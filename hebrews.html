Prophecy in Hebrews

Hebrews reads the whole Old Testament as a promise kept in Christ: the Son who is God's last word, the priest forever like Melchizedek, the new covenant Jeremiah foretold. Yet it is a letter for pilgrims. Christ has entered the true sanctuary, and his people still walk toward “the city with foundations.”

The book at a glance

Hebrews is more a sermon than a letter, written to Christians tempted by weariness to drift from their faith. It names no author. The Church of Alexandria received it as Paul's by the end of the second century; the West doubted this until the fourth century, and the canon lists, the Council of Trent among them, then counted it among Paul's fourteen letters. Origen already said that only God knows who actually wrote it (reported by Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 6.25). Most scholars now hold that it comes from another hand, since its style and vocabulary differ from Paul's. It was known to Clement of Rome by about A.D. 96, and many date it before the temple fell in A.D. 70. The Church receives it as inspired Scripture whoever wrote it.

Its prophecy works in two directions. Looking back, it reads the Psalms, Jeremiah and the worship of the tabernacle as prophecies and types fulfilled in Christ's death and ascension. Looking ahead, it is full of promise: a rest that remains, a second appearing, a city still to come, a kingdom that cannot be shaken. In between, the Church already approaches “the heavenly Jerusalem” in her worship.

Key to the labels

How a passage stands

What kind of passage it is

Prophecy God's word through a prophet, or Christ, about what will come.

Promise A pledge God makes, kept over generations.

Type A person or event that foreshadows Christ or the Church.

Image A picture Scripture returns to and completes at the end.

Apocalypse A vision unveiling history from heaven's side, in symbols.

The passages

Hebrews 1:1–2PromiseFulfilled
“In times past, God spoke in partial and various ways to our ancestors through the prophets; in these last days, he spoke to us through a son…”
Reading
The letter opens with a view of all prophecy. Through the prophets God spoke in pieces; in the Son he has spoken fully. The NABRE note calls “these last days” the final age, in which God's saving purpose is revealed. The Catechism quotes these verses when it teaches that Christ is the Father's one, perfect and unsurpassable Word (CCC 65), and adds that no new public revelation is to be expected before the glorious manifestation of our Lord Jesus Christ (CCC 66).
Why fulfilled
The last days began with the coming of the Son. Every prophecy that follows on this site is read in his light, not as a further word beside his.
See also
Acts 2:17; 1 Peter 1:20; 1 John 2:18.
Hebrews 1:5–6ProphecyFulfilledMystery
“And again, when he leads the first-born into the world, he says: ‘Let all the angels of God worship him.’”
Reading
Hebrews sets the Son above the angels with a chain of Old Testament texts, beginning with the royal Psalm: “You are my son; this day I have begotten you” (1:5; Psalm 2:7). Spoken first of David's line, the decree belongs fully to Christ, whom the early Church heard named Son at his resurrection and exaltation (Acts 13:33). He is the first-born whom the angels worship.
Why mystery
Which moment verse 6 means is not settled. It may be the Incarnation, when angels sang at his birth, or his entry into heaven at the Ascension. The NABRE note observes that the Greek could also be translated “And when he again leads,” in reference to the parousia. Each reading has defenders; all agree on whom the angels worship.
See also
Revelation 1:5, where Christ is “the firstborn of the dead”; Revelation 5:11–12.
Hebrews 2:5–9PromiseFulfilledFuture
“Yet at present we do not see ‘all things subject to him,’ but we do see Jesus ‘crowned with glory and honor’ because he suffered death…”
Reading
God did not subject “the world to come” to angels (2:5) but to man, as Psalm 8 sings: “you crowned him with glory and honor” (2:7). Hebrews admits frankly that the world does not yet look that way. What we can see is Jesus, the one man in whom the Psalm has come true, who suffered “that by the grace of God he might taste death for everyone” (2:9). God is “bringing many children to glory” (2:10) behind him.
Why two labels
In Jesus the promise is kept already; in us and in the world it is still to come. Christ waits, as the letter says later, “until his enemies are made his footstool” (10:13).
For now
Hebrews does not ask us to pretend all is well. It asks us to look at Jesus.
Hebrews 4:8–11PromiseFutureUnfolding
“Therefore, a sabbath rest still remains for the people of God.”
Reading
Reading Psalm 95, Hebrews argues that the rest God promised was never exhausted by Canaan: “Now if Joshua had given them rest, he would not have spoken afterwards of another day” (4:8). The rest is God's own, the rest of the seventh day (4:4): “whoever enters into God's rest, rests from his own works as God did from his” (4:10). The Catechism points to these verses when it speaks of the beatitude of heaven and quotes St. Augustine: “There we shall rest and see, we shall see and love, we shall love and praise” (CCC 1720; City of God 22.30).
Why two labels
The full rest lies ahead. Meanwhile the letter's word is “Today”: the call to enter is heard now, and “let us strive to enter into that rest” (4:11) is the work of every day.
See also
Genesis 2:2–3; Matthew 11:28; Revelation 14:13.
Hebrews 5–7TypeFulfilled
“You are a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek.” (5:6)
Reading
Psalm 110:4 promised a king who would also be a priest of a different order from Aaron's. Hebrews finds that order in Melchizedek, king of Salem, who blessed Abraham (Genesis 14:18–20). Because Scripture records no genealogy for him, he appears “without father, mother, or ancestry, without beginning of days or end of life, thus made to resemble the Son of God” (7:3). Christ is priest not by descent but “by the power of a life that cannot be destroyed” (7:16), and so “he lives forever to make intercession for them” (7:25). The Catechism sees in Melchizedek, “priest of God Most High,” a prefiguring of Christ, the unique high priest after the order of Melchizedek (CCC 1544).
Why fulfilled
The Psalm's promise is kept in Christ's death and entry into heaven, “where Jesus has entered on our behalf as forerunner, becoming high priest forever” (6:20). His priesthood does not end; it is exercised now in heaven (CCC 662).
See also
Hebrews 5:7–10, the Son who “learned obedience” and became “the source of eternal salvation”; the Roman Canon, which names Melchizedek.
Hebrews 8:8–13ProphecyFulfilledUnfolding
“Behold, the days are coming, says the Lord, when I will conclude a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah.”
Reading
Hebrews quotes Jeremiah 31:31–34 in full, the longest Old Testament quotation in the New Testament. God promises a covenant written inwardly: “I will put my laws in their minds and I will write them upon their hearts” (8:10), ending with “For I will forgive their evildoing and remember their sins no more” (8:12). At the Last Supper Jesus said over the cup, “This cup is the new covenant in my blood” (1 Corinthians 11:25; compare Luke 22:20). The Catechism cites these verses for the New Law, the law of the Gospel, which the Holy Spirit makes “the interior law of charity” (CCC 1965).
Why two labels
The covenant was sealed once in Christ's blood. Its promise that God's law will be written on hearts is still being kept, one heart at a time, by the Spirit.
Note
“When he speaks of a ‘new’ covenant, he declares the first one obsolete” (8:13) speaks of the old order of sacrifice, not of rejecting the Jewish people, whom the Church honors as those to whom the covenants belong (Romans 9:4; CCC 839).
Hebrews 9:11–12, 24–28TypeFulfilledFuture
“…so also Christ, offered once to take away the sins of many, will appear a second time, not to take away sin but to bring salvation to those who eagerly await him.”
Reading
Once a year, on the Day of Atonement, the high priest went behind the veil with blood and then came out again to the waiting people. Christ fulfills the rite: “he entered once for all into the sanctuary, not with the blood of goats and calves but with his own blood, thus obtaining eternal redemption” (9:12). “Christ did not enter into a sanctuary made by hands, a copy of the true one, but heaven itself, that he might now appear before God on our behalf” (9:24). The Catechism cites these verses for Christ's priesthood exercised permanently in heaven (CCC 662).
Future
As the high priest reappeared to the people, the NABRE note says, Jesus will appear a second time at the parousia. Hebrews does not describe that appearing as terror. It comes “to bring salvation to those who eagerly await him” (9:28).
See also
Leviticus 16; The Temple of His Body; Revelation 11:19.
Hebrews 10:35–37ProphecyFuture
“For, after just a brief moment, he who is to come shall come; he shall not delay.”
Reading
Hebrews takes up Habakkuk's vision, “If it delays, wait for it, it will surely come” (Habakkuk 2:3), and reads its subject as a person, the one who is to come. The context is endurance: “You need endurance to do the will of God and receive what he has promised” (10:36). Earlier the letter urges the community to keep meeting together, “and this all the more as you see the day drawing near” (10:25).
Note
“A brief moment” is God's measure, not a timetable. The letter gives no date and no sign to count down; it gives a reason to persevere (CCC 673). See also 2 Peter 3:8–9.
See also
Revelation 22:20.
Hebrews 11:10, 13–16PromiseFuture
“…for he was looking forward to the city with foundations, whose architect and maker is God.”
Reading
Abraham lived in tents in the land of promise, because what he was really promised was a city. The patriarchs “died in faith. They did not receive what had been promised but saw it and greeted it from afar” (11:13), confessing themselves “strangers and aliens on earth.” “But now they desire a better homeland, a heavenly one,” and God is not ashamed to be called their God, “for he has prepared a city for them” (11:16).
Future
The chapter ends with the saints of the old covenant still waiting for us: “God had foreseen something better for us, so that without us they should not be made perfect” (11:40). The city is the new Jerusalem, whose wall has “twelve courses of stones as its foundation” (Revelation 21:14).
Hebrews 12:22–24ImageUnfoldingFuture
“No, you have approached Mount Zion and the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, and countless angels in festal gathering…”
Reading
Israel stood trembling before Sinai (12:18–21). Christians stand before another mountain, and Hebrews says they have already come to it: angels in festival, “the assembly of the firstborn enrolled in heaven,” “the spirits of the just made perfect,” and “Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant, and the sprinkled blood that speaks more eloquently than that of Abel” (12:23–24). The NABRE note calls it the assembly of the new covenant, with direct access to God.
Why two labels
The Church reaches this assembly now in her liturgy. The Catechism teaches that in the earthly liturgy we share in a foretaste of the heavenly liturgy celebrated in the holy city of Jerusalem, toward which we journey as pilgrims (CCC 1090), and that in the Eucharist we already unite ourselves with the heavenly liturgy and anticipate eternal life (CCC 1326). The Sanctus at every Mass is sung with the angels. The full gathering is still to come.
See also
Revelation 4–5; 14:1; 21:2; CCC 1137–1139.
Hebrews 12:26–28ProphecyFutureUnfolding
“His voice shook the earth at that time, but now he has promised, ‘I will once more shake not only earth but heaven.’”
Reading
The promise is Haggai's: “In just a little while, I will shake the heavens and the earth, the sea and the dry land” (Haggai 2:6). Hebrews reads “once more” as pointing to the removal of shaken, created things, “so that what is unshaken may remain” (12:27). What remains is the kingdom.
Why two labels
The final shaking of heaven and earth is still to come, the passing of the former heaven and earth that Revelation sees (Revelation 21:1). But the kingdom is already being given: “we who are receiving the unshakable kingdom should have gratitude, with which we should offer worship pleasing to God in reverence and awe” (12:28).
For now
The letter's answer to a shaking world is not fear but gratitude and worship. What God gives cannot be shaken.
Hebrews 13:14PromiseFuture
“For here we have no lasting city, but we seek the one that is to come.”
Reading
Jesus “suffered outside the gate, to consecrate the people by his own blood” (13:12), so the letter invites its readers: “Let us then go to him outside the camp, bearing the reproach that he bore” (13:13). Christians do not despise the cities of earth; they simply do not mistake any of them for home. The one fixed point is the Lord himself: “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever” (13:8).
Future
The city to come is the one Abraham looked for (11:10) and that John sees “coming down out of heaven from God” (Revelation 21:2).

Hebrews and Revelation: the heavenly liturgy
