Prophecy in 2 Chronicles

The second half of the Chronicler's history runs from Solomon's Temple to its burning. Yet it does not end in ruins. Its last words are a king's decree and an invitation: “let them go up.” In the Hebrew Bible, these are the last words of all.

The book at a glance

2 Chronicles continues the single work begun in 1 Chronicles, written after the exile by the unknown author called the Chronicler, most likely in the fourth century BC. It retells the reign of Solomon and the building of the Temple (chapters 1–9), then follows only the kings of Judah, David's line, down to the fall of Jerusalem in 587 BC and the decree of Cyrus that ended the exile (10–36). Much of it runs parallel to 1 and 2 Kings, but the Chronicler tells it his own way, judging each king by his care for the Temple and its worship and showing again and again that God answers those who seek him. In the Hebrew Bible this is the closing book; in the Catholic canon, following the Greek and Latin Bibles, Chronicles stands after Kings, and the Old Testament ends instead with the prophets.

The book is history, not prediction. Its forward-looking content is type and promise: a Temple filled with God's glory, Passovers that gather a scattered people, a promise of pardon to all who return, and a final word of hope. Each card below names which form it takes.

The passages

2 Chronicles 6:18–7:3TypeFulfilledFuture
“Is God indeed to dwell with human beings on earth? If the heavens and the highest heavens cannot contain you, how much less this house which I have built!” (6:18)
Reading
Solomon dedicates the Temple with a long prayer, asking that God's eyes be open toward this house and that he hear every prayer made toward it, even a foreigner's. The Catechism sees in this prayer the whole people's prayer resting on God's promise and covenant, offered so that the nations may know he is the only God (CCC 2580). The Chronicler alone adds what happens next: “When Solomon had ended his prayer, fire came down from heaven and consumed the burnt offerings and the sacrifices, and the glory of the LORD filled the house” (7:1). The priests cannot even enter, and the people fall on their faces, praising the LORD “who is so good, whose love endures forever” (7:3).
Fulfilled
Solomon's question receives an answer he could not have imagined: “And the Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us, and we saw his glory” (John 1:14). The glory that once filled a house of stone now dwells in the body of Christ, the true Temple (John 2:19–21). Fire from heaven falls again at Pentecost, resting not on an altar but on the disciples (Acts 2:3).
Future
In the end, the question is answered for all creation: “Behold, God's dwelling is with the human race” (Revelation 21:3). John sees no temple in the new Jerusalem, “for its temple is the Lord God almighty and the Lamb” (21:22).

2 Chronicles 7:12–16PromiseUnfolding
“if then my people, upon whom my name has been pronounced, humble themselves and pray, and seek my face and turn from their evil ways, I will hear them from heaven and pardon their sins and heal their land.” (7:14)
Reading
God appears to Solomon by night and answers his prayer. If drought, locusts or plague come because of sin, there is a way back, and it is the way of the heart: humility, prayer, seeking God's face, turning from evil. Then God gives his pledge for the house: “now I have chosen and consecrated this house that my name may be there forever; my eyes and my heart shall be there always” (7:16). This verse is the key to the whole of Chronicles. King after king who humbles himself and seeks the LORD is heard, from Rehoboam (12:6–7) to Manasseh, the worst of them all, who prays in exile and is brought home (33:12–13).
Unfolding
The promise was given to Israel, the people called by God's name. Christians have rightly taken it to heart as a word for the Church, whose members are baptized into the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit (Matthew 28:19), and it is often prayed for one's own country. Read with the whole of Scripture, it is not a guarantee about any one nation's fortunes but an assurance about God himself: he is always ready to hear and forgive those who turn to him. That promise is still being kept wherever anyone repents.
2 Chronicles 30; 35TypeFulfilled
“The LORD, your God, is gracious and merciful and he will not turn away his face from you if you return to him.” (30:9)
Reading
Two great Passovers frame the last century of the kingdom of Judah. King Hezekiah sends letters not only through Judah but to the remnant of the fallen northern kingdom, inviting all Israel to Jerusalem. Many who come are not ritually clean, and Hezekiah prays for them: “May the good LORD grant pardon to all who have set their heart to seek God, the LORD, the God of their ancestors, even though they are not clean as holiness requires” (30:18–19). The LORD hears him, and “There was great rejoicing in Jerusalem” (30:26). A century later Josiah keeps a Passover so great that “No such Passover had been observed in Israel since the time of Samuel the prophet” (35:18). Soon after, he falls in battle, and Jeremiah composes a lament for him (35:25).
Fulfilled
Every Passover remembered the lamb whose blood saved Israel in Egypt (Exodus 12). These two show what the feast was always meant to do: gather the scattered, forgive the unready, and fill a city with joy. Jesus brings the feast to its fulfillment: “I have eagerly desired to eat this Passover with you before I suffer” (Luke 22:15). St. Paul says simply, “For our paschal lamb, Christ, has been sacrificed” (1 Corinthians 5:7), and the Church celebrates that Passover in every Eucharist (CCC 1340).
2 Chronicles 36:21–23PromiseFulfilledUnfolding
“All among you, therefore, who belong to his people, may their God be with them; let them go up.” (36:23)
Reading
The Temple is burned and the people carried to Babylon, “to fulfill the word of the LORD spoken by Jeremiah,” while the land keeps its lost sabbaths “while seventy years are fulfilled” (36:21; Jeremiah 25:11; Leviticus 26:34). Then, “In the first year of Cyrus, king of Persia,” God stirs the heart of a pagan king to send his people home and to build God's house again (36:22–23). These verses are repeated at the start of Ezra (Ezra 1:1–3), and the NABRE note says they were added here so the work would not end on a note of doom.
Fulfilled
The exiles did go up, and the Temple was rebuilt (Ezra 1–6). See Prophecy in Ezra and, for Jeremiah's seventy years, Prophecy in Jeremiah.
Unfolding
Because Chronicles closes the Hebrew Bible, the Scriptures of Israel end not with a full stop but with a summons: go up and build. Jesus seems to have known the books in that order. He spoke of the blood of the prophets “from the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah who died between the altar and the temple building” (Luke 11:51), and many scholars think he meant the first and last martyrs of Scripture as it was then arranged, from Genesis to Chronicles (Genesis 4:8; 2 Chronicles 24:20–22). The call to go up is still open. The Church is a people on pilgrimage, going up to the city God himself has built, the holy city “coming down out of heaven from God” (Revelation 21:2).
