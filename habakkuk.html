Prophecy in Habakkuk

The prophet who argued with God. Habakkuk does not speak to the people for God so much as to God for the people, asking how long evil will go unanswered. The answer he receives is a vision to wait for, and a single line St. Paul built a letter on: the just one shall live by faith.

The book at a glance

We know almost nothing of Habakkuk himself beyond his name and his title, “the prophet” (1:1; 3:1). The book speaks of God raising up the Chaldeans, the Babylonians, as the instrument of his judgment (1:6), which places it in Judah in the last years of the seventh century before Christ, as Babylon rose and Jerusalem's fall drew near. The hymn of chapter 3 carries its own heading and musical notes, like a psalm, and many scholars think it once circulated separately before being joined to the prophet's dialogue; the Church receives the whole as inspired Scripture either way. The prophet also appears, carried by an angel, in the Greek additions to Daniel (Daniel 14:33–39).

Habakkuk is a short book with an unusual shape: two complaints by the prophet, two answers from God, a series of woes on the oppressor, and a closing hymn. Its prophecy is less about events than about how to live while waiting for them. Its central verse, 2:4, became one of the most quoted Old Testament lines in the New Testament, and its question “How long?” is still asked by the martyrs in Revelation.

The passages

Habakkuk 1:2–4ProphecyUnfolding
“How long, O LORD, must I cry for help and you do not listen? Or cry out to you, ‘Violence!’ and you do not intervene?”
Reading
The book opens not with “thus says the LORD” but with a protest. The prophet sees ruin, strife and a law that has grown numb, and he asks why God allows it (1:3–4). God's first answer only sharpens the question: he is raising up the Babylonians, a people more violent still (1:5–11). So Habakkuk asks again how a God too pure to look on evil can stay silent while the wicked swallow those more just than they (1:12–13). Scripture keeps the complaint. It is part of the word of God that faith may argue with God, as Job and the psalmists do (Psalm 13).
Unfolding
The cry is taken up at the end of the Bible. Under the heavenly altar the souls of the martyrs call out, “How long will it be, holy and true master, before you sit in judgment and avenge our blood on the inhabitants of the earth?” (Revelation 6:10). They are given white robes and told “to be patient a little while longer” (6:11). The question is not rebuked; it is answered with a promise and a call to wait.
For now
Every generation that suffers injustice may pray Habakkuk's prayer. The Church does not ask her children to pretend that evil is small; she teaches them to bring it to God.
Habakkuk 2:1–3PromiseUnfoldingFuture
“For the vision is a witness for the appointed time, a testimony to the end; it will not disappoint. If it delays, wait for it, it will surely come, it will not be late.”
Reading
The prophet takes his stand like a sentry: “I will stand at my guard post” (2:1), watching for God's answer. It comes as a command: “Write down the vision; Make it plain upon tablets, so that the one who reads it may run” (2:2). The NABRE note gives two reasons for the writing: so that a herald may carry it to the people, and so that its truth may be checked against what later happens. Then comes the heart of the answer. God's word has its own appointed time. It may seem slow, but it is not late.
Unfolding
The Letter to the Hebrews reads the vision as a person, the coming Christ: “For, after just a brief moment, he who is to come shall come; he shall not delay” (Hebrews 10:37). St. Peter answers those who mock the Lord's apparent delay in the same spirit: “The Lord does not delay his promise, as some regard ‘delay,’ but he is patient with you” (2 Peter 3:9).
Against date-setting
“The appointed time” is God's, not ours to calculate. Jesus told the apostles, “It is not for you to know the times or seasons that the Father has established by his own authority” (Acts 1:7). Habakkuk is told to write and to wait, not to count. The waiting is not anxious but sure: “it will not disappoint.”
Habakkuk 2:4PromiseFulfilledUnfolding
“See, the rash have no integrity; but the just one who is righteous because of faith shall live.”
Reading
The proud oppressor will not last; the one who trusts God will. The NABRE note explains that in Habakkuk's setting “the faithful survive the impending doom because they trust in God's justice and wait patiently for God to carry it out.” Faith here is steadfast fidelity: holding on to God through the long night until his word is kept.
Fulfilled
The New Testament cites this line three times. St. Paul makes it the theme of Romans: in the Gospel “is revealed the righteousness of God from faith to faith;” and he quotes Habakkuk, “The one who is righteous by faith will live” (Romans 1:17). He uses it again to show that no one is justified before God by the law, “for ‘the one who is righteous by faith will live’” (Galatians 3:11). Hebrews joins it to the vision of 2:3: “But my just one shall live by faith, and if he draws back I take no pleasure in him” (Hebrews 10:38). The NABRE note says these passages cite the verse to confirm that people receive justification and supernatural life through faith in Christ.
Unfolding
The verse stood at the center of the Reformation debates, and Catholics and Lutherans have since stated a shared understanding of justification by grace through faith in the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification (1999). For the Church, the faith that justifies is a living faith, “faith working through love” (Galatians 5:6). Each believer still lives by it, day after day, until faith gives way to sight.
Habakkuk 2:14ProphecyUnfoldingFuture
“But the earth shall be filled with the knowledge of the LORD's glory, just as the water covers the sea.”
Reading
The verse stands in the middle of five woes against the tyrant who builds his city with bloodshed (2:6–20). Empires labor for nothing; what will truly fill the earth is the knowledge of God. Habakkuk is echoing Isaiah's promise for the reign of the shoot from Jesse: “for the earth shall be filled with knowledge of the LORD, as water covers the sea” (Isaiah 11:9). Habakkuk adds one word, glory. The woes end with the same turn: “But the LORD is in his holy temple; silence before him, all the earth!” (2:20).
Why two labels
The knowledge of God's glory has begun to spread through the whole world in the face of Christ, as St. Paul writes (2 Corinthians 4:6), carried by the Gospel to the ends of the earth (Matthew 28:19). Its fullness is still to come, in the city that “had no need of sun or moon” because the glory of God gives it light (Revelation 21:23).

Habakkuk 3:17–19ImageUnfolding
“For though the fig tree does not blossom, and no fruit appears on the vine,” … “Yet I will rejoice in the LORD and exult in my saving God.”
Reading
The hymn begins, “O LORD, I have heard your renown” (3:2), and recalls God coming in power as at the Exodus and Sinai, shaking the earth and the nations to save his people (3:3–15). Then the prophet, trembling, chooses to wait quietly for the day of distress to come upon the invader (3:16). The last verses list every loss a farming people could suffer: no figs, no grapes, no olives, no grain, no flock, no herd. Yet the prophet will rejoice: “GOD, my Lord, is my strength; he makes my feet swift as those of deer and enables me to tread upon the heights” (3:19).
Why unfolding
This is the answer to the “How long?” with which the book began. Nothing outward has changed yet, but the prophet has. He has learned to live by faith, as 2:4 promised. The Church prays this hymn in the Liturgy of the Hours, and it is lived out wherever believers praise God with empty hands. St. Paul wrote “Rejoice in the Lord always” (Philippians 4:4) from prison.
For now
Hope does not wait for good news before it rejoices. It rejoices in God, who is himself the good news.
